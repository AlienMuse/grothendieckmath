After the father’s departure

By August 8, Scharlau places Tanaroff in Paris; Chapter 4 gives the evidence for his departure.11Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18.

The chronology now follows the three people who remained in Germany. Hanka intended to leave, but the children did not travel with her, and the household’s dissolution therefore proceeded through separate care arrangements rather than a common flight. Grothendieck’s later account places both separations within the latter part of 1933, while Scharlau’s narrative places Alexander’s transfer near the end of December; neither source supplies Hanka’s border record or Maidi’s 1933 intake file.22Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

This division of routes is the chapter’s subject. It does not require a railway itinerary or a repeated account of Tanaroff’s departure, because the supported sequence begins with Hanka still in Germany, Maidi already living elsewhere by Grothendieck’s later recollection, and Alexander remaining with Hanka until the December transfer. The route south belonged to Hanka; for the children, the immediate movement stayed within Germany.33Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Grothendieck later gathered the period into an adult interpretation of family rupture, drawing on sparse memories and parental correspondence that he examined decades afterward. His account establishes the weight he assigned to 1933, not a continuous record of childhood anxiety or a precise calendar for each decision. The narrative can preserve that retrospective judgment while keeping the event floor narrower: the family ceased to share one household, the two children entered separate care, and Hanka left around the turn of the year according to Scharlau’s chronology.44Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 18.

The months after Tanaroff’s departure placed different practical questions around each child. Maidi was nine and already had a history of residence with maternal relatives, whereas Alexander was five and still living with Hanka; those facts leave each child’s needs and feelings unresolved, but they show why one undifferentiated account of the family’s flight would be historically false.55Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 12, 15, 17.

Hanka’s role during this interval is visible mainly through outcomes. She remained in Germany after Tanaroff, made two separate care arrangements, brought Alexander to Blankenese, and then left around the year’s turn; the unavailable correspondence and admission records prevent a reconstruction of the order in which alternatives were considered or of Tanaroff’s participation in particular decisions.66Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

This source boundary also limits moral shorthand. The acts had lasting consequences and can be described without reducing Hanka to a mother who simply walked away, because arranging care was itself an action, while the record’s silence about consultation and the later pain remembered by Grothendieck remain part of the ethical weight. Consequence can be acknowledged without supplying a private calculation.77Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

The political environment did not choose the arrangements for the adults, but it narrowed the world in which choices were made. Nazi repression transformed Tanaroff’s danger, affected oppositional households, and created uncertainty around people connected to Jewish and political refugees; Hanka’s documented position differed, yet the sources do not establish a safe, stable alternative available to her.88United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Nor did the road south produce one destination called home. Tanaroff was already in France according to Scharlau, Hanka was preparing to leave, Alexander would remain in Blankenese, and Maidi’s exact 1933 institution is unknown; the map is therefore a record of separation, not a line showing a family traveling together.99Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The chapter ends before any account of schooling begins. Alexander’s education in Blankenese, later German school records, movement into France, and eventual French-language schooling belong to the next chronological sequence; Maidi’s later institutional and educational history likewise supplies no explanation for her 1933 placement.1010Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19.

Maidi’s unknown 1933 destination

The evidence for Maidi begins with a gap. Grothendieck wrote decades later that she had been placed in an institution several months before his own transfer, but he did not provide an intake record, institutional name, or route. Scharlau later connected her to Hilde Wulff’s Erlenbusch home through a letter on that institution’s stationery dated June 9, 1938; the later object supports an association by 1938, not a 1933 admission.1111Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Wulff’s institutional history bridges neither the five-year gap nor Maidi’s missing intake. Through Petra Fuchs, Scharlau reports that Wulff opened a Berlin-Westend home in April 1933 and moved it to Volksdorf in September, but neither source available here places Maidi on the 1933 intake or residence list. The former draft treated that route as a strong reconstruction; the present account withdraws it and leaves the 1933 destination unresolved.1212Scharlau, Anarchy.

The distinction is substantive rather than verbal. A later letterhead can identify a subsequent relationship, and an institutional chronology can show where the home operated, yet neither proves when Maidi entered, whether she moved with the institution, what administrative category applied, or who arranged the admission. Those unanswered questions prevent the chapter from narrating Berlin-Westend or Volksdorf as her 1933 route.1313Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The same limit applies to judgments about benefit or harm. Scharlau considered Wulff’s care fortunate in retrospect, but Maidi left no contemporaneous account in the available record, and her brother’s later language is not her testimony. The chapter therefore records separation while leaving her emotional state, view of the institution, and knowledge of her mother’s plan unresolved.1414Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Scharlau also describes a July 1938 letter in which Maidi referred to visits with Alexander and considered a later household-service arrangement involving the Heydorns. That document supports contact between the siblings in 1938, while saying nothing about consultation, resilience, injury, or destination in 1933. It belongs here only to demonstrate the temporal boundary of what survives.1515Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The gap illustrates how an attractive reconstruction can become stronger than its evidence. Wulff’s institutional chronology fits Grothendieck’s broad memory of an institution, and the 1938 stationery supplies a later association, but compatibility across sources does not create a missing admission record; without that record, Berlin-Westend and Volksdorf remain possible contexts rather than Maidi’s established route.1616Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The difference between association and intake is especially important because an institution can serve many roles over time. A child might reside there, work there, correspond on its stationery, visit, or remain connected to its director without having entered on the date later biography assumes; only a dated intake or residence source can distinguish those possibilities for 1933.1717Scharlau, Anarchy.

Grothendieck’s own account carries a different limit. He knew that a separation had occurred and later grouped his sister’s placement with his own family rupture, yet his text was not Maidi’s testimony and did not supply the administrative detail needed to identify her institution; it establishes his understanding of the family structure, not her route or response.1818Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

Maidi should therefore remain a person whose evidence is partly independent of her brother’s fame. Her later letters show continuing family contact and a life connected to institutions and households beyond the Berlin home, while offering no basis for a nine-year-old’s emotional adaptation, consent, or judgment of Hanka.1919Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The exact care arrangement also matters for legal language. Terms such as intake, guardianship, residence, and institutional placement imply distinct procedures, while the available record proves none of them for a named 1933 home; the chapter uses the broad fact of separate care and reserves administrative vocabulary for an authenticated record.

This restraint does not imply that nothing happened until 1938. Hanka made an arrangement for Maidi before leaving, and Grothendieck later remembered that separation as part of the household’s dissolution; the historical fact is the loss of common residence, whereas the receiving institution, route, terms, and contemporary experience remain unknown.2020Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607.

The distinction also prevents later institutional success from becoming retrospective justification. Wulff’s work and the survival of children in her care may be historically admirable, but until Maidi’s 1933 connection is proved, those facts cannot be used to evaluate Hanka’s decision or the quality of Maidi’s placement.2121Scharlau, Anarchy.

Hanka’s unfinished departure

After Tanaroff reached France, Hanka still had to secure daily care for Alexander before she could leave Germany. Dagmar’s late memoir connected that task to Hanka’s passport and remembered a financially elaborate initial approach, but no administrative file, inquiry letter, contract, or ledger survives in the available record; the chapter therefore omits the claimed rule, amount, and representations while retaining only the fact that care was negotiated under financial uncertainty.2222Scharlau, Anarchy.

How Hanka found the Heydorns remains unresolved. Wilhelm Heydorn had appeared years earlier in a proceeding connected with the Hamburg periodical for which Hanka had worked, yet Scharlau found no continuous friendship, and both Dagmar’s memoir and Grothendieck’s later account present the 1933 contact as one between people without an established personal relationship.2323Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 11 and 17.

The earlier institutional crossing may explain why later researchers looked for a connection, but it supplies no intermediary. The available record names no referrer, alternative household, or participating welfare office; the route from need to the Heydorn address remains a genuine gap.

The Heydorns’ existing practice of caring for several children makes the destination socially intelligible without making the contact inevitable. A household already organized around private foster care could accept another child, but that general capacity does not establish the terms offered, the duration contemplated, or the adults’ prior knowledge of one another.2424Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 20.

Scharlau reports correspondence between Hanka and Tanaroff during the separation, although the letters were later destroyed after Grothendieck had used them in his own inquiry. Their existence means that physical distance did not end communication between the parents, while their loss prevents any allocation of proposals, instructions, urgency, or responsibility sentence by sentence.2525Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18; Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

What remains visible is Hanka’s action in Germany. She found a household that accepted Alexander, brought him there near the end of December, and then left the country around the year’s turn according to Scharlau’s chronology; the evidence does not reveal every alternative, but it does show that departure followed rather than preceded the arrangement of care.2626Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

This order matters ethically without becoming exculpation. Arranging a place protected Alexander from being left without an adult household, yet the separation removed him from his mother and placed daily authority with people he did not know; both consequences can be stated without attributing a private motive to Hanka or an immediate feeling to the child.2727Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

The source record therefore supports neither a story of effortless abandonment nor one of fully planned safeguarding. It shows an urgent private arrangement made under material and political pressure, viewed later from sharply different positions by the receiving adult and the former child.

The doorway in two memories

The transfer to Blankenese survives through two retrospective perspectives rather than a contemporaneous scene. Dagmar Heydorn wrote from the receiving household many decades later, and Scharlau identifies errors in her dates and geography; Grothendieck wrote as the former child after examining family correspondence in 1979–1980. Chai and Oort repeat the broad chronology while relying substantially on Scharlau, so they do not add an independent witness.2828Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1.4.

At the event level, those sources support only a narrow sequence: Hanka brought five-year-old Alexander from Berlin near the end of December 1933, the Heydorns accepted responsibility for his daily care, and Hanka left Germany around the turn of the year. Dagmar’s remembered clothing, possessions, dialogue, educational requests, facial expression, and station departure are omitted because the exact German text and contemporaneous corroboration are unavailable.2929Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy.

The two writers also assign different meanings to the same transfer. Dagmar’s memoir centers a household accepting another child despite limited resources, whereas Grothendieck’s later account centers the end of the family in which he had lived; neither perspective can be converted into neutral access to Hanka’s motive or Alexander’s immediate feeling. The chapter therefore reports the change in care and residence while leaving rescue, abandonment, gratitude, hostility, and comprehension outside the event claim.3030Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The distance between the accounts is not a defect to be averaged away. Dagmar remembered the event from the standpoint of adult responsibility and later affection, while Grothendieck revisited it after decades and after reading family correspondence; combining their most vivid details would create a third witness who never existed.

Scharlau’s warning about Dagmar’s errors provides a concrete reason for restraint. Her memoir displaced the timing of the initial inquiry toward the eve of war and misstated other biographical details, so its remembered sequence cannot establish a precise day, dialogue, object, or gesture; its durable value lies in the receiving household’s later account of accepting the child.3131Scharlau, Anarchy.

Grothendieck’s account carries its own temporal structure. He wrote as an adult who regarded 1933 as a decisive break and interpreted the placement through the later experience of foster residence, parental letters, and war; his text establishes that retrospective judgment without turning each remembered feature into a contemporaneous observation.3232Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

The transfer-level chronology is nevertheless clear enough for narrative movement. Alexander was five, Hanka brought him from Berlin to Blankenese near the end of December, and the Heydorns accepted daily care before Hanka left Germany; no exact scene is required to understand that the child’s place, carers, and ordinary surroundings changed at the year’s boundary.3333Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Financial uncertainty belongs to that chronology only in broad form. Both retrospective accounts remember insecure payment, but no contemporary agreement or receipt survives; fee, payment history, and financial influence on the Heydorns’ decision remain unresolved.3434Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Authority is similarly divided. Hanka remained Alexander’s mother, Scharlau reports a youth-welfare framework whose original file is missing, and the Heydorns assumed daily responsibility; the evidence does not allocate legal power among them or show which office approved the arrangement.3535Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

This threshold is the proper stopping point for education. Whatever requests Dagmar later remembered about schooling are not enumerated without the German text, and the actual school years belong to Chapter 7; Chapter 5 records a transfer of care, not the curriculum or language that followed it.3636Scharlau, Anarchy.

Their disagreement about timing further limits reconstruction. Dagmar misplaced the initial inquiry close to the Second World War, while Scharlau corrected the transfer to late 1933 and distinguished it from the 1939 departure for France. That correction supports a year-end range, not a dated doorway scene, and it keeps the French schooling and wartime journey outside this chapter.3737Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19.

Care as an agreement without security

Both later accounts describe uncertainty about payment, but no contemporaneous agreement, ledger, or receipt has been examined; the account consequently retains no exact fee and makes no claim about performance. Scharlau also reports youth-welfare guardianship, although the underlying file does not establish for this account which official, if any, approved the December arrangement.3838Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

The supported change concerns daily care rather than a fully reconstructed legal transfer. The Heydorn household became Alexander’s residence, while later correspondence described by Grothendieck shows that Hanka remained part of his family history; the precise terms, duration originally contemplated, and division of authority among parent, guardian, and carers remain unavailable. What became a five-year residence must not be treated as the plan that every adult held in December 1933.3939Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19.

Schooling, relations within the foster household, official travel permissions, and the 1939 journey belong to Chapter 7. At this boundary, the evidence supports only that the Heydorns accepted daily responsibility and gave Alexander a place to live before Hanka departed; it does not support a conclusion about belonging or emotional adaptation.4040Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19.

Private care and public administration need not have coincided in one document. Scharlau’s later guardianship narrative shows that offices entered Alexander’s history, while the 1933 transfer survives mainly through retrospective accounts; without an approval or placement file, the biography cannot convert household acceptance into a defined legal procedure.4141Scharlau, Anarchy.

The arrangement’s eventual duration was not necessarily its original term. Alexander remained in Blankenese for years, but no surviving agreement states that Hanka, Tanaroff, the Heydorns, or a guardian expected that outcome in December 1933; emergency, boarding, fostering, and indefinite care remain later descriptions of a relation whose initial terms are lost.4242Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 608; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Daily responsibility nevertheless had concrete content even when its legal form is obscure. Another household would provide residence, meals, supervision, and eventually schooling, while the parents remained abroad and family contact continued unevenly; these consequences explain why the December transfer mattered without requiring a claim about who possessed final authority.4343Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19.

The payment question belongs to the same missing agreement. Later witnesses remembered uncertainty, which may illuminate the household’s resources, but an absent ledger prevents the chapter from turning remembered scarcity into a record of unpaid debt or parental neglect; financial conditions remain context rather than verdict.

Care exceeds the later alternatives of gratitude and criticism. Grothendieck could acknowledge what he received while objecting to hierarchy within the foster household, and Dagmar could remember affection while overlooking how the arrangement appeared to the child; those evaluations belong to Chapter 7’s account of the years themselves.4444Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 608; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

By keeping the initial transfer narrow, the chapter preserves room for that later complexity. It neither prelabels the Heydorn years as rescue nor frames every act of care as an extension of parental abandonment; the year-end evidence establishes a new residence, not a settled moral meaning.

Hanka goes south

Scharlau places Hanka’s departure after Alexander’s transfer and around the turn of 1933/1934, but the available record contains no ticket, passport stamp, arrival registration, or verified route. Dagmar’s later memory of same-night urgency is not used to narrow that range, and the chapter names France only as the destination reported by the biographical chronology.4545Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

Grothendieck later interpreted his mother’s journey through his understanding of the parents’ bond, yet his interpretation leaves her practical choices and the pressures of a particular day unresolved. The record shows different kinds and degrees of danger for Hanka and Tanaroff, while offering no basis for a counterfactual claim that she could simply have remained.4646Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

The immediate consequence is geographic rather than psychological: Hanka and Tanaroff were reported in France, Alexander lived in Blankenese, and Maidi lived elsewhere at an institution that the available 1933 evidence does not identify. Scharlau’s separate account places Alf Raddatz under mounting political pressure, but that context neither identifies Maidi’s destination nor supplies an alternative care arrangement.4747Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 16–18.

The resulting network joined parents abroad to children in separate German care arrangements, although the frequency of letters, payments, official involvement, and personal contact remains only partly visible. It was neither a common family migration nor a state-organized evacuation; the available record leaves the exact allocation of responsibility among decisions made under political and material pressure unresolved.4848Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

The pressure surrounding those decisions was not abstract. Germany’s new regime had already suspended civil protections, expanded detention, and begun systematic political and anti-Jewish exclusion, while Scharlau reports that Tanaroff’s history placed him in direct danger; Hanka’s exposure differed, but difference does not identify a viable alternative residence, income, or family arrangement.4949United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Hanka’s documentary position also remains bounded by time. Scharlau later found German consular documentation from 1939, yet that object cannot establish the passport or permission used at the year turn, and Dagmar’s remembered explanation about a withheld passport has no supporting administrative file; the crossing therefore remains a biographical range rather than a legal event reconstructed from papers.5050Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

France was a destination rather than a demonstrated common home. Tanaroff had earlier contacts and work there, Hanka followed according to the reported chronology, and later sources place the adults in different French locations; arrival in one country did not by itself reunite the four-person household or settle the parents’ own domestic relation.5151Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

Reported parental correspondence complicates a picture of total disappearance. The letters indicate continued communication between Hanka and Tanaroff, and Grothendieck later remembered occasional maternal letters during foster care, but the destruction or private status of those objects prevents a detailed account of money, planning, affection, or conflict across the border.5252Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 608; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

The journey’s moral consequence therefore lies first in changed responsibility. Hanka left after arranging care, but departure placed ordinary decisions in other households and made contact dependent on distance, money, and postal exchange; stating those consequences is more defensible than claiming either that she had no choice or that an easy alternative was available.

The family’s political commitments remain relevant without becoming an excuse. Opposition to authoritarian rule and allegiance to anarchist ideals help explain why accommodation to Nazi Germany was difficult, while they do not erase the children’s dependence or predetermine how care should have been arranged.5353Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

This balance also prevents Hanka’s move from being narrated solely through the absent father. She organized the children’s care, crossed the border under unknown documentation, and entered a precarious life in France; her actions had consequences that can be judged historically even though her private hierarchy of motives remains unavailable.

The long future inside a short passage

Grothendieck’s retrospective passage crosses from December 1933 to 1939 in a few lines, summarizing residence with the Heydorns, intermittent maternal letters, paternal silence, and eventual movement to France. Those later endpoints explain the weight he assigned to the transfer, but the five intervening years, including German schooling and the route into French education, belong entirely to Chapter 7.5454Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

The present chapter closes at the turn of 1933/1934. Alexander’s residence with the Heydorns had begun, Hanka had left Germany according to the reported chronology, and Maidi’s exact institution remained unknown; nothing in that boundary authorizes a preview of French lessons, lycée attendance, or wartime schooling.5555Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 608; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Keeping those limits also prevents the late-December transfer from becoming a dramatic summary of the whole family history. The supported sequence is modest: Hanka found care for Alexander, another arrangement had already been made for Maidi, and Hanka then left around the year’s turn; the unavailable records do not reveal every negotiation, motive, or legal step.5656Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Refuge without a common destination

Paris was familiar from Tanaroff’s earlier western exile, when Scharlau places him among émigré and anarchist contacts and describes street photography as his trade. Those connections make the destination intelligible without establishing a 1933 host, address, job, or permit; later photographs show the occupation in Paris only within an unresolved date range.5757Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 18.

Scharlau also describes interwar France as a major destination for immigrants and political refugees whose reception grew increasingly contested. That background explains why France could function as refuge without proving that Tanaroff received asylum or secure residence, and the chapter makes no legal claim from the unseen Nansen or police objects.5858Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18; United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds) (n.d.), https://archives.ungeneva.org/refugees-mixed-archival-group-nansen-fonds.

Hanka’s crossing belongs to a different documentary chain. A later German consular record shows continued documentation under a married surname in 1939, while leaving the paper, permission, and border category used around the turn of 1933/1934 unidentified; Dagmar’s remembered passport explanation remains late testimony rather than an administrative fact.5959Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

The children did not cross an international border in this chapter. Alexander moved within Germany to Blankenese, and Maidi moved into an unidentified care arrangement within Germany; describing either child as legally exiled or stateless would import a status that the available records do not establish.6060Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

Later foreigner language and French schooling cannot be projected backward onto these domestic transfers. Those experiences belong to wartime France, while the 1933 evidence supports only parental migration and the children’s continued residence in Germany.6161Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 17; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

Different verbs are therefore necessary for different people: Scharlau describes Tanaroff as fleeing danger, Hanka as leaving after arranging care, and the children as entering separate German households or institutions. The words mark distinct movements and responsibilities rather than four versions of one journey.

The political history remains essential because none of these movements occurred in ordinary conditions. Nazi consolidation transformed the risk around a Jewish anarchist refugee and oppositional networks, while Hanka’s practical alternatives and both parents’ private motives remain unresolved.6262United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”.

What the accounts can carry

Dagmar and Grothendieck wrote for different purposes and from opposite sides of the transfer. Dagmar memorialized a household that accepted a child, whereas Grothendieck examined the loss of the household formed by his parents, sister, and himself; Scharlau selected and translated both, and Chai and Oort later compressed Scharlau’s account rather than supplying independent corroboration.6363Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1.4.

Their common event floor excludes dialogue and private motive. Hanka contacted the Heydorns despite no established prior personal relationship, brought Alexander to Blankenese near the end of December, and left Germany around the year’s turn after the household accepted daily care. The sources remember financial uncertainty while remaining silent about contemporaneous terms; later assessments of happiness, gratitude, injury, hierarchy, and rescue belong to their speakers rather than the narrator.6464Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17–20.

The asymmetry of agency can be stated without inventing a child’s mind: adults corresponded, negotiated, accepted responsibility, and departed, while Alexander was moved between households. Maidi’s position is less recoverable because no contemporary account records consultation or response, and the 1938 material establishes later contact only; neither child receives an emotional label in this chapter.6565Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The source dependency behind those accounts is part of their meaning. Grothendieck’s French text is directly inspectable as his later testimony, Dagmar’s memoir is known through Scharlau’s translated excerpt, and Chai and Oort derive the early-life summary substantially from Scharlau; agreement among the latter two therefore does not create a second witness to the transfer.6666Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1.4.

This hierarchy is why the revised draft contains no direct language from the private inquiry, remembered dialogue, educational requests, or farewell. Removing those phrases does not make the sources useless; it assigns them the narrower task of showing how the participants later framed the transfer while leaving the event narration at the level supported by chronology.

The two perspectives also prevent a simple moral binary. A household can accept care under difficult conditions and a child can later experience hierarchy or rupture, because protection from one danger does not determine the meaning of every daily relation; the chapter preserves both retrospective positions without choosing a complete verdict before the years themselves are narrated.

Maidi’s absence from the witness pair is equally significant. Her later letters show connection to Alexander and the Heydorns, but the sources do not give her 1933 words, making any three-person synthesis of the siblings’ experience impossible.

The account therefore remains intentionally asymmetrical while still being complete at its boundary. It identifies the adults who acted, the child who entered Blankenese, the sister whose exact destination is unknown, and the year-turn departure reported for Hanka; no additional scene is needed to move the chronology into Part II.

Where the year ended

At the turn of 1933/1934, Scharlau’s chronology places both parents in France and Alexander in Blankenese, while Grothendieck’s retrospective account says that a separate arrangement had been made for Maidi without identifying the institution. Those are the only endpoints used. The private Paris letter is not quoted or independently treated, Wulff’s later association is not projected backward, and no guardian, welfare office, station, or border route is supplied without an examined record.6767Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The four people also continued under different names and administrative descriptions, but those forms do not settle nationality or the division of legal authority. Tanaroff used the identity under which Scharlau follows him in western records; Hanka appears under changing married and family names; Maidi remained Raddatz; Alexander bore Grothendieck. Their distribution across files shows a dispersed family, not a single legal status.6868Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

Political pressure supplied context without dictating one inevitable decision. Nazi rule placed Tanaroff, a Jewish anarchist refugee, in a different documented danger from Hanka, while her practical alternatives and predominant motive remain unresolved. The children’s care arrangements emerged through private contacts and institutions rather than a unified state plan.6969Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17, 19, 20.

The positions at the end of the year are few but sufficient. Tanaroff was already in France by the end of Chapter 4, Hanka left around the year’s turn, Alexander lived in Blankenese, and Maidi lived elsewhere in Germany at an institution not identified by a 1933 record.7070Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Two children, two histories

The siblings entered the family rupture with different prior histories. Maidi had spent substantial periods with maternal relatives, whereas Alexander’s first five years were remembered within the Berlin household. Those outlines leave each child’s response unresolved.7171Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 12 and 15.

Maidi’s age may have changed the practical questions adults faced, but age is not evidence of independence. At nine she still required care, schooling, and an adult household, while the record remains silent about consultation. Later competence is insufficient to fill that silence.7272Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Alexander’s younger age likewise supplies no immediate feeling. He was five when Hanka brought him to Blankenese, and adult recollection later gave the event decisive weight. The chapter states dependence and movement without imagining comprehension at the doorway.7373Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

The sources also preserve the siblings unevenly. Alexander became the author of a vast retrospective text, while Maidi appears through later letters summarized by someone else. Biography must not let abundance of one voice become authority over the other child’s experience.7474Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

That imbalance affects language about family injury. Grothendieck interpreted both placements within one rupture, but only his own experience is available in his voice. Maidi’s separation belongs to the chronology, whereas its emotional meaning remains hers and is unavailable.

The care arrangements therefore require separate statements. Alexander’s destination is supported by retrospective testimony and later residence evidence, while Maidi’s exact 1933 institution lacks an intake record. Combining them as parallel foster placements would give the second route a certainty it does not have.

Later association with Wulff’s institution remains historically relevant when properly dated. The 1938 stationery and Scharlau’s account connect Maidi to Erlenbusch by that later period, while the moment, place, and terms of her earlier entry remain unresolved.7575Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Wulff’s institutional history also deserves its own chronology. The home operated in Berlin and then Volksdorf during 1933, according to the mediated scholarly account. Until Maidi appears in its records, those dates describe the institution rather than the child.7676Scharlau, Anarchy.

The Heydorn evidence has the opposite shape. The receiving household is known, but the initial inquiry, agreement, payment, and official approval are missing. Retrospective accounts identify the transfer while leaving its procedure incomplete.

This asymmetry shows why one general word for care can mislead. Residence, boarding, fostering, guardianship, and institutional intake refer to different social or legal relations. The chapter uses ordinary care language unless a specific record supports something narrower.

Daily care nevertheless had real consequences. The children no longer lived with Hanka, and other adults assumed responsibility for food, shelter, supervision, and later education. Those outcomes do not require a complete legal file to be historically significant.7777Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607.

Separation also changed sibling contact. Scharlau’s later material indicates visits between Maidi and Alexander, showing that different residences did not erase the relationship. The evidence begins too late to establish how often contact occurred in the first years.7878Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The parents remained connected to this dispersed network unevenly. Grothendieck remembered occasional maternal letters and no paternal letter during foster care. His testimony defines his correspondence history, not Maidi’s or the parents’ full conduct.7979Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 608.

Financial support is similarly fragmented. Later reports mention uncertainty and later receipts, but no complete ledger covers either child’s care. The chapter therefore refuses both a total-support claim and a total-nonpayment claim.

The household choices occurred while political danger was accelerating. That context matters because ordinary family resources were already limited and oppositional networks faced new pressure. It does not prescribe one correct arrangement after the fact.8080United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”.

Hanka’s action remains the clearest link between the routes. She arranged care for both children, personally brought Alexander to Blankenese, and left Germany afterward. The record does not show every conversation, but it preserves that sequence.

Tanaroff’s part in the arrangements is less visible. Scharlau reports correspondence with Hanka, yet the absent letters leave particular proposals unattributed. Physical absence proves neither complete control nor complete disengagement.8181Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

The receiving adults also deserve measured agency. The Heydorns agreed to care for Alexander, and an unidentified institution accepted responsibility for Maidi. Acceptance protected the children from having no placement, while its later quality requires evidence from subsequent years.

This distinction prevents protection from erasing loss. A new household can supply necessities while the end of a previous household remains painful in retrospective memory. Both propositions may be true without yielding a complete judgment of the carers.8282Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

The same distinction prevents loss from erasing care. Grothendieck’s later criticism does not make every reported act by the Heydorns harmful, just as Dagmar’s affection does not settle his experience. The years must be narrated from their own evidence.8383Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

At the December threshold, then, biography can state place and responsibility more securely than emotion. Alexander entered a known household, Maidi remained in an unidentified institution, and Hanka prepared to leave. The rest belongs to later chronology.

This is also why French education remains absent. Neither child crossed into a French school system during the chapter’s event range. Any account of language, curriculum, or lycée life would move beyond the year-turn boundary.8484Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 17; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

The divided routes did not predict the later mathematician. They established new conditions of residence and care, but no source connects those conditions causally to a mathematical style. The chapter leaves that false prophecy unused.

The two evidentiary histories finally meet only at the level of family structure. By the time Hanka left, neither child shared her daily household. Their destinations, records, and later memories remained distinct.8585Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607.

What crossed and what remained

Hanka crossed into France while the children remained in separate German care arrangements, and later evidence indicates continuing but uneven family contact. Grothendieck remembered occasional maternal letters and paternal silence during the foster years, whereas Scharlau describes later sibling contact; these traces show persistence while leaving the frequency of letters, payments, visits, and 1933 decisions unresolved.8686Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17–19.

Daily care shifted without yielding a complete legal account. The Heydorns supplied Alexander’s residence, and some official framework existed according to Scharlau, but the unopened guardianship and placement files prevent a conclusion about approval, authority, or nationality; Maidi’s corresponding 1933 institution remains unknown. Kinship, residence, financial support, and legal status must therefore remain separate rather than being condensed into a claim that the children simply acquired new families.8787Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 19.

The road south ended this chapter with provisional safety rather than reunion. Hanka and Tanaroff were reported in France, Alexander had a place in Blankenese, and Maidi lived elsewhere under an arrangement whose location the evidence does not recover; the family’s shared Berlin residence had ended, while its later correspondence and memories continued across the separation.8888Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 18.

That separation created several lines of later contact without restoring one household. Grothendieck remembered occasional letters from Hanka, Scharlau reports later sibling visits, and the parents themselves corresponded across periods of separation; these traces show persistence of relation while leaving frequency, tone, payment, and practical support unresolved.8989Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 608.

The receiving households also did not erase the parents’ social identities. Alexander continued to be known through Hanka and Tanaroff’s family history, while Maidi’s later evidence connected her to relatives and carers across institutional boundaries; residence changed who provided daily care without producing a simple replacement family in the available record.9090Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Legal vocabulary cannot complete what the domestic evidence leaves open. Scharlau’s mediated paternity and guardianship narratives explain why offices later appeared, while the 1933 placement procedure, Hanka’s crossing paper, and both children’s nationality remain unauthenticated and outside the chapter’s factual claims.9191Scharlau, Anarchy.

The chapter closes, then, with movement rather than resolution. The adults had left Germany, the children had entered separate forms of care, and every later claim about schooling, adaptation, danger, or reunion depends on evidence from the years that follow; the road south had divided the family before it carried Alexander anywhere beyond Germany.

The turn of the year

The chapter’s final date remains a range rather than a day. Scharlau places Hanka’s departure around the turn of 1933/1934, and no opened travel object narrows it. That range is sufficient for continuity.

Alexander’s next chapter begins in Blankenese. The transfer has occurred, but schooling, household hierarchy, political danger, and the 1939 journey have not yet been narrated. French education remains later still.9292Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

Maidi’s position is different because her 1933 institution is unresolved. Later Wulff evidence may guide research but stays outside continuity as a proven residence, and her emotional response remains unassigned.

The parents’ position also resists simplification. Both were reported in France, yet the available record does not establish one residence, secure status, or continuous partnership. Their later chronology must begin from that uncertainty.

The family entered 1934 dispersed across households and borders. That statement is complete at the level of supported chronology, even though the records of care, payment, and legal authority remain incomplete.

The remaining research questions are concrete rather than rhetorical. An intake record could identify Maidi’s institution, an inquiry letter could clarify the Heydorn contact, and a travel object could date Hanka’s crossing. The prose does not simulate any of them.

Until those records appear, the chapter’s ethical task is restraint. It names the adults’ actions and the children’s changed residences while withholding motives, feelings, and administrative conclusions. That is enough to preserve consequence.

The road south therefore closes as a divided route. It does not become a verdict about love, care, or belonging before the later evidence is heard.
