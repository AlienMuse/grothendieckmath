The ellipsis

On page 32 of the ninth issue, beneath a heading spread across the width of the page, the journal explains that its name had changed. Survivre had become Survivre… et vivre. The change was provisional, adopted at a monthly meeting in the Paris region while responses from readers were still awaited. The ellipsis did more than soften a slogan. It held apart two ideas that the first year of activism had shown could not be collapsed. Survival meant escaping destruction. Living meant asking what would follow, and what had to change before the danger passed.

The unsigned note refuses a diminished meaning of survival. It would not mean leaving a few human beings after nuclear catastrophe, nor adapting patiently to polluted, regimented, or mutilated life. A crisis could end in death or in renewal; the movement wanted the second sense of sur-vie, life carried beyond a break. The name-change note presents survival as entry into the crisis and a struggle to live; it does not offer a policy. It changes the object of attention. The journal would no longer ask only how civilization might avoid an endpoint. It would ask how work, knowledge, food, education, love, authority, and community had come to feel unliveable.11Survivre … et vivre, no. 9, p. 32.

This was not Grothendieck’s private renaming. Readers and participants had objected that Survivre sounded miserable, defensive, and thin. The regional meeting adopted the new title through discussion. The note carries the voice of a movement, even if Grothendieck’s prose and concepts remained powerful within it. That distinction is essential. During the year after Montreal, Survivre changed as new people entered, older political experiences returned, and the work of making a journal exposed questions that no founding manifesto had settled.

The living Earth of this chapter is therefore not a passive landscape outside human history. It is the world the journal assembled in its pages: soils damaged by industrial agriculture, rivers and air treated as sinks, bodies exposed to radiation, villages emptied by centralization, animals enclosed in production, children disciplined by schools, knowledge divided into professions, and people trying to alter those relations. Some of these connections were carefully documented. Others were conjectural, rhetorical, or utopian. Taken together, the records suggest a bounded significance: a group formed around scientists’ responsibility became one workshop in the emergence of political ecology without producing a finished environmental science.

A calendar in twelve issues

The change can be followed in the contents before it is named in theory. No. 1 placed movement structures beside essays on science, life, and the military. The combined nos. 2–3 reported the Nice congress, printed American nonviolent action, and asked why another movement was needed. No. 4 proposed a series of short monographs on subjects ranging from pollution and weapons to education. No. 5 gave substantial space to ionizing radiation and readers’ arguments. No. 6 joined Grothendieck’s activist autobiography to René Cruse on nonviolence, César Chávez, detergents, radioactive pollution, and Daniel Parker’s challenge to the nuclear industry.22Survivre … et vivre, no. 1; Survivre … et vivre, nos. 2–3; Survivre … et vivre, no. 6; Survivre … et vivre, no. 7.

The sequence did not move cleanly from one stage to the next. Old questions remained while new ones accumulated. No. 7 opened with ecology and revolution, then carried environmental ethics, waste, the pillage of the planet, nonviolence, Spanish conscientious objection, internal commitments, and movement news. No. 8 brought a nature fête at Lesvenant, media criticism, radioactive pollution, Bugey, the Saine Expo, population, high-school activity, and the first sustained presence of Savard’s drawings. The journal was becoming ecological through juxtaposition before its editors could define what ecological politics meant.33Survivre … et vivre, no. 7; Survivre … et vivre, no. 8.

No. 9 made the shift explicit. The attack on scientism stood next to the Collège de France dispute, an appeal to conscripts, criticism of experts, a report on Science for the People, and the name-change page. No. 10 developed the proposed new science while publishing Samuel’s reservations, articles on medicine and cultivation, and arguments about method. No. 11 placed a debate at Orsay beside food, agrobiology, drugs, communes, war, and readers’ mail. No. 12 attempted the most extensive synthesis: cultural subversion, school, NATO, industrial pollution, food networks, and the practice of debate.44Survivre … et vivre, no. 9; Survivre … et vivre, no. 10; Survivre … et vivre, no. 11; Survivre … et vivre, no. 12.

The issue calendar prevents a retrospective category from becoming too neat. The paper did not wake one morning as an ecological journal. It learned from campaigns, copied addresses, translated articles, quarrelled about words, and allowed new contributors to alter its center. Some themes appeared once and vanished. Others returned with changed meaning. Population, for example, could be framed through ecological limits, individual freedom, or the coercive politics of demographic control. Nature could name biological balance or a romantic escape from society. The movement’s development consisted partly in recognizing that each term carried political dangers of its own.

The evolving masthead also complicates the idea of a fixed membership. Early issues printed structures and adherents. Later numbers denied any individual authority to represent the movement and described direction as something still emerging. That formula invited participation but made accountability elusive. It left responsibility for an editorial uncertain and supplied no clear process for correcting a harmful claim attributed to the movement. The journal’s openness was an experiment whose procedural questions never disappeared.

The no. 9 masthead makes that experiment visible on one page. It dates the movement’s founding to July 20, 1970, while declaring that no one had received a mandate to speak in its name. The first statement supplies retrospective institutional memory; the second limits institutional representation. Their coexistence helps explain why later source records can disagree over a founding day without yielding one authoritative minute, and why an editorial collective must still be named when it advances a position.55Survivre … et vivre, no. 9.

Reading the issues in order also reveals a rhythm between emergency and pleasure. Warnings of annihilation sat beside recipes for another life, cartoons, fêtes, readers’ jokes, and practical notices. The lighter material was not simply relief from seriousness. It countered a politics in which fear became the only motive and sacrifice the only virtue. The addition of and live condensed that rhythmic change into the title.

Ecology before ecology

France in 1970 did not lack concern about nature. Naturalist societies, regional conservation groups, journalists, and administrators had already made parks, pollution, wildlife, noise, waste, and planning matters of public controversy. The defense of the Vanoise against tourist development had become a national cause. A French federation of nature-protection societies had formed in 1968. The government created a Ministry of the Environment in 1971. The word environment was entering administrative practice before Survivre learned to speak fluently about it.66Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 26–32.

What was new around Survivre was not the discovery that pollution existed. The early issues instead suggest a conjunction of environmental damage with antimilitarism, industrial growth, scientific authority, and the search for alternative ways of living. The first issue’s statement of purpose placed ecological imbalance beside armed conflict as two threats to life. The juxtaposition permitted a weapons critic, a conscientious objector, an organic farmer, a mathematician, and an opponent of a nuclear plant to recognize adjacent problems without pretending they were identical.

The movement’s use of ecology must be distinguished from ecology as a biological discipline. Scientific ecology studies relations among organisms and environments through concepts, field observations, experiments, and models. Survivre rarely engaged that discipline in sustained technical detail. It borrowed the image of interdependence and extended it into a political language. Ecological came to describe attention to long-term viability, small scale, diversity, decentralization, bodily experience, and relations that did not reduce nature or people to resources. That extension was suggestive but not self-validating. A social order is not an ecosystem, and the fact that a practice is local does not make it just.

The distinction also helps locate Survivre among neighbouring currents. Associations devoted to habitat and species often worked through recognized consultation and scientific expertise. The young Friends of the Earth, created in France in the same month as Survivre, would grow into a more durable environmental organization. Pierre Fournier’s pages in Charlie Hebdo, and later La Gueule ouverte, brought antinuclear and ecological arguments to a large countercultural audience with a sharper comic voice. Survivre was smaller, more theoretical, and for a time more organized as a national network than its improvised appearance suggests. It helped create connections, but it neither invented the French environmental movement nor controlled its direction.77Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 23–30.

From the military to the whole machine

The earliest issues remained predominantly antimilitary. They printed accounts of American scientific protest, conscientious objection, nonviolent resistance, and the relation between research and weapons. Ecological items entered as part of a widening inventory: radioactive pollution, detergents, population, industrial food, waste, and the use of natural resources. The pages did not yet present a single causal theory. Their arrangement said that these matters belonged in one conversation.88Survivre … et vivre, no. 1; Survivre … et vivre, nos. 2–3; Survivre … et vivre, no. 6; Survivre … et vivre, no. 7.

Grothendieck’s own generalization was rapid. The army had first appeared as the clearest moral offence. It then became one part of a civilization organized around centralized production, technical power, professional hierarchy, and growth treated as an end. Scientific research was implicated because some results became weapons and because scientific prestige legitimated the larger order. In the Grothendieck–Guedj theses of late 1971, exact science was described as having developed at a distance from essential needs while serving economic and ideological purposes. That thesis moved well beyond the evidence required to criticize a military grant. It turned the history and method of modern science into a problem.99Survivre … et vivre, no. 10; Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?”

The expansion attracted people for whom military funding had never been the only issue. Claude Chevalley brought an older libertarian and nonviolent politics and a dislike of moral sermons. Pierre Samuel combined ecological commitment with a more pragmatic willingness to work through campaigns and organizations. Denis Guedj, teaching at the experimental university at Vincennes, helped move the journal toward desire, speech, anti-hierarchy, and the cultural politics of everyday life. Daniel Sibony brought questions shaped by psychoanalysis and distrust of the movement’s more solemn certainties. Younger writers connected schools, medicine, sexuality, food, communes, and anti-authoritarian politics to the critique of science.1010Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 84–151.

Their coexistence kept the journal from becoming a transcription of Grothendieck’s will. It also produced friction. A campaigner might ask for accurate information about radiation; a radical critic might reply that turning to benevolent experts preserved the division between those who knew and those expected to obey. A nonviolent activist might value disciplined organization; another participant might hear discipline as a new hierarchy. An organic grower might want practical advice; a theorist might suspect that consumer alternatives left industrial power intact. The widening from army to civilization multiplied causes and standards of political value.

Learning from nonviolence

Nonviolent movements gave Survivre one bridge between moral refusal and daily practice. Conscientious objectors did not wait for a government to abolish armies. They declined service, accepted legal and economic consequences, organized support, and argued that defense could be conceived through non-cooperation rather than superior force. Communities associated with Lanza del Vasto had opposed nuclear weapons and experimented with agriculture using fewer industrial means. Groups in Lyon and elsewhere connected resistance to militarization with support for objectors and local action.1111Survivre … et vivre, no. 7; Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 64–69.

For Grothendieck, such action answered the criticism that Survivre offered only alarm. A person could refuse a military contract or service immediately. Yet the encounter also began to alter the scale of refusal. If military obedience was sustained by habits learned in family, school, workplace, and party, then nonviolence could not be confined to the moment of conscription. It became a question about authority in ordinary relations. The route from survival to living passed through this expansion.

The expansion was politically contested. Some anti-imperialist militants regarded support for national liberation struggles as incompatible with universal nonviolence. Communists accused ecological critics of distracting workers from ownership and class power. A politics of individual conscience could underestimate the coercion facing people without the resources to refuse a job. Survivre did not resolve these objections. It often answered them by claiming that a revolution which reproduced hierarchy, industrial growth, and expert rule would reproduce the civilization it opposed. That was a serious challenge to productivist Left traditions, but it could also become a way of dismissing concrete struggles for wages or power as insufficiently radical.

The journal’s most useful pages allowed these positions to remain visible. Letters, reports, and internal bulletins placed disagreement beside program. Chevalley’s suspicion of coercive moral language mattered because Grothendieck’s urgency could become exhortation. Samuel’s reformism mattered because campaigns needed institutions and achievable demands. Guedj’s emphasis on desire mattered because survival discourse could make political life joyless. None supplied the missing synthesis. Together they changed the meaning of the paper.

A page begins to move

Issue 8, dated June–July 1971, looked different. Didier Savard’s drawings entered the journal and would become a regular feature. The first numbers had the visual severity of a report: blocks of type, headings, rules, lists. Savard gave technical power and institutional absurdity bodies, faces, machines, speech balloons, and jokes. A scientist peering at a small measurement could be placed beside the catastrophe outside his frame. Progress could appear as a machine moving too fast to stop. The drawings made the argument’s scale and exclusions visible.

The visual change was also a redistribution of tone. Grothendieck’s prose could accumulate premises until disagreement appeared morally impossible. A cartoon could puncture that solemnity, including the solemnity of the movement itself. Savard mocked experts, bureaucrats, consumer life, and Survivre’s appetite for debate. Grothendieck later credited the drawings with much of the journal’s relative success. His pleasure in them is evidence against a picture of a leader unable to tolerate every irreverence. It does not mean that the group ceased to experience his persuasive intensity as pressure.1212Scharlau, Spirituality, ch. 6; Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles (Université des Sciences et Techniques du Languedoc and Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique, 1986), 758.

Layout gradually became less obedient. Articles began on one page and resumed later; notes, letters, cartoons, notices, and addresses occupied the margins and gaps. The form resembled the movement’s desired network: multiple voices, interrupted sequence, information crossing theory. It could also be hard to read. Disorder was not automatically participation, and playful pseudonyms did not remove inequalities among contributors. Still, the change from the first issue’s austere program to the later pages records a real evolution in political sensibility.1313Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 16–18.

The ninth issue makes scientism its principal theme. Its collective editorial, The New Universal Church, argues that science’s success had produced an ideology with religious characteristics: only scientific knowledge counted as real; experts became priests; social questions were divided into specialities and returned to the public as authoritative decisions. The analogy was intentionally provocative. It identified reverence and exclusion, but it risked treating science as a single church and underestimating internal criticism, empirical correction, and the differences among disciplines. Pierre Samuel’s published reservations showed that agreement on the central danger did not entail assent to every formulation.1414Survivre … et vivre, no. 9, pp. 3–7; Survivre … et vivre, no. 10, pp. 23–27.

Who made the paper

The journal’s circulation depended on labor less visible than its signed arguments. During the first two years, Ségolène Aboulker performed extensive typing, correspondence, layout, cutting and pasting, fabrication, and routing. Looking back, she called her role minor and practical; the list of tasks demonstrates that supposedly minor work was what allowed the theoretical work to exist. She used a large electric typewriter, assembled pages with adhesive tape, and managed readers’ mail. Her recollection is not a complete staff record, but it restores a material foundation easily lost in a biography centered on one prominent author.

Gender shaped that foundation. Women were few in meetings and often appeared in records as a man’s wife or companion. Practical secretarial work fell disproportionately to Aboulker and, after she left Paris, to conscientious objectors serving at the movement. The journal attacks hierarchy between manual and intellectual labor, yet the people composing arguments and the person typing them did not automatically exchange places. Participants came as couples, families, laboratory groups, and friends; the informality that made the room welcoming to some could make speaking more difficult for others. Pessis’s interviews preserve both warmth and intimidation.1515Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 11–20.

Grothendieck undertook an exceptional share of correspondence and editing. For roughly two years he treated Survivre as full-time work, apart from travel and teaching. Denis Guedj later recalled that keeping the machine running consumed whole days. By 1972 approximately fifteen letters a day arrived at the secretariat, according to Pessis’s reconstruction. A group of participants divided replies by interest or availability. Objectors assisted with office work. The bulletin of liaison, begun in February 1972, condensed letters, local reports, announcements, and arguments about the function of the network.

This labor changed the relation between author and reader. A letter could lead to a local meeting, a distributor, a commune visit, a technical enquiry, or an article printed substantially as received. The central group did not only transmit doctrine; it sorted and amplified information arriving from places it did not control. But sorting was power. Decisions about what to print, whom to answer, and which local initiative represented the movement remained concentrated among people with access to the secretariat. The later refusal of representational mandate was an attempt to confront that fact, not proof that the fact had disappeared.

A room of strong personalities

Pessis’s interviews describe meetings shaped by unusually forceful people. Grothendieck could persuade with an intensity that some experienced as generosity and others as violence. Mireille Tabare remembered warmth, spontaneity, music, and welcome; Ségolène Aboulker remembered being formed by the discussions while remaining wary of his power of conviction. These memories do not cancel one another. A person may open intellectual possibilities and make disagreement difficult in the same conversation.1616Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 11–15.

Chevalley’s presence offered a different authority. Twenty years older than Grothendieck and one of the founders of Bourbaki, he possessed mathematical stature without sharing the younger man’s appetite for moral exhortation. Grothendieck later recalled that Chevalley tried to teach him something about inner freedom and resisted discourse that constrained through virtue. The memory is retrospective, but the disagreement appears in the early movement’s tone: proclamations of urgent duty sit beside warnings that persuasion can become domination.1717Scharlau, Spirituality, ch. 6.

Samuel occupied another position. He accepted much of the ecological and antinuclear critique, helped with treasury work and administration, and was willing to work in broader organizations. Grothendieck later affectionately cast him as the group’s reformist, but that label should not make him timid. Samuel’s longer career in Friends of the Earth and antinuclear politics demonstrates a durable commitment. Inside Survivre, his pragmatism pressed against a tendency to value an action chiefly for how radically it broke with existing forms.

Guedj’s relation with Grothendieck was described by contemporaries through opposites: rigor and improvisation, ascetic drive and pleasure, formal power and analogy. Such character pairs can become caricatures, yet their political collaboration had concrete effects. Guedj helped name desire as something more than a need identified by planners. He favored a movement that dissolved into social life rather than one that enrolled members under a line. The Grothendieck–Guedj theses joined the former geometer’s systematic reach to arguments circulating through Vincennes, Maoism, libertarian politics, and psychoanalysis.

Sibony and others prevented even that pair from exhausting the debate. Participants brought simultaneous commitments to medicine, feminism, anti-psychiatry, anarchism, Marxism, education, ethnology, and regional struggles. For many, Survivre was one engagement among several rather than a total vocation. Grothendieck was exceptional in devoting nearly all available time to it. That difference in commitment could look like moral superiority from one side and unreasonable demand from the other.

Younger or less confident participants did not necessarily experience the room as a republic of equal voices. Some listened for months before speaking. Women’s limited visibility reflected both the composition of mathematics and the habits of radical groups. People arrived through friendship and couple networks that could welcome insiders while leaving others without a route in. The movement’s later theory of the debate as a space for hidden speech grew from these limitations as much as from success.

The biographical temptation is to turn the group into a chorus around one life. The archival record suggests another picture: Grothendieck’s life was being changed by a room whose other occupants had histories and futures not defined by him. He brought fame, urgency, and sustained labor. Others supplied political languages he had not previously used, practical skills he did not possess, and resistance to his certainties. The living part of Survivre… et vivre was partly this refusal to remain the instrument of its initiator.

Twelve thousand five hundred copies

The growth of the print run was striking for a handmade political journal. Movement records reconstructed by Pessis show about 1,300 copies for no. 6 in January 1971, 2,000 for no. 7, 3,000 for no. 8, 5,000 for no. 9, 10,000 for nos. 10 and 11, and 12,500 for no. 12 in June 1972. Earlier issues sold out and were reprinted in Nantes. Print run is not readership, and these figures do not prove a mass organization. They do show that the paper moved beyond a few dozen Paris mathematicians.

Distribution bypassed most ordinary commercial channels. Bundles went to more than a hundred people in provincial towns, who sold them in universities, associations, streets, and personal networks. Militant bookshops carried copies. Lecture visitors recruited distributors. A liaison bulletin itself reached around 1,500 copies at one point. The method made each distributor a possible node rather than the last link in a sales chain. Money and comments returned toward Paris; contacts traveled laterally when addresses were shared.1818Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 18–24.

Early issues and later research describe an operation sustained through subscriptions, donations, and unpaid work. Paper, printing, postage, travel, and time still had to come from somewhere, and access to those resources remained uneven.1919Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 18–20.

The journal’s success produced its own burden. More copies meant more addresses, accounting, unanswered mail, and demand for public meetings. Growth could contradict the wish for decentralization if every request went through one overworked office. The group considered a communal press and did not realize it. It continued using a printer in Aubervilliers. As provincial contacts became more active, the Paris group increasingly imagined itself as one local group among others. That was a political aspiration and an administrative necessity.

A network without a center

At one point roughly twenty local initiatives used or approached the Survivre name. The number must be handled lightly: some were a few correspondents, some short-lived discussion groups, some already existing organizations, and some active campaigns. Their variety is more revealing than an exact count. In Brittany contacts formed around ecological information and food networks. In the Loire country and elsewhere, groups opposed proposed nuclear plants or military expansion. In the Mediterranean region, scientists and activists investigated industrial pollution, the Fos complex, and regional development. Others organized mobile film screenings, free-school discussions, vegetarian practice, or alternative health work.2020Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 160–63.

The local groups did not all reproduce the Paris critique of expertise. Some Mediterranean participants described a scientific committee and performed measurements—forms of counter-expertise closer to established environmental associations. A Toulouse group insisted that ecological criticism could not be organized on a Parisian centralist model. A group in the Orléanais opposed a nuclear project while questioning the usefulness of another journal. The name became a resource that people adapted rather than a franchise whose use could be policed.

This diffusion made the movement influential in ways that a membership total would miss. People who met through Survivre later worked in Friends of the Earth, antinuclear organizations, alternative medicine, organic agriculture, regional movements, and ecological journalism. The Paris journal did not command those later trajectories. It supplied addresses, arguments, and an occasion to recognize allies. Pessis aptly treats it as a place of crystallization—a temporary form in which currents met before moving on.2121Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 23–32.

The metaphor should not be allowed to become mystical. Networks were made by postage lists and travel, by people able to take time from academic jobs, by unpaid work, and by access to printing. Provincial participants could feel used as distributors for a Paris publication even while the editors praised decentralization. The liaison bulletins repeatedly debated their own growth and legitimacy. Their self-criticism indicates both sensitivity and failure: the problem persisted because no declaration could remove the material advantages of the center.

Ecology enters public view

The movement’s expansion coincided with a rapid change in public language. By 1972 the report to the Club of Rome on limits to growth was being debated well beyond specialist circles. Sicco Mansholt, a European commissioner associated with agricultural modernization, provoked controversy by considering zero growth. The Nouvel Observateur assembled an ecological supplement and placed Grothendieck among figures such as Edgar Morin, Herbert Marcuse, and Edward Goldsmith. Fournier founded La Gueule ouverte in November 1972. What had looked marginal in 1970 was becoming a field that newspapers, parties, industries, and ministries sought to name.2222Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 28–30.

Recognition did not simply reward the pioneers. It changed the object they had made. Environment could become a new administrative sector whose experts managed pollution without questioning industrial growth. Antipollution equipment could become a market. Nature could be reserved as a recreational zone for urban consumers while rural communities continued to lose control over land. The Ministry of the Environment demonstrated that a problem had entered government; for Survivre it also demonstrated how quickly criticism could be assigned to a bureau.

The journal distinguishes an ecology of control from an ecology of desire. The first treated ecological limits as reasons for more measurement, planning, surveillance, and expert management. The second asked whether people could recover power over needs and relations. This opposition was too clean—public health and pollution control can require coordinated measurement, and desire offers no guarantee against harm—but it identified a political choice often concealed by technical language. The decisive question was who would define needs and bear limits; ecological crisis could deepen democracy or authorize a new hierarchy.

Concerns about population made the danger acute. Environmental alarm could turn poor people, colonized populations, or women’s bodies into variables to be controlled. A movement that began by defending life could end by granting experts power to decide which lives were excessive. Survivre’s later critique of ecological authoritarianism arose partly from this recognition. It separated the journal from versions of environmentalism willing to preserve order through coercive limits.

At the same time, refusing public institutions could leave corporate and state projects uncontested. Friends of the Earth, regional conservation federations, trade unions, and local committees made different choices about consultation, legal action, elections, counter-expertise, and demonstration. Survivre’s Paris issues often evaluated actions by their symbolic and relational effects rather than by negotiated technical success. Provincial groups facing a plant, road, or expropriation could not always afford that standard. The tension between prefigurative politics and immediate protection widened as ecology became a recognized cause.

Grothendieck’s public visibility belonged to this transitional moment. A newspaper could present the famous mathematician who had rejected science, turning a collective movement into a remarkable personality. That framing opened attention and narrowed understanding. It made his extremity newsworthy while making the secretarial workers, local organizers, and prior conservation campaigns disappear. The biography must resist repeating the same economy of fame even as it explains why his fame mattered.

Limits are distributed

The language of limits seemed to offer ecology an objective foundation. Resources were finite, wastes accumulated, populations and production could not grow without end. A curve crossing a boundary appeared to settle what political argument could not. Survivre adopted this urgency and distrusted the claim that more scientific management would preserve indefinite growth.

Physical limits are real and do not distribute their consequences by themselves. A national average can hide luxury consumption beside unmet need. A policy that reduces total use can leave poorer households with less and powerful industries untouched. Saying that humanity exceeds a limit can make responsibility equal where consumption, decision, and vulnerability are radically unequal.

The movement’s focus on industrial civilization partly corrected the problem. It identified a form of production rather than human existence alone. Its population sources sometimes reintroduced the abstraction by treating more people as the central danger. The two diagnoses led to different politics: control births, or transform high-consumption systems and unequal power.

Zero growth was similarly ambiguous. Measured output can stop rising while hierarchy remains. Essential services can expand while destructive production contracts. A single aggregate cannot say which change has occurred. Survivre’s attention to needs and desire tried to give qualitative content to the number, though it never developed an economic program adequate to the task.

Distribution also applied across time. Nuclear waste, soil degradation, and resource depletion placed obligations on people unable to participate in the original choice. The language of survival represented them imperfectly. Future life could not vote; present institutions had to act without claiming to know every future desire.

An ecological politics therefore needed limits and democracy together. Without limits, promises of universal abundance could externalize damage. Without democracy, limits could become the expert administration of scarcity. The journal’s shift from survival to living expressed this double demand more successfully than its forecasts did.

For Grothendieck, the distinction challenged the habits of mathematical generality. A universal statement gains power by abstracting from irrelevant differences. In politics, deciding which differences are irrelevant is itself a distribution of power. The species named a shared danger and could hide the unequal world inside it. The living Earth required both common condition and particular lives.

This is why the journal’s best ecological passages moved between scales rather than selecting one. A damaged drum, a worker’s exposure, a village losing land, a national energy program, and a planetary atmosphere were connected without becoming interchangeable. Action had to begin somewhere and remain answerable to consequences elsewhere. The network form attempted that movement institutionally: local groups held situated knowledge while a journal circulated patterns across them. Paris repeatedly threatened to become the center it denied, and purely local action threatened to miss the system. Living named no final balance. It named the work of keeping scale, power, and experience in the same argument.

A library of possible lives

The paper became a guide to objects and practices as well as arguments. Addresses led to conscientious-objector support, antinuclear committees, communes, organic growers, small presses, traveling exhibitions, and alternative schools. Book notices introduced Ivan Illich on schooling, Jacques Ellul on technique, Lewis Mumford, Herbert Marcuse, Paul Ehrlich, Barry Commoner, and American countercultural writing. An improvised lending library offered books by post. Its notice invited readers to educate one another outside a curriculum, although the scale and use of the library remain uncertain.

American materials had particular force. Earth Day, radical science groups, the organic-gardening movement, and the New Alchemists suggested that technical knowledge could be detached from large industry and reorganized around small communities. Translation was selective. The United States could appear at once as the center of military-industrial power and the source of the most inventive resistance to it. This double image suited a movement that rejected both simple anti-Americanism and faith in American technology.

French sources mattered differently. Naturalists supplied knowledge of soil, plants, animals, and regional damage. Nonviolent communities offered lived experiments in agriculture and common work. The ethnologist Robert Jaulin’s critique of ethnocide helped connect technical civilization to the destruction of other cultures and ways of knowing. Serge Moscovici’s writing on society and nature offered a social theory of ecology. Fournier’s journalism gave campaigns humour and reach. The journal’s intellectual world was therefore assembled through borrowing, argument, and personal contact rather than a single canon.

This eclecticism could encourage credulity. Practices presented as natural, popular, or non-Western were not automatically safe, effective, or free. Reports from distant experiments arrived through sympathetic translations. The desire for knowledge outside institutions sometimes made institutional testing look merely oppressive. Survivre’s best moments held that risk open by printing disagreement. Its weakest moments treated an alternative label as evidence of virtue.

New science

The Grothendieck–Guedj theses in no. 10 moved beyond new research priorities to propose a new practice of knowledge. Agriculture, animal husbandry, fish cultivation, decentralized energy, popular medicine, and light technologies would take priority over fields driven by prestige or industry. Intuition, sensation, beauty, and a sense of unity with nature would rejoin rational analysis. The specialist would disappear as a social type; fields, ponds, workshops, construction sites, and sickbeds would replace the laboratory as the center of inquiry. The text’s formula was science of the people, not science for the people.2323Survivre … et vivre, no. 10; Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?”

The program answered a real democratic problem. Giving people the results of an unchanged institution did not give them power over what questions were asked. Popularizing a technical decision after experts had made it was not participation. The theses therefore moved from access to production: people should construct the knowledge appropriate to their needs. This was a radical criticism of benevolent expertise and remains intelligible beyond the predictions of industrial collapse that surrounded it.

Yet the people could become as abstract as the expert. Communities contain differences of power, gender, wealth, health, and education. A person cannot become competent to treat every illness merely by being freed from hierarchy. Some technologies require coordination beyond a village. Sensation can expose what an instrument misses, but an instrument can also reveal a danger no body can immediately feel. Radioactivity, one of the movement’s central concerns, made the limits of direct experience especially plain. The program raised questions that its rhetoric sometimes outran.

Grothendieck’s mathematical history sharpened the paradox. He had spent decades constructing concepts whose value depended on long specialized formation and whose consequences could not be judged by immediate need. His new program did not simply distribute those concepts more widely. It anticipated that much of existing science would fall into deserved disuse and that disinterested inquiry might return later on healthier foundations. The vision was not an argument that everyone should learn algebraic geometry. It was a claim that society should stop treating the production of such knowledge as self-justifying while essential needs remained organized destructively.

Need, desire, and authority

Essential needs had supplied the early movement with moral clarity. Food, shelter, health, safety, and peace seemed more defensible objects of knowledge than prestige, weapons, or consumer novelty. But needs can be defined from above. Governments, doctors, planners, employers, and parents all claim to know what others require. Guedj’s emphasis on desire disturbed the apparently neutral category. Efficient satisfaction of needs certified by specialists was insufficient; people should participate in naming what made a life their own.

The move from need to desire shifted the critique of consumption. It was easy to say that advertising manufactured false needs and that industrial society kept people working for objects that did not make them free. It was harder to distinguish a false desire from one that an activist simply disliked. Austerity could become another morality imposed by educated people on workers who had only recently acquired comfort. Communists used this objection against ecological criticism: those already secure appeared to be telling others that refrigerators, cars, or higher wages were vulgar.

Survivre’s answer was not that deprivation was virtuous. Its writers tried to separate material security from an economy compelled to expand every form of production. They asked whether shorter work, durable goods, shared tools, local food, public life, and less hierarchical relations might satisfy people better than continual consumption. The answer remained hypothetical and was tested unevenly in communes and networks. But the question prevented ecology from becoming a simple program of less for everyone.

Grothendieck’s personal example carried both force and danger. He had enjoyed exceptional working conditions and material security before renouncing their center. Leaving IHÉS made the appeal a public action rather than rhetoric alone. Yet an action taken from international fame could not define what refusal cost a junior researcher, technician, secretary, or factory worker. His CERN discussion acknowledged career constraint, while other interventions presented refusal in more general terms. The movement’s social diversity was too limited to correct the problem fully.2424Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 18–24.

Desire also entered the discussion of bodies and relationships. Medicine could treat a patient as an object measured by rates; school could classify a child; family authority could suppress a young person’s speech; sexual freedom could challenge convention while leaving male power intact. By putting these matters beside pollution and weapons, the journal argues that domination did not reside in one institution. The breadth made the critique alive and sometimes indiscriminate. Not every clinical measurement is domination, and not every personal conflict is an effect of industrial civilization.

The recurring political test became agency. Did a practice give people more capacity to understand and alter their conditions, or did it place them under another specialist. This test linked food networks, free schools, popular medicine, and debate. It also exposed a paradox: capacity often grows through learning from people who know more. The problem was not the existence of knowledge differences but their conversion into permanent obedience. Survivre’s radical rhetoric sometimes attacked expertise itself; its more durable insight concerned the institutions that made expertise unanswerable.

That distinction would prove decisive in the nuclear campaign. Radiation cannot be judged by unaided senses. Reactor engineering cannot be learned in one public meeting. Residents nevertheless bear consequences that cannot be delegated. The movement had to use technical findings while refusing the claim that technical specialists alone could choose an energy system. The living Earth was not a realm beyond knowledge. It was the shared condition under which knowledge had to become accountable.

Bodies are not a metaphor for Earth

The journal’s ecological widening entered medicine because the body was one place where expertise, industry, and lived knowledge met. Articles criticized specialization by organs, quantitative norms detached from biography, routine pharmaceutical intervention, and a patient’s conversion into an object. Contributors involved in medical politics brought experience different from the mathematicians’ institutional analogy.2525Survivre … et vivre, no. 10; Survivre … et vivre, no. 11; Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 164–66.

The strongest claim was democratic: a patient knows pain, history, fear, and the effects of treatment in a way no chart exhausts. The strongest caution is equally important: experience does not by itself diagnose infection, dose, or pathology. An accountable medicine must join forms of knowledge rather than replace one monopoly with another. Survivre’s rhetoric sometimes moved too quickly from dispossession to rejection of professional competence.

Women’s health exposed authority beyond the clinic. Access to contraception and abortion, sexual norms, domestic labor, and the right to speak about one’s body were central political questions in the period. The journal did not consistently center them, and later activism by individual participants cannot be retroactively claimed as the movement’s achievement.

Population discourse made the intersection dangerous. Ecological arguments about finite resources could authorize control of women’s reproduction or blame poorer populations for consumption driven elsewhere. A universal human species hides who is asked not to have children and who uses the most resources. The movement’s opposition to expert rule provided grounds to criticize coercive population policy, even when some of its alarmist sources made population a principal threat.

The analogy between an organism and the planet was tempting: pollution as disease, industry as cancer, ecological balance as health. Such images made systemic damage intelligible and could naturalize a preferred social order. A body is not harmonious by remaining unchanged; immune, microbial, developmental, and environmental processes are dynamic. A society is not a single organism with one interest. Political disagreement cannot be diagnosed away as pathology.

Care also challenged the movement’s celebration of autonomy. Children, disabled people, sick people, and older people depend on sustained relations that cannot be reduced to self-sufficiency. Communities must organize care, and that work often falls invisibly to women. A politics of small independent units can deepen burden unless dependence is acknowledged and shared.

The living Earth, then, was not one large body whose needs a new expert could read. It was a condition of interdependent and unequal lives. Survivre moved toward this insight when it allowed patients, women, workers, and local people to speak; it moved away when life became an abstract value pronounced by the most confident voice. The distinction would remain unresolved.

Looking toward China

China appeared in these texts as an exception to global scientism. At CERN, Grothendieck would explain that he had long been repelled by the official cult and language surrounding Mao, then became interested after contact with the New Alchemists. Reports of barefoot doctors, local improvisation, and slogans against dependence on experts seemed to show knowledge returning to ordinary people. He praised cases in which practical invention emerged without long credentials and contrasted them with Western professional monopolies.2626Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 13–16.

These claims must remain in their contemporaneous frame. European visitors and sympathetic publications had partial access to China during the Cultural Revolution. Accounts of decentralized medicine arrived through a political system that also exercised coercion, persecution, and control over knowledge. Grothendieck’s hostility to Soviet repression and initial distrust of Maoist hagiography show that he was not simply an obedient Maoist. His later enthusiasm nevertheless relied on idealized and insufficiently tested reports. The point is not to ridicule a mistaken traveler from the safety of later knowledge. It is to show how urgently he searched for evidence that another practice of science already existed.

Other participants related to China differently. Maoist and post-Maoist currents at Vincennes valued the Cultural Revolution as an attack on hierarchy and a release of popular creativity. For Guedj, desire and spontaneous speech could matter more than Grothendieck’s moral discipline. For sceptics, the imported vocabulary threatened another dogma. Survivre’s references therefore cannot be reduced to one party line. China was a screen for arguments about experts, manual and intellectual labor, medicine, and revolution within France.

The episode also reveals a recurrent asymmetry. The movement demanded exact accountability from Western nuclear and military institutions while accepting some affirmative reports of alternatives with less scrutiny. That asymmetry did not invalidate the demand for accountability. It showed the difficulty of building hope under conditions of incomplete information. A biography should preserve both the generosity of the search and the weakness of the evidence.

Food, soil, energy

The most grounded alternatives began with practices close enough to examine. Articles discussed organic cultivation, the effects of fertilisers and pesticides, soil life, vegetarianism, food distribution, and the distance between producer and consumer. Mireille Tabare’s contribution on new forms of agriculture grew from a meeting rather than an imposed editorial doctrine. Pierre Lieutaghi brought detailed attention to plants and rural environments. Regional groups tested gardens and heating systems. A Paris food network asked how products might move outside ordinary commercial circuits.2727Survivre … et vivre, no. 10; Survivre … et vivre, no. 11; Survivre … et vivre, no. 12; Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 15–24, 160–63.

These activities changed the meaning of technical work. A solar heater or a garden was not important only because it reduced pollution. It allowed participants to learn by making, to connect mental and manual labor, and to reduce dependence on systems they could neither understand nor control. The small scale was ethical and pedagogical. It made consequences visible and gave users some power to repair.

Smallness, however, did not settle justice. Land, tools, free time, and a house in the countryside were not equally available. A network selling natural food could become a niche market for affluent consumers. A commune could reproduce male authority and unpaid domestic labor. The journal sometimes recognized these risks with unusual candour. Its later articles criticized communes that depended on outside wages or state subsidies and warned that sexual liberation could multiply rather than dissolve unequal relations.

The practical experiments should therefore be read neither as quaint retreat nor as blueprints. They were tests of whether means could anticipate ends. Participants tried to make production less centralized, work less divided, and knowledge less proprietary. Some experiments lasted; many did not. Their failures supplied information that no abstract manifesto could provide. The movement’s ecology became credible where it accepted that feedback and least credible where it announced a future civilization in advance.

The debate as a way of living

By June 1972 the collective text opening no. 12 described Survivre’s activity as cultural subversion. For more than a year, it said, participants had organized meetings in faculties, research centers, schools, technical and agricultural colleges, elite institutions, youth centers, bookshops, and primary schools. The subjects varied—pollution, science, the army, peasant life, education, communes—but discussions repeatedly reached the wider crisis of civilization.2828Survivre … et vivre, no. 12, pp. 2–10.

The text defines subversion less by a program than by the relations created in an action. It locates subversion in small acts, including gesture and vocal tone, and described creative freedom as potentially liberating for participants. These phrases can sound weightless beside a nuclear plant or an army. Their political point was that a campaign reproducing manipulation and obedience had already conceded something essential, even if it won its technical demand.2929Survivre … et vivre, no. 12, pp. 7–9.

That standard could become impossibly severe. Movements need slogans, delegates, expertise, timetables, and strategic decisions. A refusal to influence anyone can disguise influence rather than remove it. Grothendieck’s fame and force of speech did not vanish because a meeting was called a debate. The collective text was aware of the danger. It recommended several facilitators, silence, smaller groups, and an invited speaker willing to withdraw rather than answer every question. The lecturer should become a catalyst, not an oracle. Chapter 46 will follow that experiment into the Collège de France, Orsay, and CERN.

For now, the importance of the debate lies in how it joined politics to daily conduct. Living differently did not begin only on a farm or after industrial collapse. It could begin in who spoke, who listened, whether uncertainty was allowed, and whether the prestigious visitor needed to win. The movement’s principal public asset—Grothendieck’s authority—became one of the relations it had to transform.

Earth and atom

The move from survival to living did not leave catastrophe behind. It gave the opposition to nuclear power a wider grammar. A reactor had a calculable accident probability; for the journal it also concentrated decisions, required specialized knowledge, linked civilian and military nuclear systems, produced wastes extending beyond an ordinary political horizon, and encouraged an ever-growing demand for electricity. The debate about the atom became a debate about what kind of life centralized energy made possible and necessary.

That analysis emerged through campaigns rather than from one theoretical leap. Early antinuclear writers found space in the journal. Reports from Fessenheim and Bugey connected local resistance to the national silence of large media. Questions about radioactive waste became concrete at Saclay. Scientists supplied measurements and documents while activists questioned scientists’ right to monopolize judgment. Each action exposed a different part of the problem and a different contradiction inside Survivre.

Grothendieck was one actor among many in that development. He used his name to seek publicity and his time to organize debates. Samuel would sustain a much longer engagement in ecological and antinuclear organizations. Regional committees, journalists, engineers, teachers, residents, trade unionists, and scientists made the movement possible. Treating Grothendieck as the father of French ecology would erase those people and exaggerate the continuity of his own brief, intense participation.

The more exact claim is substantial enough. For two years, a mathematician whose name carried unusual public authority helped produce and distribute a journal that connected scientific responsibility to the emerging politics of ecology. The journal in turn changed the terms of his refusal. Military money had forced a decision about where he could work. The living Earth forced questions about how anyone should work, eat, learn, speak, heal, use energy, and share power. Survival had opened an exit from one institute. Living opened a field with no single door.
