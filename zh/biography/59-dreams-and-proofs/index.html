At Les Aumettes on April 30, 1987, according to the dating of the surviving typescript, Grothendieck began La Clef des Songes — ou Dialogue avec le bon Dieu. He continued the main text until September 13. The surviving work comprises six chapters in about 315 pages, though a table of contents projected 12. Around it accumulated a much larger body of notes, begun on June 3, 1987, and extending into early April 1988. The hosted sequence of notes reaches roughly 691 pages, with Les Mutants occupying a major portion. These are facts about manuscripts. They do not establish the truth of the manuscripts’ religious claims, and they do not authorize a diagnosis of their author. 11Alexandre Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes, ou Dialogue avec le bon Dieu (1987), I–III, Manuscript, https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/ClefI-II-III.pdf; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI; Alexandre Grothendieck, Les Mutants (n.d.), Manuscript, https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/Mutants.pdf; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*,” https://webusers.imj-prg.fr/~leila.schneps/grothendieckcircle/Clefsummary.pdf.

That editorial boundary is necessary because the text makes claims unlike those in Esquisse or Pursuing Stacks. A Belyi map can be defined, a theorem cited, a proof checked. La Clef des Songes states Grothendieck’s conviction that dreams are sent by a “Dreamer,” and that the Dreamer is God. It presents personal experiences, interpretations, memories, and a spiritual cosmology. A biography can identify when he wrote those statements, how he argued, what earlier events he recalled, and how readers responded. It cannot turn first-person certainty into external verification.

The opposite error is equally serious. To summarize the manuscript with a diagnostic label would bypass the words, dates, structure, and intellectual questions that make it historically legible. Retrospective diagnosis based on writings, without examination and outside a clinical relationship, is methodologically unsound. It can also become a device for dismissing religious language as symptom or romanticizing suffering as genius. This chapter does neither. It treats the texts as texts and observable events as events.22Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The manuscript’s first chapter attributes every dream to the figure it calls the Rêveur. The second identifies that figure with God. The structure then moves through Voyage à Memphis, reflections on mission, creativity, spiritual knowledge, and a return to the author’s life. Dreams initiate the argument, but the surviving chapters do not provide a catalog of dream narratives. Grothendieck often withholds the dream content and discusses the act of interpretation, the resistance of the conscious self, and the conviction that meaning comes from beyond it. 33The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*,” 1–4.

This absence is important. A title may lead a reader to expect a dream diary. The manuscript is instead a meditation on what dreams mean, where they come from, and how attending to them changes the interpreter. Because the underlying dream is frequently absent, an outside reader cannot reproduce the interpretive process. The evidence is Grothendieck’s report of conviction, not a shared data set from which his conclusion follows.44Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

He acknowledged that his conclusion was not a logical proof of God’s existence. According to the synopsis, he treated it as knowledge by experience and revelation. This distinction protects neither claim from criticism, but it identifies the genre. A theorem seeks public reasons that any competent reader can check under the same definitions. A revelation is presented as received by a person and may not be transferable. The manuscript addresses readers in hope that they will attend to their own dreams, not that they will verify his by calculation.55Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

A text with a date and a prehistory

The 1987 beginning did not mark Grothendieck’s first interest in dreams. Correspondence and later recollection describe attention to them earlier, especially from 1980 onward. In a 1980 letter he wrote of examining dreams and treating them as a rich source of meaning. In La Clef, he retrospectively identified an insight in August 1982 and a more intensive “religious instruction” beginning in late 1986. These dates are self-reported stages in his interpretation. They should not be narrated as externally witnessed supernatural events. 66Scharlau, Spirituality.

The prehistory overlaps with mathematics. In 1981 he was writing La Longue Marche à travers la théorie de Galois. In 1983 came Pursuing Stacks, the Faltings letter, and the beginning of Récoltes et Semailles. The final line of Esquisse reads “Janvier 1984”; a curated catalog instead assigns 1983, and Part X leaves that conflict unresolved. The chronological coexistence matters. Dreams did not arrive after a clean cessation of mathematical thought. Spiritual attention, mathematical meditation, gardening, correspondence, and community criticism occupied overlapping years.77Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The habit of dating sections connects the manuscripts materially. Grothendieck’s pages often record when a reflection began or resumed. Dates allow later readers to see that a claim changed. They also show long nocturnal sequences of writing. It would be tempting to infer mood or mental state from pace, but pace alone does not prove either. The safe inference is about work: he wrote extensively, revised by addition, and regarded the temporal path as part of the text.88Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The home at Les Aumettes appears in documentary accounts as secluded, near vineyards and Mont Ventoux. Grothendieck wrote in 1985 of a quiet country life, gardening and living with a black cat, seeing few people, and valuing solitude. Those statements can anchor place without staging an imagined night of revelation. The biographer does not know which window was open, what the sky looked like, or where a particular page lay.99Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Solitude had several meanings. It was practical protection from interruption. It was an ideal condition of creative listening. It was also social distance from mathematical institutions and many former colleagues. La Clef treats solitude as potentially fertile, but the historical effects were mixed. It enabled immense writing and made correction through ordinary dialogue less likely. Neither consequence proves that solitude was wholly chosen or wholly imposed.

The Dreamer and the standards of a claim

Grothendieck’s argument begins from experienced meaning. A dream, in his account, can surprise the waking self, expose resistance, and connect images in a way he did not consciously design. From this alterity he inferred an external source. He called that source the Dreamer, then identified the Dreamer with God.

Other explanations of dreams exist: memory consolidation, affective processing, symbolic association, neurophysiology, culturally learned interpretation, and chance. The chapter need not choose among them to describe his position. It must only avoid presenting his inference as forced. The fact that a thought surprises its thinker does not, by public logical necessity, establish an external personal sender.

The manuscript anticipates readers who might regard words such as God and soul as empty or superstitious, and it addresses them alongside sympathetic readers. This is one reason the manuscript is testimony rather than private notebook. It seeks communication across a difference in first premises. Yet because the central evidence remains experiential, communication reaches a limit. A reader can understand the structure of his conviction without sharing it.1010Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI.

The word “God” in the manuscript should not be assigned casually to a denomination. Grothendieck drew on Christian language, read Marcel Légaut and accounts of mystics, and referred to God, soul, mission, and revelation. He also developed an idiosyncratic vocabulary and did not submit the text to ecclesiastical doctrine. Labels such as Catholic, Protestant, New Age, or atheist-turned-believer would each require qualifications. The accurate phrase is that his late writings articulate a personal religious and spiritual cosmology in conversation with several traditions.1111Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Nor should biography reduce spirituality to belief propositions. The manuscript asks about attention, self-knowledge, creativity, fear, and the transformation of the person who creates. Some readers may reject its supernatural framework while recognizing those ethical questions. Others may read it devotionally. The historian records these possible receptions without assigning a correct spiritual verdict.1212Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Dreams and mathematical creation

There are formal similarities between Grothendieck’s descriptions of dream interpretation and mathematical discovery. Both begin with material not yet understood. Both require patient attention rather than force. A misleading image can organize confusion until it is recognized and released. A new relation can make scattered details fall into place. Writing participates in discovery as well as reporting it.1313Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

These similarities explain the chapter’s title. They do not make dreams proofs or proofs dreams. Mathematical proof is public, rule-governed, and revisable through shared checking. A dream interpretation can be personally compelling without a method that another person can repeat. The standards of warrant differ. To blur them would misrepresent both mathematics and spiritual experience.

Grothendieck himself drew broader connections among carnal, intellectual, and spiritual creativity. In the synopsis of La Clef, the act of creation transforms not only an external object but the creator. Mathematical work can therefore have a spiritual aspect in the relation between the person and the work, even when the theorem’s validity remains independent of that person’s spiritual condition. 1414The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*,” 2–4.

This distinction is subtle and useful. A proof of a theorem does not become false because its author is vain, cruel, or confused. Yet the choice of problems, treatment of students, acknowledgment of predecessors, and willingness to revise can be ethically better or worse. Mathematical truth and the life of mathematical practice are neither identical nor unrelated.1515Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Récoltes et Semailles had already treated creative attention as a listening. La Clef radicalized the source of what is heard. The earlier text could describe ideas emerging from an unknown depth without naming that depth God. The later text assigns agency to the Dreamer. Continuity in metaphor should not conceal the new ontological claim.

The late mathematical manuscripts also use exploratory language, but they retain definitions, diagrams, conjectures, and arguments addressed to mathematicians. Pursuing Stacks asks correspondents to correct references and structures. Les Dérivateurs compares formalisms. The spiritual texts address another form of recognition. The same author moved between them; the genres did not merge into one standard of proof.

Autobiography inside a spiritual journey

For biography, La Clef des Songes is valuable because it recalls childhood, parents, wartime internment, Le Chambon-sur-Lignon, early intuitions of God, mathematical life, activism, relationships, and the departure from IHÉS.

It is also hazardous. The memories are selected to explain a spiritual journey. Events that do not serve that journey receive little attention. Persons appear through their significance to the narrator. The text was written four decades after wartime childhood and nearly two decades after the IHÉS break. Contemporary records and other witnesses remain necessary.

Grothendieck explicitly denied that he was writing a conventional autobiography. He called the work a meditation or testimony intended to be public. This self-description should control its use. A sentence about his father may offer unique memory; it does not become the complete history of Sascha Shapiro. A sentence about his mother may reveal the son’s 1987 understanding; it does not exhaust Hanka Grothendieck’s life. 1616Scharlau, Spirituality, ch. 24, p. 5.

The section on leaving IHÉS offers a clear example. Grothendieck retrospectively used a nautical image of severed moorings to describe the departure. The image communicates how he later integrated the event into a spiritual history. The factual account still requires the funding records, resignation correspondence, positions of colleagues, and date of departure. Metaphor and archive answer different questions.

The wartime material demands special restraint. La Clef contains memories of Rieucros, Gurs, and Le Chambon. Trauma should not be used as a universal explanation for later belief or conduct. No source allows a biographer to draw a straight causal line from internment to a particular dream 50 years later. The chapter can observe that Grothendieck himself connected episodes in a spiritual itinerary. It cannot claim more.1717Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI.

The same rule applies to his father’s death at Auschwitz and his mother’s death in 1957. These losses are central to the life. They may appear in his own reflections on fidelity, calling, and silence. Explaining every later withdrawal through them would turn historical suffering into a narrative device and erase intervening choices and contexts.1818Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The architecture that exceeds the surviving book

The surviving main text has six chapters, grouped into 66 sections according to the hosted synopsis. A projected table of contents lists 12 chapters. It is uncertain whether later chapters were never written, destroyed, lost, or remain elsewhere. The biography must use “surviving” and “projected,” not call the six-chapter state the author’s completed 12-chapter book.

The notes complicate the boundary. Notes 1–57 accompany the earlier chapters; notes 58–144 form a much larger sequence associated with the projected chapter on “mutants.” The term names figures whom Grothendieck saw as ahead of their time or outside ordinary social formation. The list and assessments belong to his spiritual-historical scheme. They are not a scientifically established human type.1919Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The hosted Les Mutants file extends to hundreds of pages. Its scale shows that “notes” can be larger than the main chapter they support. As in Récoltes et Semailles, supplementation becomes an independent mode of composition. A critical edition must decide whether to place notes beside their triggers, preserve their separate sequence, or provide both routes.2020Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The surviving material was typed by an experienced typist and corrected by hand, according to the source account. This again places solitary testimony inside a chain of material collaboration. The typist’s identity and exact contribution require archival care. A clean typescript can make a text appear finished even when its architecture remains provisional.

Grothendieck at times imagined La Clef as the third movement of a larger sequence concerned with physical, intellectual, and spiritual creativity. The earlier Éloge and Récoltes et Semailles supplied other movements in that retrospective arrangement. Whether he held this architecture consistently is less important than what it reveals: he was organizing manuscripts across domains as one inquiry into creation.2121Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Six surviving chapters, read as claims

Chapter I moves from the importance of dreams to their asserted source. Grothendieck describes interpretation as penetration through layers of resistance. The image is archaeological: meaning is present but covered. A skeptical reader may instead regard meaning as produced in the interpretive act. The manuscript argues for discovery, not construction.2222Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

That difference matters because it determines authority. If meaning is discovered, an interpretation can be more or less faithful to a message already there. If meaning is constructed, several readings may be possible without one external sender. Grothendieck’s language permits revision but assumes a real intended message.2323Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The chapter also distinguishes ordinary dreams from especially forceful ones while maintaining that all come from the Dreamer. The category is therefore universal in source but variable in urgency. The text offers no independent test by which another person can determine whether a particular interpretation is correct.

Chapter II identifies the Dreamer with God. Grothendieck treats personal experience as primary and logical proofs of God as beside the point. His argument is testimonial: he presents the conclusion as knowledge gained through experience and invites readers to attend to their own.

The chapter’s God is not introduced through systematic theology. The Dreamer knows each person and sends individually shaped messages. This relation gives dreams purpose. It also makes failed understanding part of the receiver’s resistance rather than evidence against a sender, a structure that can become difficult to falsify.

Chapter III, the first Voyage à Memphis, turns toward biography. Parents, childhood, adolescent belief, wartime experience, and the mathematical vocation are arranged as stages in a long wandering. “Memphis” functions inside the manuscript’s symbolic geography; it should not be converted into evidence of literal travel.2424Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI.

The chapter reports an early recognition of God in adolescence and later moments interpreted as calls. Because these memories were written in 1987, their safest use is double-dated: an event assigned to an earlier year and an interpretation articulated in 1987. A contemporaneous diary would carry different evidentiary weight.2525Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Chapter IV examines mission and freedom. Grothendieck resists, at least in part, the grandiose model in which mission means delivering one universal doctrine. He links mission to truthful witness and the release of creativity. The chapter treats that position as a claim within the verified manuscript and does not extend it through later uncorroborated reports.2626Scharlau, Spirituality.

The chapter gives Eros a place in creativity and criticizes social repression. This belongs to a broader late inquiry into physical, intellectual, and spiritual creation. A biography should summarize it cautiously because the manuscript’s sexual ethics include claims requiring their own contextual and legal review. No sensational excerpt is needed for the present period.2727Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Chapter V turns to spiritual knowledge and the quality of mathematical activity. Grothendieck can distinguish the truth of mathematical laws from the spiritual state of the mathematician. This supports the genre boundary used in this chapter: a person may prove a theorem while failing ethically, and a spiritual insight does not establish a theorem.2828Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

He also reflects on beauty. Mathematical beauty becomes evidence of a relation between person and form, not a proof of a particular theology. A secular reader can recognize the phenomenon of beauty without accepting its divine source. The text often moves from shared experience to metaphysical conclusion; the transition must be marked.

Chapter VI returns to the life after 1970, activism, communes, solitude, and the perceived failure of collective movements to transform inner life. The surviving section 57 retrospectively casts the IHÉS departure as cutting moorings. The account joins political ethics to spiritual independence.

Across all six chapters, actual dream narratives are sparse. The book’s true repeated object is interpretation itself: how a person listens, resists, names, and reorganizes a life. This is why the title can mislead a reader seeking images and episodes. Its “key” is a theory of attention.

Self-knowledge and its hazards

Grothendieck calls self-knowledge central to spiritual life. Without it, he argues, images of other people are projections of fear, hope, frustration, and desire. This insight has an ethical force independent of his dream theory. A critic of others should examine his own stake.2929Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Récoltes et Semailles demonstrates both the force and difficulty of the rule. Grothendieck subjects himself to long examination, yet his judgments of former students remain contested. Introspection does not guarantee knowledge of another person. It can reveal projection without eliminating it.3030Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Dream interpretation intensifies the same risk. If every image is treated as a message about hidden resistance, the interpreter can always find confirmation. A robust method needs conditions under which an interpretation fails. The manuscript’s personal framework supplies experiential correction, but not a public protocol comparable to proof.3131Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The chapter can state this methodological difference without diagnosing why Grothendieck accepted the framework. Beliefs have intellectual structures that can be analyzed. Explaining them solely by cause—childhood, isolation, illness, genius—would avoid engaging those structures.

Self-knowledge also has a social limit. Other people can correct one’s self-image. Solitude may permit honesty, but dialogue can expose blind spots inaccessible from within. Grothendieck often treated collective pressure as an enemy of truth; his opened correspondence shows that particular friends could also be sources of correction.3232Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Spiritual interlocutors and traditions

Marcel Légaut, a French Christian thinker who emphasized personal faith and spiritual becoming, was an important interlocutor in Grothendieck’s reading. The relation was intellectual and, at points, personal. Légaut offered a language of mission and authenticity outside rigid institutional doctrine.3333Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Grothendieck also read about Marthe Robin, a French Catholic spiritual figure, and referred to her in notes from June 1987 onward. The verified point is textual influence. Claims about Robin’s body or sanctity are neither required nor evaluated here.3434Scharlau, Spirituality.

The Findhorn community in Scotland, an early intentional ecological community associated with New Age spirituality, also entered his field of reading. Its combination of ecology, community, and claimed divine guidance overlapped with concerns present since Survivre et Vivre. Similarity does not prove wholesale adherence.

Christian vocabulary, anarchist suspicion of institutions, ecological critique, psychoanalytic reading, and personal dream interpretation therefore met in the late texts. No one label contains the combination. “Spiritual writings” is a descriptive umbrella, not a denomination.

Religious studies offers a useful distinction among experience, interpretation, tradition, and institution. Grothendieck reports experiences; interprets them as divine messages; draws from several traditions; and remains institutionally independent. Keeping the four levels separate avoids both theological endorsement and reduction.3535Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

His word “mission” similarly has levels. It can mean the ordinary task of becoming truthful, the writer’s task of testimony, or a prophetic role in a coming age. These senses shift across documents. A sentence saying “he believed he had a mission” is too vague unless the date and sense are supplied.

A gap in the 1990 record

The available record does not contain an independent primary witness for the 1990 circular, its distribution, the reported follow-up, or recipient reactions. Scharlau’s later account supplies a chronology and quotations, but it does not independently corroborate the sensitive facts it reports. This account therefore leaves out the recipient estimate, the forecast’s detailed content, the account of circulation, and the follow-up sequence.3636Scharlau, Spirituality.

The gap is the historical result available. No account of recipient response, motive, effect, or change of confidence is given here. 3737Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Evidence from memory

When La Clef supplies an otherwise unknown childhood detail, a biography should apply four checks. First, identify the manuscript page and composition date. Second, ask whether the event has a contemporaneous record. Third, seek independent testimony. Fourth, preserve uncertainty if only retrospective memory survives.3838Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI.

Names require similar care. Private correspondents represented by initials should not be expanded because another website guesses an identity. The text’s biographical value does not override privacy. People alive during late archival collection may not have consented to publication.

Precise dialogue is prohibited unless a letter or transcript supplies it. A phrase remembered decades later can be introduced as recollection, not staged speech. The temptation is especially strong in childhood scenes; the result would be historical fiction.3939Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI.

Location detail should be proportional. Rieucros, Gurs, and Le Chambon are historically significant and documented. The exact room in which a dream occurred is not supplied and need not be invented. Les Aumettes can be named as the place printed or reported on the manuscript.4040Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Causal claims require the highest restraint. A parent’s death may be followed by a period of withdrawal, but sequence does not prove one complete cause. Grothendieck offers his own causal interpretations; they should be marked as his. The biographer may compare them with records, not replace them with another total explanation.4141Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI.

Literary form without literary license

The prose of La Clef uses recurring figures: the child, soul, boss, Dreamer, Mother, whisper, mission, and journey. These make the text literary even when it rejects literary pretension. An analysis can trace them without assuming they are fictional characters.

The “whisper” figure is especially important. God’s voice is described as quiet in a world of shouting. This extends the earlier ethics of attention: truth is not necessarily what institutions repeat most loudly. The figure can inspire and can immunize a belief from criticism, because external disagreement becomes noise.

The “journey” gives narrative direction to disconnected memories. Events separated by decades become stations. Such arrangement produces meaning after the fact. It is neither false for being retrospective nor factual because it feels coherent.

The “book” repeatedly comments on its own unexpected development. Grothendieck observes that it no longer resembles what he intended. This metafictional quality is actually documentary: the text records the author losing control of plan while retaining control of typing and sequence.4242Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Literary analysis must not grant license to reproduce long copyrighted passages. Short headings suffice to identify the architecture. Paraphrase can communicate argument while the notes preserve locators. Dreams, psychoanalysis, and non-equivalence

Grothendieck reportedly sought books on dreams and knew of Freud and other traditions. His theory, however, rejected a purely intrapsychic source. It should not be described as a version of Freudian dream analysis on the basis that both attend to hidden meaning.4444Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

Freud’s framework treats dreams through wish, repression, condensation, displacement, and individual history. Jungian approaches add archetypal and collective dimensions. Religious traditions may treat dreams as revelation. Grothendieck’s account borrows no single system wholesale.

The distinction between source and use is crucial. A person can read a theory and oppose it. Citation in correspondence proves contact, not adherence. A comparative chapter would need exact reading lists and dates, which remain outside this account.

No contemporary neuroscience can retrospectively decide the personal meaning of a specific unrecorded dream. It can study mechanisms of dreaming and memory. The biography should not enlist science as a rhetorical weapon where data are absent.

Coexistence in the archive

In the archive, mathematical and spiritual pages may share handwriting, paper, and dates. Classification separates them for readers, but the material maker did not live in separate folders of identity. He could write a spiritual letter, return to derivators, and handle practical bills.4545Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

This coexistence resists two myths. The first says mathematical genius proves spiritual authority. The second says spiritual extremity proves mathematical incapacity. Both infer across domains without examining the work.

The derivator manuscript provides a particularly strong control because it is structured, technical, and addressed in part through expert correspondence. Its presence after the late-1980s spiritual manuscripts makes any narrative of permanent mathematical cessation before then false. It does not settle the quality of every section.4646Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

Conversely, technical competence cannot validate a spiritual claim or erase an evidentiary gap. Standards remain domain-specific; a dated mathematical manuscript proves only that mathematical writing continued.

Drawn as a timeline, the record shows overlap rather than succession: the complements to Récoltes et Semailles, La Clef, its notes, and Les Dérivateurs run in parallel. Parallel bars rather than a staircase prevent the misleading idea that one genre permanently replaced the previous.4747Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

Dreams in letters and dreams in the book

Correspondence sometimes gives dates and reactions absent from the main manuscript. Grothendieck told friends that interpreting dreams absorbed time and delayed replies. He asked for books. He reported shifts in conviction. Letters therefore provide a running chronology beside the more composed testimony.4848Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The two witnesses have different audiences. A letter to long-standing friends can assume shared history and respond to their concern. La Clef addresses an imagined wider reader and organizes claims into chapters. A phrase should not be transferred from one genre to the other without its addressee and date.

Letters can also preserve immediacy that the book retrospectively orders. An uncertainty expressed in correspondence may disappear in the main argument; a conviction may later be dated earlier than surviving letters show. Comparing them reveals development without assuming deception.

The correspondence archive is incomplete from the biographer’s perspective. Recipients may have destroyed letters; replies may remain private; hosting sites select what to scan. Absence from the available archive does not prove a subject was never discussed.

Dream content raises privacy beyond the author. A dream may feature identifiable family or friends in symbolic roles they did not choose. Publishing it can expose intimate associations without factual relevance. This account quotes no dream narrative and uses only chapter headings.

The distinction between dream and report is also important. A typescript records a memory of a dream after waking and often after interpretation. It is not direct access to a sleeping experience. Date the report even when the dream date is supplied.

Reception and health: the limits

The primary spiritual manuscripts establish what Grothendieck wrote; they do not establish how every recipient reacted. No complete response archive has been verified for this account. Reactions, alarm, consent, and practical care therefore remain outside the narrative rather than being inferred from profession, silence, or later recollection.

The same rule excludes the reported fasting episode from body-level biography. The available record contains one later secondary account but no independent contemporary witness for so sensitive a fact. Duration, physical condition, intervention, intake, and motive are omitted. The chapter records only the evidentiary gap and makes no causal link to Les Dérivateurs, whose October 1990 start is reported by its editors and by Jean Malgoire rather than shown by a dated title page.4949Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), IV–V; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), VI.

This omission is not a claim that no event occurred. The available evidence is not strong enough to narrate it.

Physics and the boundary of expertise

The June 24, 1991, letter to “A.Y.” shows that Grothendieck was reflecting on physics as well as mathematics and spirituality. Late writings considered space-time, discreteness, determinism, freedom, and the relation between physical and psychic reality.5050Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

These interests connect to the 1986 topology notes, where he questioned whether physical space must be modeled as a continuum. The question has philosophical legitimacy. Any concrete physical claim requires evaluation against physics, not the authority of algebraic geometry.5151Scharlau, Spirituality; Grothendieck, La Clef des Songes (1987), I–III; Grothendieck, Les Mutants; The Grothendieck Circle, “A Brief Description of *La Clef Des Songes*”.

The manuscript tradition often calls such work a vast theory of physics. Page count and ambition do not establish scientific success. This Part uses the letter only to document subject and activity. A later chapter would need physicists to review definitions, predictions, and relation to established theory.

Grothendieck’s skepticism about points and continua has mathematical precedents: topoi can be point-free, finite spaces can encode shape, and quantum gravity motivates nonclassical space-time. Similarity of concern does not prove connection to a particular physical theory.

This boundary mirrors the spiritual one. A renowned mathematician’s statement outside his established domain deserves attention and ordinary scrutiny. Neither dismissal by credential nor acceptance by prestige is adequate.

Source language and respectful distance

Secondary accounts sometimes use psychiatric terms, ridicule, or devotional certainty. This chapter extracts dates and document descriptions from those accounts while declining their verdicts. Source use does not require adoption of every sentence.

Where a secondary writer speculates about what “must” have happened in Grothendieck’s mind, the claim is excluded. Where the same writer cites a dated letter, the letter can be traced and used. This selective method should be explicit so readers do not mistake silence for agreement.

Respectful distance also applies to Grothendieck. His own language about other people can be severe. The no-diagnosis rule protects him from unsupported labeling; it does not grant his labels authority over others. One evidentiary standard governs all persons.

French religious terms carry tone. Le bon Dieu can sound intimate, traditional, or ironic depending on context; “the dear Lord” is only one English possibility. The title remains in French where nuance matters. Short translations are identified as working translations.

Capitalization such as “Dreamer,” “Mother,” or “New Age” reflects personified concepts in the texts. The narrator uses capitals only when reporting that vocabulary. Typographic adoption can otherwise look like theological endorsement.

Reading without diagnosis

The no-diagnosis rule does not require euphemism. A historian can identify the religious and prophetic language present in a verified manuscript. What the historian cannot do is infer a clinical condition from selected texts, turn an uncorroborated later report into fact, or use clinical vocabulary as a literary verdict.

This discipline improves rather than weakens factual writing. Diagnostic labels often conceal uncertainty about dates and documents. They invite the reader to stop examining the argument. By contrast, a textual account can say exactly what was claimed, how widely it was sent, and whether the author later modified it.

Religious language deserves the same neutrality granted political or philosophical language. Neutrality is not endorsement. To write “Grothendieck stated that God spoke to him” reports a statement. To write “God spoke to Grothendieck” adopts it. To write “he imagined that God spoke” adopts a reductive explanation. The first form is the historian’s.

The rule also guards against romantic myth. The trope of the mad genius converts suffering and danger into evidence of depth. It makes readers spectators and can encourage the false belief that mathematical creativity depends on instability. Grothendieck’s proofs stand on mathematical grounds. His spiritual writings deserve reading on documentary and literary grounds. Neither requires mythologizing the other.

Renewed mathematical composition alongside other writing

Calling Les Dérivateurs a “return to mathematics” is understandable but incomplete. It suggests mathematics had been absent. In fact, mathematical ideas and letters persisted through the decade, though spiritual writing occupied much of the later 1980s. “Renewed sustained mathematical composition” is more precise.

In that editorial account, the derivator manuscript runs from October 1990 through the first half of 1991. That precise span rests on the editors’ report. Its subject grows from section 69 of Pursuing Stacks. A letter to Robert Thomason dated April 2, 1991, provides a concise primary orientation. The editors describe work on diagram categories, homotopy theory, model categories, accessible categories, and the structure called a derivator. 5656Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

The underlying loss can be stated more precisely. A homotopy category is formed by making weak equivalences invertible. That operation answers which objects become equivalent, but it need not remember every coherent diagram that existed before localization. Diagram-level constructions can therefore depend on information that is no longer visible in a category of objects and morphism classes alone.

In one common modern convention, a prederivator is a 2-functor

𝔻:Catop→CAT. \mathbb D:\mathrm{Cat}^{op}\longrightarrow\mathrm{CAT}.

For a diagram shape II, the category 𝔻(I)\mathbb D(I) represents coherent II-shaped diagrams in the homotopy theory. A functor u:I→Ju:I\to J induces restriction from JJ-diagrams to II-diagrams. Under derivator axioms, derived left and right Kan extensions provide the corresponding changes of shape in the other direction.5757Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

The terminal diagram shape recovers the underlying homotopy category, but the values at nonterminal shapes carry the additional structure that motivates the formalism. This is the precise kernel behind the less formal statement that a derivator remembers diagrams. It is not a claim that every detail of the original model can be recovered.

The span example shows the difference. Let II have the shape x←z→yx\leftarrow z\to y, and let ℳ\mathcal M be a suitable model category. A coherent span formed in ℳ\mathcal M can be localized as an object of Ho(ℳI)\mathrm{Ho}(\mathcal M^I). A diagram assembled only from morphism classes in Ho(ℳ)I\mathrm{Ho}(\mathcal M)^I may lack the higher compatibility needed for a functorial homotopy pushout. The two expressions therefore need not encode the same information.

For the unique functor p:I→ep:I\to e, a derived left Kan extension models the homotopy colimit of the coherent span. In this example it yields the homotopy pushout. The point is not the notation by itself; it is that the construction is attached to the diagram before its coherence has been discarded.

Cofinality provides a second true anchor. Replacing an indexing category by an appropriate cofinal subcategory can leave a homotopy colimit unchanged. The relevant criterion is homotopical, commonly expressed through contractibility properties of comma categories. Numerical density or visual similarity of diagrams is not enough.5858Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Maltsiniotis, La théorie de l’homotopie de Grothendieck; Cisinski, Les préfaisceaux comme modèles des types d’homotopie.

Modern adjectives such as strong and stable refine the theory. A stable derivator supports the diagrammatic behavior associated with stable homotopy theory and triangulated structures. These terms help explain later developments, but they must not be inserted into Grothendieck’s manuscript without a locator showing his exact usage.

The comparison with ∞\infty-categories marks the boundary. An ∞\infty-category can retain higher mapping data that a derivator may not detect. A derivator can still provide an efficient calculus of diagram shapes and homotopy Kan extensions. Neither observation makes one formalism a defective version of the other.

The manuscript also transfers geometric vocabulary into categorical homotopy, including analogues described as proper or smooth. Such names signal hoped-for base-change behavior, but only exact definitions and theorems justify the analogy. A shared word does not establish identity with geometric properness or smoothness.

Historically, the term appears in section 69 of Pursuing Stacks and, according to the unreopened editorial account, then becomes central to the manuscript that the editors date to 1990–1991. Heller’s 1988 memoir supplies an independent diagram-based line, while later authors develop derivators in several settings. Chronological priority, public availability, and causal influence must be recorded separately.5959Grothendieck, À la Poursuite des Champs (Pursuing Stacks); Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Heller, Homotopy Theories.

The biographical return is narrower than a claim about personality. After formal retirement in October 1988, Grothendieck produced a manuscript that Künzer, Malgoire, and Maltsiniotis report as 1,976 author-numbered pages organized around this and related homotopy-theoretic questions. Until the editor page and original pagination are reopened, that exact count remains attributed rather than established here. The reported scale supports continued mathematical labor. It does not validate unrelated spiritual claims, establish the correctness of every page, or make later derivator theory his exclusive work.

This seven-step explanation now belongs only here in Part X: the localization problem, a span as anchor, the diagram-category kernel, the model-category example, cofinality and ∞\infty-categorical limits, later historical development, and the retirement-era return. Chapters 58 and 60 are restricted to chronology and documentary consequences.6060Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

The terminology must remain double-dated. Section 69 is evidence for the appearance of the term and an early program inside the 1983 typescript. The editorial record reports a much larger development in the manuscript it dates to 1990–1991, but that precise dating rests on the editors’ report. The compact 2-functor description used above is a modern explanatory formulation; it is not presented as a verbatim definition copied from either witness.

That distinction affects every technical noun. “Diagram,” “localization,” “Kan extension,” “stable,” and “model category” have established modern uses, but the manuscript’s exact notation and axioms require leaf-level comparison. A clean modern sentence can explain the target while still misrepresenting what was available or asserted at the time.

The span is therefore an explanatory example rather than an attribution claim. It exhibits the difference between coherent diagrams formed before localization and diagrams of morphism classes formed afterward. It does not prove that Grothendieck used this exact example, wording, or notation in the same place.6161Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

The example also has a limit inside the biography. It explains why diagram data matters; it does not assess the manuscript as a whole or independently confirm the editors’ reported 1,976-page count. The text ranges across additional topics, and no one worked example can certify their correctness, originality, or completion.

Two kinds of specialist reading are still needed. A specialist must verify the modern mathematical explanation and its boundary. A historian of mathematics must compare the manuscript, the Thomason letter, Heller’s independent work, and later derivator literature without treating shared vocabulary as proof of influence.

The location in Chapter 59 serves a biographical purpose rather than a psychological one. The chapter already compares private interpretation with public proof. A derivator supplies a concrete case in which mathematical validity depends on explicit structures, functorial relations, and review by others. The contrast clarifies standards of warrant; it does not diagnose the writer.

A correct formula does not license a private claim, and a cautious sensitive passage does not certify a theorem.

The chapter therefore ends its technical work with explicit limits. It does not claim that the modern explanation reproduces every manuscript axiom, that the manuscript displaced later frameworks, or that mathematical productivity explains the surrounding life.

Chronology defeats a simple sequence of reason followed by unreason followed by reason. The same period contains spiritual correspondence, mathematical manuscripts, practical letters, and plans. Human activity does not obey the genre boundaries of a biography. The historian should not force those activities into a story of disappearance and recovery unsupported by the dates.

Nor does mathematical production certify every other judgment. The ability to write sophisticated category theory does not prove a prophecy. The failure of a prophecy does not invalidate a mathematical definition. Standards remain domain-specific.

The word “proof” in the chapter title therefore carries an ethical responsibility. In mathematics, proof makes a result available to anyone able to check it. It detaches validity from the authority or holiness of the prover. This impersonal feature was one of mathematics’ gifts to Grothendieck’s life. Yet he also believed discovery involved dimensions that proof did not capture: beauty, attention, trust, and transformation. The spiritual manuscripts explore those dimensions by another route.

Readers need not accept his metaphysics to see continuity in the questions about the source of a genuinely new idea, the distinction between received insight and projection, the kind of attention that lets a form emerge, and the way vanity can deform listening. In mathematics, communal verification supplies a powerful answer to the second question. In dreams, the manuscript relied on personal interpretation. That difference may be the deepest boundary between the genres.

Publication and circulation status

La Clef des Songes was written as if for readers and at points called a book. The surviving French typescript and notes circulated far less widely than Récoltes et Semailles. Digital scans later made chapters available through the Grothendieck Circle. Availability online is not the same as formal publication, and it does not settle rights.

This account therefore uses “manuscript,” “typescript,” “hosted scan,” and “editorial synopsis.”” It does not say that La Clef was published in 1987. It does not call the online files a critical edition. It reproduces only brief headings as quotations. This account assigns no circulation status to the reported 1990 materials. A later secondary account is not enough to establish the governing text, recipient list, translations, or mailing history. Those fields remain explicit gaps pending primary documents.6363Scharlau, Spirituality.

What an edition would require

A reliable edition of La Clef would need more than transcription. It would identify all surviving chapters, compare corrected typescripts, map notes to sections, distinguish authorial and editorial contents tables, and state which projected chapters have no witness.

The main text and Les Mutants notes should not be merged invisibly. Their dates overlap, but their functions differ. Hyperlinks can connect them while preserving separate pagination.

Religious vocabulary needs a glossary based on usage, not a theological system imposed by editors. “Dreamer,” “Mother,” “mission,” and “soul” change nuance. Cross-references can show development without declaring one final doctrine.

Biographical annotations should verify names, camps, schools, and dates through external records. They should not interrupt every memory with suspicion, but unique claims need corroboration. An edition of testimony is strongest when it makes verification possible.

Third-party privacy review is essential. Dream figures, relationships, health details, and correspondence may identify people who never sought publication. Initials in the source should remain initials unless public-interest justification and reliable identification exist.

The edition should include a chronology of composition and circulation. “Written 1987” is insufficient for notes continuing into 1988, while later reported materials must remain outside the chronology until their primary witnesses are checked. A timeline should distinguish the book’s argument from uncorroborated later claims.6464Scharlau, Spirituality.

Translation should involve both literary and subject expertise. A translator must preserve repetition, shifts of register, and technical mathematical vocabulary without smoothing the text into conventional theology. Notes should identify untranslatable wordplay.

Long quotations in a biography should wait for rights clearance. The existence of online scans does not make the prose public domain. The short-heading policy is a conservative rule of this account, not a legal determination.

Finally, the edition should state what it is not. It is not a clinical record, a proof of revelation, or a comprehensive autobiography. It is a scholarly presentation of a spiritual-literary manuscript by a mathematician, with substantial autobiographical content.

A place in the oeuvre without a hierarchy

Readers often ask whether La Clef is “as important” as the mathematical works. The question lacks one scale. Importance to algebraic geometry, to the author’s self-understanding, to historians of spirituality, and to family biography will differ.

Grothendieck at times ranked spiritual discoveries above mathematics. That ranking is itself a fact about his values. A mathematical biography need not adopt it, and it should not dismiss what he regarded as central.

The works can be related without a contest. Récoltes et Semailles is indispensable for his account of community and creation. La Clef is indispensable for his late spiritual self-description and memories. Pursuing Stacks and Les Dérivateurs contain mathematical programs. Each requires a different reviewer.

Placing the spiritual text beside mathematics also prevents the “lost genius” trope. The record shows an author who redirected attention, made claims of varying warrant, and continued technical work rather than a mind that vanished. The resulting portrait is less tidy and more humane.

It also prevents an opposite trope: that late mathematics proves nothing changed. The subjects, audiences, and standards of the spiritual texts are different. Continuity of intellect does not require sameness of life.

The appropriate connective word is “and.” He wrote mathematics and spiritual testimony; pursued public proof and private interpretation; and worked in solitude while maintaining selected correspondence. “And” preserves coexistence where “therefore” would invent cause.

This grammar matters for readers who identify with one domain. Mathematicians are not asked to accept revelation to value the late programs. Religious readers are not asked to treat a theorem as evidence of holiness. Each work can be approached under its own claims.

The archive’s plurality is the final safeguard against total explanation. No one manuscript defines the whole person. Biography arranges documents, but it should leave their differences audible.

What images cannot show

No illustration attempts to reconstruct a dream. The source withholds many dreams, and any painted or generated vision would replace absence with invention. It could also make the narrator appear to endorse one interpretation.

No “split portrait” opposes mathematician and mystic. Such design treats two genres as two personalities and invites diagnosis by layout. The chronology already shows coexistence without claiming division.

No devotional symbol is placed behind Grothendieck’s face. It would assign institutional religion and turn belief into iconography. No clinical imagery is used either. Both would pre-judge the text.

Image ethics are especially important in a spiritual chapter because readers look for visual confirmation of inner states. There is none. Documents can show what was written; portraits can show appearance at a date; neither shows revelation or diagnosis.

The refusal of spectacle is not refusal of narrative. Dates, genres, and consequences already produce movement. The chapter asks readers to attend to words and acts rather than decode a face.

This is also why the title uses “Dreams and Proofs,” not “Dreams versus Proofs.” The conjunction permits comparison of attention while the plural preserves different dreams and different mathematical arguments. “Versus” would manufacture a trial in which one mode must acquit or convict the other.

The chapter’s verdict is procedural rather than metaphysical. Attribute spiritual claims, verify public events, preserve correction, protect private people, and submit mathematics to mathematical review. Readers remain free to form religious and philosophical judgments beyond that procedure.

Such freedom is not neutrality about evidence. Unsupported sensitive details are omitted, and verified textual claims retain their stated limits. Respect requires calibrated consequence, not soft focus.

Accuracy also means admitting what cannot be known. The content of an unrecorded dream, the truth of an uncorroborated private report, and the inner meaning of a move are not gaps that elegant prose may fill. They remain gaps.

Those limits do not impoverish the chapter. They move attention toward what is unusually well documented: manuscript architecture, dated correspondence, public circulation, later correction, and renewed mathematical work.

The resulting account may frustrate readers seeking either revelation or diagnosis. That frustration is evidence that the biography has not converted a person into one of the available myths.

The biographer’s restraint

Late spiritual writings tempt narrators to use foreshadowing. A childhood event becomes a sign of future revelation; a 1970 withdrawal becomes the first step toward 1991 seclusion; a dream becomes prophecy of the archive. Such construction creates inevitability where the life contained alternatives. This chapter refuses it.6565Scharlau, Spirituality.

At each date, only what was then written or done is narrated. In April 1987, Grothendieck did not know the 2022 publication history of Récoltes et Semailles. In January 1990, recipients did not know he would write Les Dérivateurs months later. In April 1991, Thomason received a mathematical letter from someone whose public image would soon be dominated by disappearance. Hindsight can join these moments; it must not erase their uncertainty.6666Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.

Restraint also means allowing religious readers and secular readers to encounter the document without being told what sort of person can understand it. The biography describes a claim, its context, and its consequences. It does not shame belief or unbelief. It does not recruit the reader into Grothendieck’s mission.

The most secure conclusion is textual. La Clef des Songes extends the late project of meditation from mathematical life to spiritual self-knowledge. It contains important autobiographical material but is not a conventional autobiography. Its central thesis is asserted through experience rather than public proof. Its notes vastly enlarge the surviving book. Later sensitive reports remain gaps for this account. Mathematical writing resumed in a manuscript of nearly 2,000 pages. None of these facts requires a diagnosis.

By 1991, the genres converged not in doctrine but in an archive. Typescripts, notes, letters, mathematical diagrams, spiritual reflections, and practical papers occupied the same material world. When Grothendieck left Les Aumettes, much of that world remained behind. Retirement had already removed the institutional frame; departure would remove the address familiar to correspondents. Yet before the move he wrote one of his longest mathematical works and several dated letters. The final institutional transition joins prize refusal, pension, derivators, and the distinction between retiring, withdrawing, and ceasing to work.6767Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason.
