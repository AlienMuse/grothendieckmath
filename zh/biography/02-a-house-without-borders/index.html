Berlin, Paris, Brussels

Scharlau’s reconstruction places Sasha in Berlin under Tanaroff during 1921 and then in Paris; exact crossing dates and immigration status remain unverified. 11Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7. Border and police details are Scharlau’s reconstruction from family testimony unless otherwise stated.

During that stay, the family account associates police scrutiny with Sasha’s assistance to the Italian anarchist Francesco Ghezzi. Eine Frau supplies dialogue and comic detail that cannot be treated as a transcript. Scharlau reconstructs a move toward Paris after the encounter; no immigration file inspected here establishes the legal mechanism or status consequence. 22Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7.

Paris offered another network of eastern European refugees, but entry and residence remained unstable. Hanka’s novel describes Sasha being moved between borders and eventually obtaining recognition through the intervention of a comrade’s French wife. No official case file in the available record confirms the route or the intervention. The account is credible as testimony to repeated administrative insecurity, not as a scene with recoverable words. For someone without a dependable national passport, deportation did not necessarily produce a country willing to receive him. It could result in circulation between jurisdictions. 33Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7. Border and police details are Scharlau’s reconstruction from family testimony unless otherwise stated.

A camera for one hand

The first workshop job reported in Paris exposed the relation between disability and wage labor. An employer allegedly assumed that a one-armed worker would produce less and placed Sasha on piece rates. Once he reorganized the repeated motions and exceeded expectations, the employer proposed hourly pay. Sasha left rather than accept the changed arrangement. The episode comes through Eine Frau and may compress several experiences. Its importance lies less in an exact exchange than in the type of work it describes: bodily adaptation could increase output without granting control over the terms of employment. 44Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7. Border and police details are Scharlau’s reconstruction from family testimony unless otherwise stated.

Street photography appeared as an alternative. Comrades in Belgium used an instant camera that produced a small image on a metal plate within minutes. The operator approached passersby, made the exposure, processed the plate inside or beside the apparatus, rinsed it, and attempted a sale. The work required modest equipment, mobility, chemical skill, and the ability to create demand on the street. It also offered a form of self-employment. Sasha later explained the attraction in political terms: he neither hired workers nor sold his time to an owner, and the customer paid for an immediate product. 55Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7 and 13.

That account should not be confused with economic independence in a stronger sense. The photographer depended on crowds, weather, permissions, the cost of plates and chemicals, and police tolerance. A customer could decline the picture after it had been made. Winter reduced the trade. Mobility allowed Sasha to seek fairs and resort visitors, but it also exposed him to inspection at every border. The camera weakened one relation of dependence while creating others.

For a man missing his left arm, the apparatus demanded a remade sequence of movement. Surviving photographs show Sasha beside the camera, and Hanka’s later technical description explains how he used his right hand with pressure from a knee or foot to steady or shift the tripod and processing mechanism. The exact performance in the novel is highly shaped, but the material core is supported by the equipment and his visible body. He learned a craft whose tools did not assume his body and made them workable without concealing the injury. 66Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13. Technical and domestic detail is mediated through Hanka’s autobiographical narrative.

Belgian authorities eventually expelled him, according to the family narrative. They reportedly arrested him while he was working and did not allow him to retrieve his possessions. Among the items left behind was a manuscript about the revolution. No manuscript survives, and no inventory records what was lost. Hanka and Grothendieck both remembered Sasha as an aspiring writer who connected literary work with revolutionary witness. The loss can be reported as family testimony; the quality, extent, and contents of the absent work cannot be assessed. 77Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7. Border and police details are Scharlau’s reconstruction from family testimony unless otherwise stated.

The distinction matters because the vanished manuscript later became part of a family judgment about unrealized gifts. Grothendieck wrote about both parents as people with serious literary capacities whose works remained incomplete. That later assessment belongs to his understanding of them. It does not give access to the pages that disappeared or prove that a finished book would have resulted under different conditions. Arrest, expulsion, poverty, repeated moves, and the absence of a stable workspace were not incidental obstacles to writing. Neither were they a complete explanation for every unfinished project.

Sasha-Piotr in print

Scharlau reports a catalog finding of two pieces printed under the byline Sasha-Piotr in La Revue Internationale Anarchiste, dated November 15, 1924 and February 15, 1926, on anarchism in Bulgaria. The original pages have not been consulted here. The bibliographic lead may be stated, but authorship, original language, style, travel, and eyewitness knowledge cannot be inferred from it, and no line is quoted. 88Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7.

The byline adds another identity without resolving the first ones. Sasha-Piotr was neither simply a private nickname nor the surname recognized by the state. It was a name used within the movement and in publication. Together, Schapiro, Tanaroff, Sasha, and Sasha-Piotr identify one man only after a chain of evidence has been established. A matching given name or political affiliation is not enough, as the confusion with Sania Shapiro demonstrated.

The subject of the articles—anarchism in Bulgaria—also sets a limit. Publication under his name establishes an interest or claimed knowledge; it does not by itself establish travel to Bulgaria, membership in a Bulgarian organization, or personal witness to the events discussed. The note that the text may have been translated raises further questions about original language and editorial intervention. Until the pages are inspected, even small conclusions about style or fluency would be premature.

The CIRA bibliographic trail is valuable because it identifies a recoverable object rather than a circulating anecdote. Date, periodical, issue, title, byline, language note, and page can eventually be checked together. This is a higher-quality path than assigning unsigned material by resemblance. In a life crowded by aliases, bibliographic exactness is a form of identity work.

The two articles do not make Sasha a professional journalist, any more than Hanka’s attributed poem made her one. They establish publication within a political periodical culture supported by intermittent labor and voluntary networks. The distinction between a published writer and a person earning a regular living from writing matters for both parents. Each claimed a literary vocation while relying on other trades.

This shared claim may help explain the intensity with which they later revised one another, but it cannot prove compatibility. A pair can value the same vocation and disagree over whose time, income, and pages receive priority. The surviving evidence records aspiration and conflict more clearly than completed work. It should not be reorganized into the romance of two unrecognized authors destined to meet.

A bust and a permit

Scharlau reports that the sculptor Aron Brzezinski made and exhibited in 1925 a bronze bust designated Sasha-Piotr; Scharlau traced later testimony but had not secured the object for direct study. The report places Sasha within an émigré artistic circle more securely than speculation about whom he might have met in a famous café. 99Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7.

The city’s artistic and political networks overlapped. A refugee could write for a short-lived anarchist review, pose for a sculptor, earn money with street photographs, and depend on comrades for a room or a border intervention. None of these roles created a settled profession. They formed an economy of intermittent labor and mutual assistance whose resources were easily exhausted. Sasha’s gifts to people in need, later documented in Berlin, suggest that earnings were not kept separate from political obligation.

Scharlau places Sasha’s return to Berlin in the autumn of 1925 and reports an entry permit valid for eight days. The permit and its legal consequences have not been checked against an immigration file. What the family narrative supports is a return followed by prolonged documentary insecurity, photography, and a new domestic partnership.

Hanka and Alf

Scharlau gives May 18, 1922 in Hamburg as the civil marriage date of Hanka and Johannes “Alf” Raddatz, while describing their advocacy of free love. The original marriage entry has not been consulted here, and their reasons for marrying are not documented. 1010Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 12.

Hanka and Alf lived through irregular work and acute poverty. Eine Frau describes improvised schemes, debts, pawning household goods, small thefts, meals obtained without payment, and repeated dependence on relatives or acquaintances. Its episodes are literary and sometimes comic in form; they should not be staged as if observed by a neutral witness. The broader material condition is corroborated by movement between lodgings, incomplete work, and the family’s later recollections. The pair rejected regular employment while also encountering an economy in which secure work was scarce. Principle and necessity cannot be cleanly separated.

The novel describes a son born after the marriage who died after nine days. No civil record has been located in the supplied research, surviving relatives did not remember the child, and Grothendieck apparently did not mention him. The episode may record an infant death, combine experiences, or function within the novel. It remains unresolved. This biography cannot silently add the child to the genealogy merely because the account is detailed. 1111Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 12; Hanka Grothendieck, Eine Frau (n.d.), Unpublished autobiographical novel.

According to Scharlau, Frode Raddatz was born in Hamburg on January 24, 1924; the family knew her as Maidi. Hanka and Alf’s unstable lodging and income now affected a child whose care required other adults. The novel’s delivery scene has not been independently checked and is not reconstructed here. 1212Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 12.

Maidi spent substantial periods with her maternal grandparents. The arrangement may have protected her from the couple’s poorest lodgings and allowed Hanka and Alf to seek work, but the sources do not record a single agreed purpose or the child’s feelings. Care moved across households. That movement was not unusual in conditions of unemployment and insecure housing, yet it would become central to this particular family, whose children repeatedly depended on people outside the parental pair.

Hanka and Alf went to Berlin with the infant, reportedly traveling much of the route on foot while pushing a pram. The claim comes from the autobiographical account and has not been checked against travel or relief records. In Berlin they obtained occasional work connected with Die Welt am Montag, occupied furnished rooms and basements, and circulated through bohemian, anarchist, and left-wing acquaintances. The city celebrated in later memory for cabaret, theater, publishing, and visual art was also a city of housing insecurity. They inhabited its poorer edge. 1313Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 12.

The phrase “golden twenties” can obscure that difference. A premiere, gallery, or fashionable café does not place Hanka in its audience. No source permits a tour through famous venues on her behalf. Her Berlin is better located by jobs, addresses, debts, political contacts, and the later novel’s repeated attention to food and rent. Cultural innovation and deprivation were simultaneous without being evenly shared.

By September 1925, official papers indicated that Hanka and Alf were no longer living in marital union, although divorce came later. Their practical separation was incomplete. Alf remained within the same network, Maidi remained his daughter, and his papers and contacts would sometimes assist Sasha. A marriage could cease as a domestic partnership while continuing to govern surname, legitimacy, and the state’s account of a future child. 1414Scharlau, Anarchy, chaps. 12–13.

Alf should not be reduced to the husband displaced by a more forceful man. He was trained in mechanical work, had served during the First World War, wrote occasional verse, and moved politically from Social Democracy toward the Communist Party, which he joined in 1926. His later resistance activity under National Socialism lies outside this chapter, but it prevents an interpretation of him as merely idle or apolitical. The record shows intelligence, humor, repeated refusal of regular work, and continuing practical involvement with Hanka’s circle. It does not provide a stable psychological portrait. 1515Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 12 and 16.

A porous household

The network around them included the writer Theodor Plievier and his partner, as well as people who experimented with street photography and boatbuilding. A later memoir identifies Sasha as a source from whom others learned the instant-camera trade and links Alf to a boatbuilding project. Such testimony is useful because it comes from outside Hanka’s novel, though it too was written later. It establishes connections among work, politics, and friendship without requiring the picturesque scenes through which memoir presents them. 1616Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 14.

These circles were porous because resources were shared. Someone with wages supported people without them; a person with papers could lend them; a room became a meeting place; a craft moved from one worker to another. The arrangement embodied part of the anarchist ethic of mutual aid, but it also depended heavily on women’s paid and domestic labor. A community opposed to hierarchy did not automatically eliminate gendered expectations or unequal burdens.

Maidi’s care makes that dependence visible. The grandparents’ home in Hamburg provided continuity that Hanka and Alf’s movement could not. Sending a child to relatives may have been a response to poverty, work, and unsuitable lodging; the present sources do not rank those reasons. Nor do they support an emotional script of abandonment or rescue. What can be tracked is the transfer of daily responsibility and the financial obligation Hanka acknowledged toward her parents. 1717Scharlau, Anarchy, chaps. 12–13.

The transfer had effects on every household. The grandparents supplied food, shelter, and attention. Hanka and Alf gained mobility but incurred material and relational debts. Maidi belonged legally to her parents while living for long intervals within another domestic routine. When she later joined Hanka and Sasha, that change did not erase the grandparents’ role. Family continuity was produced across residences rather than contained within one home.

This history also resists the romantic language sometimes attached to bohemian poverty. Damp rooms, borrowed money, delayed support, and irregular meals were not proofs of artistic authenticity. They restricted work and shifted burdens toward relatives or better-paid friends. Rejecting a conventional career could be principled, but the practical consequences were borne collectively. The record is strongest when both the principle and the cost remain visible.

Hanka’s literary account can make the period seem unusually full because it preserves schemes, quarrels, reversals, and acute observations of money. That abundance should not be mistaken for complete surveillance of the past. She selected episodes, assigned pseudonyms, shaped dialogue, and stopped the surviving narrative at a charged threshold. Alf, Maidi, Sasha, and other members of the circle would not necessarily have organized the same years in the same way.

For that reason, Eine Frau serves at least three distinct functions. It records what Hanka later wished to preserve about her life. It supplies technical and social knowledge available to a participant. It is also a literary construction whose scenes and judgments require corroboration. Those functions can operate together. Calling the work a novel does not ban it from biography; calling it autobiographical does not authorize every sentence as fact.

The same caution applies to Scharlau’s use of family interviews. A later recollection that Sasha treated Maidi kindly has value as remembered relationship, particularly when supported by the way he signed a letter. It does not recover each year of her childhood. A relative’s dislike of Hanka records a durable family judgment; it does not prove the events offered to explain that judgment. Testimony must be dated in relation to the life it describes.

The legal marriage to Alf remained a more contemporaneous fact than any later interpretation. It determined Hanka’s civil name and would determine the presumption entered at Alexander’s birth. The practical partnership with Sasha was equally real but legible through other evidence: cohabitation, work, photographs, and later recognition of paternity. The conflict between these records is not evidence that one relation was authentic and the other false. It shows that law and lived household described different arrangements.

The coming birth would force those arrangements into sequence. First the hospital and registry named the married woman and her husband. Then divorce, a successful action concerning legitimacy, and Sasha’s acknowledgment altered the child’s documented position. The changes did not uncover a name that had always been waiting. Each was an act within a legal system, made at a date and preserved by a particular record.

Sasha enters the circle

Sasha’s return in 1925 occurred inside this social geography. His stated reason concerned a dispute among Russian anarchists over suspicions surrounding his escape from the Soviet Union. Eine Frau turns the confrontation into a scene of threats and theatrical gestures. Without another witness, the scene cannot be repeated as fact. What can be retained is that he came to address distrust within the exile community, lacked money for a return journey, and began using the instant camera on Berlin streets. 1818Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 7. Border and police details are Scharlau’s reconstruction from family testimony unless otherwise stated.

His eight-day permit did not provide the chain of documents needed for ordinary trade. A license required recognized residence; residence required acceptable papers; his Nansen documentation and earlier movements exposed the gap. At first he stayed unregistered with comrades or in cheap temporary rooms. The exact sequence of police summons and interventions comes mainly through Hanka’s novel. The recurring administrative problem is well supported even where individual conversations are not.

Sasha entered the circle through Alf. Hanka was away from Berlin when the men first met, according to the autobiographical account. Alf wrote about the unusual visitor, and Sasha reportedly saw a photograph of Hanka before meeting her. The novel supplies declarations, jealous confrontations, emotional reversals, and nights of quarrel. Because the manuscript was composed years later as literature, none of that speech can be reproduced as documentary dialogue. It records Hanka’s later construction of the encounter, not a stenographic record of 1925.

The relationship between Hanka and Sasha was established by about 1926. It displaced the marriage in domestic fact without dissolving all ties among the three adults. Alf at times lent Sasha identity papers or helped him deal with officials. Hanka remained legally married to Alf. Maidi stayed for long periods with the Grothendieck grandparents and later returned to the Berlin household. Affection, jealousy, political comradeship, law, and necessity drew different boundaries around the same people. 1919Scharlau, Anarchy, chaps. 7, 13, and 15.

Calling the arrangement a triangle would make geometry substitute for history. It was not a fixed three-person form. At different moments, Hanka and Alf were spouses, estranged partners, co-parents, political acquaintances, or people linked by practical aid. Sasha was Hanka’s partner, an exile assisted by Alf, and eventually a parental presence to Maidi. The sources do not establish that all parties accepted one theory of free love or lived by a negotiated plan.

Maidi’s place is clearest in later traces. She recalled Sasha as kind to her, and a surviving letter sent in 1933 was signed by him as her father. Those facts do not allow the years 1925–1927 to be narrated as a domestic idyll. They do prevent biological and social fatherhood from being treated as identical. Alf remained her legal father; Sasha sometimes acted in the role within the household that was forming. 2020Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 15 and 17.

Hanka and Sasha did not marry. That fact accorded with their criticism of bourgeois marriage, but it also left German law free to organize the family through Hanka’s existing marriage. The choice was therefore both ideological and institutional. Refusing a ceremony did not place the couple outside civil consequences. In 1927 those consequences had not yet been concentrated in a birth certificate; they were already present in names, residence, work licenses, and responsibility for Maidi.

The partnership combined shared ambitions with evident inequality. Both wanted to write. Hanka’s surviving manuscript shows sustained labor on an autobiographical novel, and the family remembered Sasha’s lost revolutionary history. Hanka described Sasha as an exacting editor of her prose. At the same time, Scharlau’s account says that he discouraged her from accepting newspaper work even when it could provide income. The source supports the conflict but not a complete theory of motive. A man committed to freedom from domination could still participate in gendered control.

This is not a paradox requiring an invented interior explanation. Political commitments do not automatically govern every domestic action. The exile economy made paid work, care, and writing compete for the same hours. Hanka depended on Sasha’s earnings and family assistance; Sasha depended on her legal capacity to obtain licenses and on her labor with the camera. Each could regard the other’s independent work as a threat to a shared project. The documents show the arrangement and its consequences more securely than they show intention.

Street photography

During the warmer months they traveled to fairs and resort areas to make photographs. In winter, when street demand diminished, the family account says they sang Russian songs in working-class taverns, with Hanka accompanying on guitar. The practice brought in coins rather than a stable wage. It also reveals how performance, political identity, and survival overlapped. A revolutionary song could be an expression of memory and a means of buying food. No specific evening can be recreated from the general account. 2121Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13. Technical and domestic detail is mediated through Hanka’s autobiographical narrative.

The small instant plate made photography unusually immediate. A studio portrait normally required an appointment, controlled lighting, processing, and later collection. Sasha’s apparatus promised an image during the encounter itself. The customer saw the operator expose and develop the plate, then decided whether to purchase it. That sequence made persuasion part of the craft. The photographer had to find a passerby, manage the apparatus, produce a recognizable likeness, and convert curiosity into payment before the person walked away.

Hanka’s novel describes the work in exceptional technical and dramatic detail. It gives prices, material costs, processing actions, crowd formation, and a division of labor between Sasha at the camera and Hanka rinsing, drying, framing, and selling the plates. The text is too carefully composed to be treated as a transcript of one workday. Its concrete knowledge nonetheless comes from practice. The apparatus, metal plates, visible amputation, and surviving photographs confirm the essential operations. 2222Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13. Technical and domestic detail is mediated through Hanka’s autobiographical narrative.

The method required Sasha to coordinate one hand with the tripod and processing mechanism. That coordination was not a sentimental triumph over disability. It was learned labor. The equipment remained awkward, the hours long, and the income uncertain. Emphasizing mastery without material cost would turn a work practice into inspiration. Emphasizing impairment without mastery would misdescribe what he did. The documented fact is adaptation at the level of repeated movement.

The sales technique also has political and ethical ambiguity. The novel portrays Sasha offering to take a picture without obliging the subject to buy it, then allowing the finished likeness to persuade. The arrangement shifted risk to the photographer, who paid for the plate whether or not it sold. It also relied on social pressure and the fascination created by the crowd. Self-employment did not remove commerce, performance, or unequal powers of persuasion. It changed who controlled the immediate labor process.

The price structure mattered. A cheap plate could draw people who would not enter a formal portrait studio, while optional fittings such as a brooch or protective glass increased the sale. Materials consumed much of the gross receipt. Hanka’s account of a highly productive day therefore cannot be read by taking the money collected as household income. Plates, chemicals, water, fittings, transport, and replacement equipment stood between receipts and food or rent. The business could be busy and the family still poor. 2323Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13. Technical and domestic detail is mediated through Hanka’s autobiographical narrative.

The customer bought both likeness and speed. The image emerged while the subject remained present, so the stages normally hidden in a darkroom became part of the public performance. Processing had to be quick enough to sustain attention and careful enough that the plate would not deteriorate immediately. The operator’s command of chemistry and timing was thus visible through the result even when the customer did not understand the procedure. Sasha’s political account of direct labor had a physical basis: production and exchange occurred in one brief encounter.

The street supplied no neutral workplace. Police could regulate hawking, crowds could block passage, and a useful location could be lost. The photographer had no employer, but neither did he control the public space on which revenue depended. Weather and season mattered, as did the willingness of taverns, fair organizers, or local officials to tolerate the trade. Travel expanded the market while increasing the risk created by Sasha’s papers. Independence was a practical degree, not an absolute condition.

The bodily division of labor was equally specific. Sasha controlled the exposure and much of the processing sequence; Hanka moved among customers, water, finished plates, frames, and payments. Her role required sustained bending, carrying, sales talk, and judgment about complaints. The novel’s admiration focuses strongly on Sasha’s concentrated rhythm, but its technical details also disclose Hanka’s exhaustion. A history that names only the photographer would lose half of the labor that made the day possible.

Their product entered lives otherwise absent from the archive. Most customers remain unnamed, and the tiny portraits cannot be assigned to a particular day without provenance. The trade nonetheless depended on workers and families finding a few coins for an inexpensive likeness. In a period when formal portraiture still carried cost and ceremony, the instant plate widened access imperfectly and temporarily. It also generated countless objects detached from the name of their maker, which is why attribution requires more than visual resemblance.

Sasha’s disability could itself draw attention, but the sources do not permit a claim that customers bought pictures out of pity. Hanka’s narrative stresses fascination with his control of the apparatus and his forceful public manner. That is her portrayal, shaped by admiration. The safest conclusion is that his visible body and technical coordination formed part of the encounter. Whether an individual customer felt curiosity, sympathy, unease, or respect is unknowable unless recorded.

The camera therefore joined three kinds of recognition. It made the customer’s face into an object. It made Sasha visible as a worker whose papers might not authorize the work. It made Hanka visible to licensing authorities as the proprietor or responsible trader. These recognitions did not coincide. The person whose name appeared on the license, the person who operated the apparatus, and the person shown on the plate occupied different legal and material positions.

Licenses and borrowed papers

The studio promised to align them by placing business, work, and residence at one address. Even there, Sasha’s name could not simply replace Hanka’s. His insecure status made her legal visibility essential. Conversely, the studio’s reputation and production depended heavily on his craft. The arrangement was neither a disguise alone nor a conventional family business. It was a practical answer to unequal access to documents.

Hanka’s work was indispensable. She helped create the crowd, handled wet plates, washed and dried them, offered brooches or glass covers, collected payments, and dealt with complaints about likenesses. In the commercial record, however, her name would later stand as proprietor because she could obtain the license, while Sasha’s embodied skill dominated the family narrative. The business inverted and reproduced conventional gender roles at once: the woman was the legal commercial surface, the man the celebrated operator, and both depended on the other. 2424Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13. Technical and domestic detail is mediated through Hanka’s autobiographical narrative.

The photographs themselves had a material fragility. A small plate could preserve a face after the encounter, but poor washing or residual chemicals could destroy the image. The object was inexpensive enough for a passerby and durable enough that some examples survived. This is not a metaphor for memory; it is a condition of the archive. The same trade that paid rent generated the family photographs through which later researchers recognized bodies, equipment, and relationships.

Sasha’s image work did not create legal transparency. He sometimes worked under Hanka’s license as her employee. Alf reportedly lent papers on occasions when officials threatened expulsion. A name on a license could belong to the person authorized to trade rather than the person making most exposures. A name on borrowed identity papers could present another relationship to the police. The household’s economic life depended on distinctions that official forms were not designed to express. 2525Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13. Technical and domestic detail is mediated through Hanka’s autobiographical narrative.

Those practices carried risk for everyone involved. Lending documents exposed Alf; unregistered residence exposed Sasha; obtaining a license connected Hanka’s legal identity to the enterprise. Mutual assistance was not an atmosphere but a set of acts with consequences. It also had limits. The same adults who helped one another could quarrel, separate, withhold income, or fail to send money for Maidi’s support.

The record of money is fragmentary but revealing. Hanka repeatedly owed support to the grandparents caring for Maidi. Income from photography arrived unevenly and was quickly consumed by materials, rent, food, travel, and help for others. When later family narratives describe chronic empty pockets, they do not necessarily indicate that the business failed on every working day. They indicate that revenue did not become reserves. Earning and security were different measures.

Brunnenstraße

Hanka’s autobiographical narrative places the couple’s move into a studio and dwelling associated with Brunnenstraße 165 near the end of 1927. Scharlau separately reports a 1929 Berlin address-book listing for Hanka’s photographic atelier at that address. The directory page and trade-license chain have not been consulted here, so neither source proves an opening day, lease, continuous occupancy, profitability, or division of labor. 2626Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13 and 15.

A studio altered the labor without ending the street trade. It provided storage for plates and chemicals, an indoor setting for portraits, a fixed commercial address, and rooms in which writing could in principle continue. It also required rent and made the enterprise more legible to officials. A fixed address could shelter a household and expose it. It gave the family a place in the directory while making loss of that place traceable.

Indoor work also changed the relation to customers. The street operator found people in motion and had minutes to complete a sale. An atelier could receive appointments, control a background, and keep equipment in place, but it had to attract customers to an address. The available record does not show the studio’s price list, client book, account ledger, or surviving portrait register. It cannot establish how much of the business moved indoors or which partner handled a particular commission.

The absence of those records limits the word “atelier.” A directory category may describe a small commercial room rather than a prosperous portrait establishment. No floor plan, props, waiting area, darkroom, or continuous residence can be inferred. Pending direct consultation, Scharlau’s report supports only a listed photographic business at the address. 2727Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13 and 15; Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929 (August Scherl, 1929), 1075, https://digital.zlb.de/viewer/metadata/34115495_1929/1/.

That distinction preserves the scale of the achievement. Securing a fixed workplace after years of border movement and temporary lodging was a material change even if the business remained poor. It did not need to resemble a fashionable studio to matter. At the same time, treating the address as proof of stability would ignore rent, licensing, and the continued street work on which the household may have depended.

The move into Brunnenstraße should not be narrated as arrival at stability. Eine Frau ends its surviving 1927 section as the rooms are being arranged and money is again short after a celebration. The scene belongs to the novel and is not reconstructed. The documentary point is that a licensed enterprise and dwelling were assembled from precarious labor. Their existence did not erase debt, police scrutiny, Maidi’s dispersed care, or the unresolved marriage.

Brunnenstraße lay in an area shaped by workers, small businesses, migrants, and eastern European Jewish life. It was close enough to the political and immigrant networks north of Alexanderplatz to make the address intelligible without inventing a street scene. No source describes the light in the rooms, their furniture, or the daily sequence of visitors. The address book and business history support walls, a commercial use, and a household; they do not furnish the interior.

Scharlau reports that a November 1927 relief-fund bulletin listed “Sasha Piotr” as a Berlin donor, gave the amount as twenty marks, and ranked it as the largest individual contribution in the German list. The original page has not been consulted here, so amount and ranking remain attributed to Scharlau rather than treated as direct evidence. 2828Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13.

The donation does not reveal the discussion that preceded it or the household budget from which it came. It demonstrates that Sasha directed money toward comrades despite his own insecurity. Placed beside missed or delayed support for Maidi, it also shows competing obligations. The point is not to expose a contradiction and declare a verdict. Political solidarity, parental responsibility, and immediate need drew on the same limited cash. The distribution of that cash had consequences for people who did not choose every commitment.

The source forms assign visibility unevenly. An aid bulletin made the donor legible. A directory made the licensed proprietor legible. Family arrangements made the grandparents’ care of Maidi essential but less likely to enter a public ledger. Hanka’s novel restored some of that work while shaping it through retrospective narrative. To reconstruct the household, no single source can be treated as complete.

Writing under one roof

The studio also gathered the pair’s literary ambitions under one roof. The surviving account says that they discussed and revised prose, and that each imagined a book capable of establishing a public life beyond photography. There is no finished collaborative manuscript from 1927 in the available record. Aspiration is supported; accomplishment cannot be supplied. Their status as writers should therefore be described through surviving publication, manuscript labor, application, and testimony rather than a prediction of books that never appeared.

An unfinished work remains historically significant without being evaluated as a lost masterpiece. Sasha’s absent revolutionary manuscript and Hanka’s eventual long novel occupied time, hope, and family argument. Their incompletion affected how the son later judged both parents. What cannot be known is whether further stability would have produced publication, whether the texts would have survived revision, or how readers would have received them. Counterfactual success is not evidence.

The contrast with photography is instructive at the material level. A plate had to be exposed, processed, and sold within minutes; prose could be revised indefinitely and deferred when money ran out. The camera generated a sequence of finished small objects even under precarious conditions. Literary projects demanded longer continuity. This difference in production helps describe the household economy without turning photographic immediacy into a theory of personality.

Hanka was pregnant before the end of the year. Eine Frau represents the pregnancy as wanted by her and initially opposed in principle by Sasha, who believed that revolutionaries living outside settled society should not have children. The novel also frames reproductive decisions through their relationship. Those private arguments cannot be narrated as verified dialogue. The pregnancy itself and the later birth are documented. Any claim about conception as strategy, rejection of maternity, or an attempt to bind the father belongs to retrospective interpretation and does not enter as fact. 2929Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13. Technical and domestic detail is mediated through Hanka’s autobiographical narrative.

This boundary is especially important because Grothendieck later developed an elaborate account of his earliest life from what his mother told him, family papers, and dreams. His writing records how the adult son understood birth and parental conflict. It cannot establish the mental state of an infant or recover an atmosphere experienced before language. Chapter 3 will separate the civil record of birth from those later interpretations rather than weaving them together into a scene.

A house without borders

At the end of 1927, then, the house existed in several incompatible senses. It was a fixed address with a studio. It was a legal enterprise under Hanka’s name, worked substantially by Sasha. It was linked to an unresolved marriage, a daughter spending much time with grandparents, a Russian wife and son beyond reach, and a network of people who crossed its practical boundaries. It sheltered a couple without giving Sasha secure national status. The title “house without borders” describes this documentary arrangement, not an ideal of carefree openness.

Nor was it a political model. Anarchism was present in the couple’s affiliations, donations, distrust of state authority, and preference for independent labor. Ordinary hierarchy persisted. Sasha’s view of Hanka’s paid work could be restrictive. Care for Maidi was displaced onto relatives. Legal privilege differed between the German partner and the Jewish refugee. Mutual aid redistributed resources without guaranteeing fairness. The history becomes less intelligible, not more, if “anarchist household” is made synonymous with equality.

The house was also larger than its residents. Rachil and Dodek remained connected through letters and photographs. Alf remained connected through marriage, paternity, papers, and friendship. Hanka’s parents cared for Maidi and absorbed some of the costs of Berlin life. Comrades assisted Sasha with work and protection and received aid in return. These relations were not scenery surrounding an autonomous couple. They were the structure by which the couple survived.

No invented domestic scene is needed to see the density of those relations. A license identifies who could operate publicly. An address book fixes a place. A donation list measures one act of solidarity. A photograph shows the camera and body at work. A later novel describes technical practice while demanding caution about dialogue. The gaps between these objects are part of the account. Filling them with weather, furniture, gestures, or a child’s imagined response would replace the archive with atmosphere.

This approach also prevents the years from becoming a moral preface to fame. In 1927, the future child had no mathematical identity. The parents’ aliases, mobile labor, and resistance to convention did not contain a theory of schemes or categories in embryo. It would be easy to turn the family’s unstable names into a symbolic origin of later abstraction. That connection has no evidentiary basis. The names matter because police, courts, employers, and relatives acted on them in the present.

What had changed since 1921 was concrete. Sasha had acquired a photographic craft, moved through Paris and Belgium, published two short political pieces, returned to Berlin, and formed a partnership with Hanka. Hanka had married Alf, become Maidi’s mother, moved into poverty in Berlin, separated in practice, and built work with Sasha. Alf had not vanished; Maidi’s care had not become simple; the state had not ceased to classify any of them. The studio gathered these consequences without resolving them.

The surviving portion of Hanka’s novel stops close to this threshold. Its silence is not proof that life became less eventful. It changes the evidence available. For the pre-studio years, the novel supplies abundant, if shaped, detail. For the birth and its aftermath, civil documents, address books, photographs, court proceedings, and later recollections carry more weight. The shift in source does not only alter the number of facts. It changes what kind of sentence can responsibly be written.

That shift also changes the narrative distance. A novel can follow a quarrel into a room and assign each participant words; a directory offers a surname, trade, and address; a court record reduces intimacy to categories with legal effect. None is a transparent window. The novel is rich in perspective, the directory in public placement, and the court file in institutional action. Reading them together requires preserving their differences rather than blending them into a seamless scene.

The years 1921–1927 demonstrate why source distance matters. A later account can describe border crossings while an uninspected passport cannot yet be made to answer them. A photograph can confirm an apparatus and a missing arm without recording the sales exchange. Scharlau’s report of a donation amount remains a lead until the bulletin page is consulted. The household becomes knowable through intersections, not through one all-sufficient witness.3030Scharlau, Anarchy, chap. 13.

Its openness was consequently both resource and exposure. Friends supplied work and documents; relatives absorbed care; political networks transmitted news and money. The same permeability made privacy difficult and obligations hard to delimit. This conclusion follows from the recorded movements and transfers. It does not require an assertion that any resident experienced the house in one particular way.

The next surviving institutional act was a birth notice dated March 28, 1928. Its names and legal effects belong to the next chapter.
