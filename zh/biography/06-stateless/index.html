The passport that did not supply a country

Scharlau reports that Tanaroff returned from Paris to Berlin in 1925 on a short permit and later encountered licensing difficulty with a Nansen document that Scharlau characterizes as evidence of statelessness. The uninspected permit and certificate leave issuer, number, validity, entries, and legal classification unresolved; the report establishes a practical obstacle only within Scharlau’s account.11Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 13.

League of Nations records explain the general identity-certificate regime created for Russian refugees in 1922, while Tanaroff’s certificate and its Berlin effect remain unauthenticated. General history clarifies why such a document might exist but leaves the individual file missing.22United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds), 1922 reports.

The general regime arose from a practical problem created by mass displacement after revolution and civil war. Russian refugees outside the new Soviet state could lack ordinary national passports while still needing identity and movement documents, and League records show an international response whose rights varied by holder and country.33United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds).

An identity certificate and citizenship answered different questions within that history. The League documents could make a refugee legible for travel or administration without granting nationality, which is why the existence of a Nansen-type document would not by itself identify a protecting state; the chapter can explain that general distinction while refusing to classify Tanaroff’s unseen certificate.44United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds).

The missing individual object remains decisive because its fields would show issuer, date, name, validity, and perhaps endorsements or travel history. Absent those fields, Scharlau’s terminology remains untested against the document and outside modern de jure or de facto classification, which requires legal analysis beyond this biography.

This difference between a general system and one person’s paper is easily blurred in narrative. A reader may recognize the Nansen name and assume a settled legal meaning, while the historian actually possesses only Scharlau’s report that such a document caused difficulty in Berlin; retaining the dependency prevents institutional history from masquerading as individual proof.55Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13; United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds).

The general regime nevertheless helps recover the human problem behind the paperwork. A refugee needed to cross borders, rent rooms, work, and appear before offices, yet each act could depend on documents issued under assumptions that another state or office did not share; legal uncertainty entered ordinary life through permits, licenses, registration, and the threat of removal.66United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds); Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 13.

Tanaroff’s political commitments intensified this dependence rather than eliminating it. Anarchism rejected the authority of states, but movement and work in Europe still required encounters with border and police institutions; the contradiction was practical, not evidence of personal inconsistency.77Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 13.

The chapter title therefore names a reported documentary predicament before it names a legal conclusion. It asks what Scharlau’s characterization can explain about work, movement, and family memory, while leaving the formal classification of Tanaroff’s certificate to an authenticated document and a legal study.

The names attached to Tanaroff come through different source layers. Family testimony preserves Shapiro as an original surname and Sasha as familiar usage, western records followed the assumed surname Tanaroff from 1921, and anarchist publications used an additional authorial name; the reported alteration mechanism remains unconfirmed, so name variation belongs to refugee practice rather than personality diagnosis.88Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 1, 6, 7.

Those names belonged to one historical life whose Jewish origin and anarchist history became increasingly dangerous under Nazi rule. The source record can follow usage and persecution without dividing the person into a real self and a merely documentary self, and without pretending that an unseen certificate settles legal status.99Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 1, 7, 15, 17.

The uncertainty begins with the paternal surname itself. Family testimony transmitted Shapiro, whereas western papers generally used Tanaroff, and no original birth register in the available record joins the two; Scharlau’s research nevertheless distinguishes Grothendieck’s father from the prominent anarchist Alexander Sania Shapiro, preventing the better-documented man’s activities from enlarging this biography.1010Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 1–2.

That distinction is more than a bibliographic correction. Two men with similar names moved through overlapping political networks, and an uncritical search could transfer congresses, articles, or friendships from one to the other; Scharlau’s separation of their records defines the evidentiary person whom this chapter follows.1111Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 2; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1.3.

Tanaroff became historically real through repeated use even if the name began in forged papers. It appears across migration, work, family, and later persecution narratives, which means that calling it false without qualification would erase the continuity by which western institutions acted upon him; origin and administrative use are different questions.1212Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7, 13, 15.

The reported transformation of Cyrillic letters offers a plausible account of how the assumed name arose, but the paper has not been inspected and the mechanism remains family testimony. The chapter therefore retains the broad fact of an assumed identity while excluding a letter-by-letter demonstration that would give the unseen object false concreteness.1313Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 1 note 25.

Familiar and political names carried still other functions. Sasha was ordinary personal usage, while the additional name in anarchist publications identified an author within a movement; neither form should be mistaken for a separate civil person, and neither settles the surname assigned at birth.1414Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 6.

The multiplication of names did not mean that Tanaroff could change identity without consequence. Each paper or signature linked him to a history that could enable work in one setting and expose him in another, while family members continued to remember an origin that official forms obscured; concealment and continuity existed together.

For Alexander, this paternal name history later became part of family memory rather than a nationality rule. The father’s assumed papers may shape adult understanding of exile while leaving the child’s Berlin citizenship unresolved.

This is why the chapter resists turning names into metaphors for the later mathematician. The documentary problem deserves historical treatment on its own terms, with no evidentiary link to an intellectual preference for invariance, abstraction, or changing presentation.

A card of uncertain legal scope

Scharlau reports expulsion threats, a period without registration, and assistance that allowed Tanaroff to remain in Berlin, but the episode comes partly through Hanka’s literary narrative and lacks the underlying order or receipt. Its specific fine and medical details are therefore removed from the event floor.1515Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

He also dates a Berlin police identification card to October 30, 1928 and says the document was accepted as valid. Because neither the card nor its issuing file has been examined, the chapter records only that secondary-source description and infers no residence class, legalization, naturalization, duration, renewal right, or end of statelessness.1616Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

Scharlau connects Hanka’s role as license holder for the photographic business with Tanaroff’s documentary difficulties, which may explain part of their commercial arrangement without defining either person’s legal status. The later appearance of Tanaroff in reported civil proceedings likewise shows administrative contact, not a general settlement of nationality or residence.1717Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

Photography reveals why document scope mattered in ordinary work. An instant camera was portable, but commercial use still involved permissions, residence, and a person under whose name the business could appear; Scharlau’s narrative links those administrative requirements to the choice of Hanka as registered proprietor.1818Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

The directly inspected 1929 address book confirms Hanka’s studio entry at Brunnenstraße, giving one secure point inside an otherwise mediated legal narrative. It does not show which permit Tanaroff held, whether he lived continuously at the address, or how an identification card interacted with the trading license.1919Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929, 1075.

Scharlau’s expulsion episode comes partly through Hanka’s autobiographical novel, whose dialogue and sequencing are literary. The broad pattern of documentary difficulty can remain attributed, while the precise fine, illness, benefactor, and official speech have been removed because the supporting files were not examined.2020Grothendieck, Eine Frau.

The 1928 identification card similarly supports only a source-history statement. Scharlau saw or worked from a document he rendered as valid, but without its title and file the biography cannot know whether validity concerned identity, residence, travel, or another administrative purpose.2121Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

Later civil contact does not resolve that ambiguity. A person may appear before one office for paternity or support while holding a provisional or uncertain position before police and border authorities, so administrative visibility should not be mistaken for comprehensive legal security.2222Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The difference matters in 1933 because a document accepted earlier could not be assumed to protect its holder after the state changed. Nazi persecution operated through political and racial categories as well as formal status, leaving the exact card less important to the broad danger than to any precise legal conclusion.2323United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”.

The chapter therefore keeps work and vulnerability connected at the practical level. Papers affected who could register a business, accept a commission, or remain in a city, but the missing files prevent a claim that one card legalized Tanaroff or that another document alone forced his departure.

Origins declared under an assumed name

Scharlau describes a 1930 Berlin declaration of origins made under the assumed surname and summarizes information about Tanaroff’s parents, but neither the form nor the private photograph used to discuss his mother was opened for this account. The chapter therefore omits the individual fields and treats the episode only as evidence that official inquiry continued under an identity whose origin remained disputed.2424Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 1.

This source history matters because an administratively usable name need not reproduce a birth identity accurately. Scharlau’s account allows the biographer to follow Tanaroff through western records while leaving the Shapiro surname, exact birth date, and complete family registry unresolved; the uncertainty concerns the record chain, not the reality of the person who lived under it.2525Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 1.

The child was not a copy of the father’s papers

Scharlau describes a sequence involving Alexander’s birth surname, a later paternity decision, Tanaroff’s acknowledgment, a change of surname, and youth-welfare guardianship. None of the underlying civil or welfare records was opened, so this chapter neither repeats their precise dates and terms nor treats the sequence as an authenticated legal chain.2626Scharlau, Anarchy.

The available sources do not establish Alexander’s nationality in 1933 or the legal effect of paternity, surname, guardianship, residence, or maternal documentation. The chapter therefore leaves his nationality unknown and sets the question aside rather than reasoning toward a probable answer from the 1913 statute, later institutional summaries, or either parent’s reported status.2727Reichs- und Staatsangehörigkeitsgesetz vom 22. Juli 1913, Reichsgesetzblatt 583 (1913); Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques, “The Scientific Heritage of IHÉS,” Virtual exhibition, https://expo-patrimoine.ihes.fr/?lang=en; CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques, 2014, https://lejournal.cnrs.fr/articles/disparition-dun-genie-des-mathematiques.

Family relations remain narratively relevant even when legal consequences are withheld. Scharlau follows Tanaroff as Alexander’s acknowledged father and reports a paternal relationship with Maidi, while Grothendieck remembered the four-person household as one family; those social facts do not settle citizenship, guardianship powers, or legal paternity beyond Scharlau’s mediated account.2828Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

No mathematical analogy is drawn from this distribution. Names and classifications mattered because institutions and adults acted through them, not because childhood paperwork anticipated a later theory of identity.

A nationality question left unanswered

The 1913 German nationality statute itself was consulted for this account, including its rules for children within and outside marriage, but a statute does not apply itself to a disputed family record. Alexander’s original registration, the later paternity decision, possible legitimation effects, surname change, and guardianship must be joined through authenticated files and period-law expertise before a nationality conclusion can be drawn.2929Reichs- und Staatsangehörigkeitsgesetz vom 22. Juli 1913, § 4.

Institutional biographies provide only the distant endpoint. IHES and CNRS state that Grothendieck was stateless and became French in 1971, yet neither source explains when statelessness began or how German law treated the 1928–1929 sequence; a later summary cannot fill the commencement of a legal status.3030Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques, “The Scientific Heritage of IHÉS”; CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques.

The contradiction between those sources is therefore real rather than verbal. The statute suggests possible routes to German nationality, while official later biographies describe a long period without nationality; resolving the tension requires the individual civil, passport, and naturalization chain, not a choice based on which summary seems more plausible.

This chapter closes the textual risk by refusing to use the missing answer. Alexander can be described as born in Berlin, as bearing different reported surnames, as living in the Berlin household, and as later entering foster care, but none of those facts functions as a proxy for nationality.

The same rule applies to paternal status. Even if Tanaroff’s individual certificate were authenticated and legally classified, a parent’s status would not automatically establish the child’s outcome without showing how the applicable law and family acts interacted. The biography therefore separates the father’s reported papers from the son’s unresolved chain.

Hanka’s documentation cannot silently resolve the problem either. Scharlau’s later consular material supports a continuing German documentary connection, but the original birth and paternity files are needed to know how maternal status operated in Alexander’s case.3131Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18.

Guardianship belongs to yet another legal relation. Scharlau reports youth-welfare involvement and later names a guardian, while the files needed to define appointment, powers, and duration remain absent; guardianship can organize care without serving as proof of citizenship.3232Scharlau, Anarchy.

Keeping these questions apart makes the chapter more, not less, precise. Nationality, surname, paternity, guardianship, residence, and family belonging interacted in life, but they are not interchangeable legal fields, and none should be used to backfill another when the governing record is missing.

The unknown status also limits the chapter title. It cannot apply to Alexander as a biographical label, and it cannot become a poetic description of family displacement; its scope remains Scharlau’s characterization of Tanaroff’s papers pending authentication.

Hanka’s names and her retained state

Scharlau follows Hanka under several surname forms associated with birth, marriage, divorce, and later consular documentation. His account supports keeping her documentary history separate from Tanaroff’s, but the 1939 consular record does not identify the paper under which she crossed at the turn of 1933/1934 or settle her earlier border status.3333Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18.

The broader contrast concerns dependence on state documents rather than a verified nationality outcome. Both adults opposed state authority in principle while relying on papers for work and movement, yet the available objects are too incomplete to compare their legal positions beyond Scharlau’s narrative.3434Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13, 15, 18.

Surname usage followed Hanka’s biography unevenly. Scharlau reports forms associated with Grothendieck, Raddatz, and a hyphenated combination across different records, which indicates changing social and administrative usage without proving that every office recognized the same form at the same time.3535Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The later consular renewal has a narrow value. It shows that a German authority issued or renewed documentation for Hanka in 1939, but it cannot identify what she carried in 1933, whether an earlier passport was withheld, or which legal status governed the crossing.

This distinction matters because family narrative often calls Tanaroff and Hanka husband and wife despite the absence of marriage between them. Social partnership, registered surname, and border documentation can point in different directions, and the biography must not make ordinary family language decide an administrative question.3636Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

Their anarchist commitments add another layer without canceling paperwork. Both adults resisted state authority, yet work, travel, and the placement of children still passed through institutions; reliance on documents was a condition imposed by political geography rather than a measure of ideological sincerity.

For the children, Hanka’s names signaled maternal relation and household history. They did not determine how an office treated paternity, nationality, or guardianship, which remain separate questions in the missing civil chain.

A remembered description

Dagmar’s late memoir recalled Hanka describing Tanaroff through both Russian origin and absence of nationality, while also connecting his departure to persecution after the Reichstag fire. Scharlau identifies errors elsewhere in the memoir, and no individual police file supports the remembered explanation, so the passage is retained only as evidence of family language rather than as a legal classification or enforcement history.3737Scharlau, Anarchy.

Scharlau is the principal source for the broader chronology: he reports that Tanaroff’s Jewish origin, anarchist history, and insecure papers made Germany dangerous, then locates him in Paris no later than August 8. Chai and Oort repeat this outline while relying substantially on Scharlau, and neither source supplies an individual order or exact departure trigger.3838Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18 note 51; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” secs. 1.3–1.4.

Dagmar’s way of identifying Alexander through his father’s Russian origin belongs to the same remembered family vocabulary. It shows that paternal history remained socially present in the foster narrative, but it cannot establish the child’s nationality or legal relation to Tanaroff’s papers.3939Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

The memoir’s value lies in reception rather than legal proof. It shows how the receiving household later remembered Hanka explaining the father and child, which terms carried family meaning, and how political danger entered the story; its known errors in date, place, and bodily detail prevent it from authenticating an individual paper or police event.4040Scharlau, Anarchy; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19.

The national chronology supplies the context Dagmar’s memory compresses. The emergency decree after the Reichstag fire suspended civil protections and widened detention, while subsequent measures intensified political and anti-Jewish persecution; those facts make the remembered fear intelligible without proving that all stateless people were treated identically or that Tanaroff received a particular order.4141United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”.

Source dependency remains crucial in this passage. Scharlau reports both the memoir and the broader family chronology, and Chai and Oort repeat his early-life account; agreement between those publications therefore reflects transmission as well as evidence and cannot be counted as independent corroboration.4242Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1.4.

Russian origin also operated differently from nationality. Family and foster narratives could identify Tanaroff or Alexander culturally through language, name, and paternal history even when no Russian state document protected them; social description should not be converted into citizenship.

This layered language helps explain why the chapter title needs a scope note. Statelessness entered the family story through Scharlau’s account of papers and Dagmar’s memory of Hanka’s explanation, while Alexander’s own legal status remains outside what either source can establish.

France did not resolve the paper

Scharlau’s account makes Paris intelligible as a place Tanaroff already knew through émigré contacts and street photography, but it supplies no authenticated permit, asylum decision, naturalization, or settled household for 1933. Hanka’s exact crossing paper is likewise unknown, and a later German consular record cannot be projected backward to define the legal terms of her journey.4343Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

The available record also leaves the parents’ French domestic arrangement uncertain. Scharlau reports later correspondence and periods of separation, which means that arrival in the same country cannot be narrated as the establishment of one stable replacement home.4444Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

France’s interwar role as a destination for immigrants and political refugees forms the broader setting for this uncertainty. Scharlau describes both the attraction of asylum and growing hostility, but the chapter does not infer Tanaroff’s permit or Hanka’s legal category from that national history.4545Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

The parents’ separate locations in later records also matter. A shared country did not necessarily mean shared residence, and the correspondence Scharlau reports for later years suggests a relation sustained across distance; the family’s Berlin household therefore had no simple French successor.

This distinction keeps refuge from becoming a moral verdict. Leaving Nazi Germany could be necessary for one adult and still have painful consequences for the children, while the later insecurity of France does not prove that the original decision was futile or foreknown.

The chapter consequently treats Paris as a reported endpoint in a continuing refugee history. It does not make France a homeland, a legal solution, or a stable family home that the surviving records cannot show.

Family homelessness is not statelessness

Grothendieck’s later account treats 1933 as the end of the four-person household, a claim about domestic history rather than a legal status shared by all four people. Hanka, Maidi, and Alexander cannot be classified through Tanaroff’s reported papers, and later foreigner or institutional descriptions cannot be carried backward to fill Alexander’s missing nationality chain.4646Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 17 and 607–608.

The distinction preserves the force of dispersal without turning it into a universal metaphor. Parents, children, carers, and offices acted through different relations, while the available records remain insufficient to adjudicate nationality, guardianship powers, or border status.

What the documents can bear

Scharlau’s biography remains the principal source for Tanaroff’s individual papers, civil contacts, and Paris terminus; the League of Nations record explains only the general refugee-document regime, Dagmar contributes late family memory, and Grothendieck contributes retrospective first-person interpretation. Chai and Oort depend substantially on Scharlau for early life and therefore do not add an independent evidentiary line.4747Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985); Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 1, 13, 15, 18; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1.4; United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds).

This source hierarchy permits a narrative about how papers were remembered and described, not a legal adjudication. Scharlau’s translated labels for a passport or identification card, his summaries of civil records, and his description of a private letter remain secondary reports; Grothendieck’s later use of foreigner language belongs to wartime memory; Alexander’s childhood nationality remains unknown.4848Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 17; Scharlau, Anarchy.

The hierarchy is not a ranking from useful to useless. Scharlau can provide a researched chronology and identify objects that require retrieval, Grothendieck can establish his own retrospective language, League records can explain an international regime, and official later biographies can supply the 1971 endpoint; error begins when one source is asked to answer another source’s question.4949Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985); Scharlau, Anarchy; United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds); Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques, “The Scientific Heritage of IHÉS”; CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques.

Mediated civil records are strongest when described as Scharlau’s findings. They can explain why paternity, surname, and support enter the family history, but they cannot be quoted, reproduced, or given archive-level authority until register numbers and scans are obtained.5050Scharlau, Anarchy; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The private Paris letter occupies a similar position. Scharlau’s date and location can serve as an attributed terminus, while wording, signature, language, and rights remain unavailable; this chapter therefore uses no quotation or close reading from the object.

Dagmar’s memoir carries participant memory through another mediation layer. Its value lies in what the receiving household later remembered, but known errors and missing German locators require every scene and legal statement to remain indirect.5151Scharlau, Anarchy.

Chai and Oort offer scholarly compression rather than independence for early life. Their account can show how Scharlau’s chronology circulated into later mathematical biography, yet it cannot raise the confidence of a claim merely by repeating it.5252Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” secs. 1.1–1.4.

This explicit dependency prevents the word corroboration from doing hidden work. Two publications support independent weight only when they do not descend substantially from the same testimony or private object, and the early-life evidence often fails that test.

The chapter’s source method is therefore cumulative but not additive. Different materials illuminate different relations, while repetitions that share an origin remain one evidentiary line.

The unverified guardianship chain

Scharlau reports youth-welfare guardianship and later names an individual guardian in 1939 documents, but the underlying files do not establish for this account when that appointment began, what powers it carried, or whether any official approved the 1933 placement. The chapter consequently makes no claim that Alexander held a particular administrative status, and it does not use later travel paperwork to reconstruct the earlier procedure.5353Scharlau, Anarchy.

The distinction between care and guardianship is essential. The Heydorns unquestionably supplied daily residence according to the retrospective chronology, whereas guardianship names a formal relation whose appointment and powers require records; one fact cannot be used to prove the other.5454Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 19.

Later travel permissions show that offices eventually entered Alexander’s movement, but they belong to 1939. Carrying those requirements backward would manufacture a 1933 approval process and could falsely identify the later guardian as the person already acting at the initial transfer.5555Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

The missing chain also affects language about parental authority. Hanka arranged the placement and remained his mother, Tanaroff was reported as acknowledged father, and the Heydorns assumed daily care; without the welfare file, the biography cannot assign final legal authority among them.

This limit is a matter of privacy as well as legal accuracy. Statements about a minor’s guardianship and family separation can affect reputations and interpretations of harm, so the chapter retains only necessary, attributed relations and removes speculative conclusions about consent or neglect.

The father’s condition in the child’s story

In Scharlau’s narrative, Tanaroff’s document problems affected work and accompanied removal threats, while Dagmar later remembered Hanka invoking statelessness in explaining the departure. Those sources show how the category entered family memory, but they do not establish what Alexander heard, understood, or legally inherited.5656Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 17.

Grothendieck’s own pages describe the 1933 family rupture and use foreigner language for wartime France, not for a legally adjudicated childhood nationality in Berlin. The chapter therefore refuses a retrospective myth of statelessness from birth and leaves later French education, internment, and adult naturalization to their proper chronological chapters.5757Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 17 and 607–608.

Names that survived movement

When the family dispersed, different names continued through family memory and Scharlau’s descriptions of western records. Tanaroff identifies the father in the administrative and migration narrative, Hanka appears under family and married forms, Maidi remains associated with Raddatz, and Alexander is known as Grothendieck while relatives used a Russian familiar name. These usages show social and documentary relations without proving nationality, citizenship, or the legal effect of any name change.5858Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1.1.

The distinction is especially important for Alexander. A familiar Russian form can carry family sound and paternal association without making the child legally Russian, just as the maternal surname cannot establish citizenship or uncomplicated acceptance by relatives. Names in this chapter are therefore historical usage, not compressed legal conclusions.5959Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

Names also reveal who produced each record. A civil office, political publication, family member, and later biographer could choose different forms for the same person, with each choice reflecting purpose and context; consistency in modern narration should not erase those historical differences.

The surname Grothendieck joined Alexander to his maternal line in ordinary biography, while Tanaroff connected him to a father whose own name history was disputed. Neither connection can be turned into a nationality rule, and neither determines the quality of later family contact.

Hanka’s changing forms similarly record marriage and later usage rather than one moment of self-reinvention. Scharlau’s documentation makes the variation visible, but only the original records could establish which form a particular office recognized.6060Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

This attention to names keeps the people from becoming examples in a theory of identity. The records are historically consequential because they affected work, movement, and family description, yet people exceed every administrative or familiar label attached to them.

Papers in daily life

The paper history mattered because it entered ordinary tasks. Street photography required access to public space and customers, a fixed studio required a registered business, and residence exposed Tanaroff to police administration; documentary uncertainty was therefore part of earning a living rather than a separate legal drama.6161Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

Hanka’s registered role in the studio shows how the household adapted. The direct address-book entry places the business under her name, while Scharlau credits Tanaroff with much of the camera work; cooperation across their different documentary positions made work possible without revealing the precise rule that required the arrangement.6262Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929.

Movement posed another problem. A short permit could bring Tanaroff into Berlin, yet staying, working, or returning involved different offices and documents; Scharlau’s narrative records recurring difficulty, but the missing files prevent a legal reconstruction of each stage.6363Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 13.

Family acts likewise passed through administration. Scharlau reports paternity acknowledgment, support, surname change, and youth-welfare involvement, which means the household was not outside public institutions despite its unconventional form; the original files are still necessary before those acts can be given exact legal consequences.6464Scharlau, Anarchy.

The same duality appears in political life. Tanaroff’s anarchism rejected state authority, yet the state could decide whether he crossed a border, registered a trade, or remained in a city; dependence on papers did not weaken the commitment, because it arose from the institutions that controlled movement.6565Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 7.

Names helped those institutions connect acts across time, even when the original surname was disputed. Tanaroff became the durable western identity under which work, family records, and later persecution were described, showing that an assumed name could acquire administrative continuity without becoming evidence of birth identity.

The family experienced that continuity differently. Hanka and the children knew paternal histories and familiar names that official forms did not fully capture, while later biographers had to join private memory to public documentation; neither archive could stand alone.6666Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4.

For Alexander, papers influenced circumstances before he could have understood them. Adult decisions about surname, paternity, residence, and care passed through offices, but no source records the child learning their legal meaning in 1933; biography should describe effects without inventing awareness.

Maidi’s record followed another route. Her surname and paternal history differed, and her later institutional association cannot be used to infer Alexander’s status or her own 1933 legal position; siblings in one household could remain administratively distinct.

The family’s dispersal made those distinctions more visible. Parents crossed into France under separate and incompletely known documentation, while the children remained in German care arrangements; physical separation did not unify their legal categories.

This practical history explains why statelessness should not become a literary synonym for homelessness. The household ended, residence changed, and documents constrained movement, but only Tanaroff is described as stateless by Scharlau, and even that description awaits the individual certificate.

The safest synthesis therefore follows actions rather than inferred status. People worked, moved, registered, corresponded, and entered care arrangements through documents whose exact powers varied; the narrative can show those encounters while leaving the unresolved legal fields open.

1933 as an administrative rupture

The political rupture of 1933 changed the practical weight of papers without producing one legal transformation shared by the family. Scharlau reports Tanaroff in France by August, Grothendieck later described separate care arrangements for the children, and Scharlau places Hanka’s departure around the year’s turn; together those events ended common residence, while the exact civil, guardianship, and nationality consequences remain unadjudicated.6767Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

Grothendieck’s retrospective language describes the experienced destruction of a household, whereas Scharlau’s statelessness language concerns the father’s reported papers. Keeping those levels separate prevents a family rupture from becoming evidence that Hanka, Maidi, or Alexander lacked nationality.6868Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The national crisis intensified that separation of scales. Nazi measures targeted political opponents and Jews, making Tanaroff’s history dangerous, while the family’s internal decisions about care and migration remained private acts whose exact deliberations are lost.6969United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”.

At the administrative level, Scharlau reports papers and office contacts whose legal effects remain uncertain. At the domestic level, Grothendieck remembered a household ending and children entering separate care; the two histories intersected but did not become one status shared by all.

The distinction also clarifies responsibility. Political persecution constrained the adults without mechanically determining each choice, while parental decisions changed the children’s daily lives without proving that the children inherited the father’s documentary position.

No single date can carry the whole rupture. Tanaroff was reported in Paris by August, Alexander moved near the end of December, Hanka left around the year’s turn, and Maidi’s exact 1933 route remains unknown; family dissolution unfolded through a sequence rather than one scene.

This chronology is complete enough for Part I because it identifies each supported transition. It remains deliberately incomplete where legal and institutional records are missing, allowing later evidence to enter without rewriting uncertainty as fact.

The eve of war, kept at its proper distance

Grothendieck’s retrospective account reaches forward to 1939, but this chapter stops at the turn of 1933/1934. The foster years, German schooling, departure from Blankenese, entry into France, French-language education, and wartime foreigner status belong to Chapters 7–12; no later paperwork is used here to define an earlier nationality or guardianship status.7070Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 17 and 608; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 19.

A document is not a homeland

Scharlau’s sequence of forged papers, a refugee certificate, a Berlin identification card, civil contacts, an origin declaration, and a Paris letter orders his documentary findings without authenticating the underlying objects. The chapter title refers to his characterization of Tanaroff alone and does not extend that status to another family member.7171Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 1, 7, 13, 15, 18.

Family sources answer different questions from administrative descriptions: the former show how people named relationships and remembered rupture, while the latter report how offices recorded particular acts. Neither set can replace the missing legal chain, and neither can be enlarged into an adjudication through narrative confidence.

What remains unknown

Four material gaps govern the chapter: Alexander’s nationality chain, Tanaroff’s individual Nansen certificate, the Berlin police identification and registration files, and any Tanaroff-specific 1933 enforcement record. Rather than soften those absences with probable legal outcomes, this account draws no conclusions that depend on them and keeps only attributed source history; Alexander’s nationality remains unknown.7272Scharlau, Anarchy.

The limits of retrospective synthesis

Later sources know outcomes that no participant could know in 1933. Grothendieck wrote after war, internment, both parents’ deaths, and a mathematical career, while institutional biographies knew the 1971 French endpoint; their hindsight can organize memory but cannot supply a missing childhood legal record.7373Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 17 and 607–608.

Grothendieck’s authority is strongest for his own later language. He can establish how he remembered family rupture, foreignness in wartime France, and relations with his parents, yet his meditation does not function as a civil register or legal opinion.

Institutional biographies carry another kind of authority. IHES and CNRS can state the public adult chronology, but their compressed descriptions of statelessness do not identify the commencement, applicable statute, or effects of the 1929 family proceedings.7474Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques, “The Scientific Heritage of IHÉS”; CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques.

Scharlau contributes the richest early documentary narrative. His access to private and administrative materials gives the chapter a path through names and papers, while the absence of archive identifiers and scans keeps that path secondary and open to correction.7575Scharlau, Anarchy.

The League of Nations archive contributes the general document regime. Its records explain why identity certificates emerged for Russian refugees, but they cannot establish which certificate Tanaroff held or how Berlin classified it.7676United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds).

These sources carry no additive weight merely through repetition. Chai and Oort rely substantially on Scharlau for early life, and Dagmar reaches the chapter through Scharlau’s translation; the dependency map matters whenever several names appear beside one claim.7777Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck”; Scharlau, Anarchy.

The resulting synthesis is deliberately uneven. It explains why papers mattered, how names changed across contexts, and why migration exposed the family to institutions, while nationality, guardianship powers, and individual enforcement remain unresolved until the missing objects appear.

This limit protects the child from retrospective legal labeling. Alexander’s later foreignness and naturalization provide no proof of statelessness at birth, just as paternal ancestry supplies no copy of Tanaroff’s documentary position.

It also protects the father from a simplified refugee archetype. Tanaroff was a photographer, anarchist, partner, and parent in addition to a person described through insecure papers, and legal uncertainty should not consume the rest of his biography.

No mathematical consequence follows from the paper trail. Later work on identity, structure, or presentation lacks a direct evidentiary link to childhood names, and the chapter refuses that form of biographical prophecy.

The remaining gaps require external expertise rather than stronger prose. Authenticated records and a legal-historical study could change the account; literary confidence offers no substitute.

The chapter title is kept, but its scope is narrow and visible. It refers to Scharlau’s characterization of the father’s reported papers, not to Alexander, Hanka, Maidi, or the family as a whole.

Two voices and their limits

The chapter has one directly inspectable family voice for its central rupture: Grothendieck’s later French meditation. It has one principal researched biography for the early paper trail: Scharlau’s Anarchy. Neither source can perform the other’s task.7878Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985); Scharlau, Anarchy.

The direct institutional sources also have narrow roles. League records explain refugee certificates, the German statute supplies general law, and later institutional profiles supply the 1971 endpoint. None authenticates Tanaroff’s paper or Alexander’s childhood status.7979Reichs- und Staatsangehörigkeitsgesetz vom 22. Juli 1913; United Nations Archives Geneva, League of Nations, Refugees Mixed Archival Group (Nansen Fonds); Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques, “The Scientific Heritage of IHÉS”; CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques.

The mediated objects remain useful as leads. Scharlau’s descriptions identify a certificate, card, civil acts, and correspondence that future research can seek. Their absence from the available record prevents quotation and legal conclusion, not acknowledgment that Scharlau reported them.8080Scharlau, Anarchy.

Family memory adds relation but not administrative effect. Dagmar remembered the father through origin and statelessness language, while Grothendieck remembered household rupture and later foreignness. These memories illuminate reception without settling law.

The chronology is therefore simple at its safest level. Tanaroff lived under an assumed western identity, encountered documentary difficulty, and left Nazi Germany; Hanka followed after arranging care, while the children remained in Germany.

The legal chronology is not simple enough to state. Missing original records interrupt nationality, guardianship, residence, and certificate classification, and the chapter leaves those fields open.

That division between historical action and legal status allows the prose to remain complete without pretending completeness of evidence. The life moves forward even where a form, ruling, or passport has not been recovered.

A family dispersed

Part I ends with a reported family dispersion, not a legal solution. Tanaroff and Hanka were in France according to Scharlau’s chronology, while the siblings stayed with different carers in Germany under statuses that remain distinct.

Alexander enters Part II in Blankenese with his nationality still unknown. Scharlau reports a guardianship framework whose original file and powers remain absent; residence and citizenship stay separate.

Tanaroff’s papers pass forward only as attributed source history. Later chapters may describe consequences documented by camps, borders, or institutions, but they may not treat the unseen certificate as already adjudicated.

Hanka’s documentation remains separate. A later consular record leaves her 1933 crossing undefined, and partnership supplies no basis for extending Tanaroff’s reported statelessness to her.

The political danger does remain part of continuity. Nazi persecution affected Jewish origin, political history, and oppositional households, although no individual police order explains every movement.

French schooling stays beyond this boundary. It begins only after Alexander leaves Germany in 1939, and the intervening German education belongs to Chapter 7. Part I ends before either development.

The open research tasks remain identifiable. Tanaroff’s certificate and police files could establish the father’s documentary position, while authenticated civil and naturalization records could establish Alexander’s chain; stronger adjectives offer no substitute for those objects.

The chapter therefore offers a complete source history rather than a completed legal history. It follows names, reported papers, work, migration, and family memory while leaving formal status to records and expertise not yet present.

That distinction is the final safeguard on the title. Statelessness is reported as part of the father’s documentary story, not inherited as the child’s identity or used as a metaphor for the family.

The chapter’s positive contribution is therefore historical rather than juridical. It follows the father’s changing names, refugee work, encounters with administration, and movement out of Nazi Germany, then shows how those experiences entered later family memory. It also separates the child’s unresolved nationality from every tempting shortcut. The result preserves a coherent life sequence while leaving the missing legal chain available for future records and expertise. Part II can begin from residence, danger, and family separation without inheriting a conclusion that Part I never proved.

Secure facts, open questions

At the end of Part I, the secure facts are relational rather than legal: Scharlau reports Tanaroff in France, Hanka followed around the year’s turn, the children lived in separate care arrangements, and Grothendieck later understood the shared Berlin household as ended. A single family nationality, authenticated guardianship chain, and legal effect for the father’s papers remain unresolved.8181Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

Chapter 7 begins from residence in Blankenese and a family history of danger, not from a settled nationality or guardianship status. Its account of his schooling, including any later French education, begins from that limited position rather than from legal conclusions supplied here.
