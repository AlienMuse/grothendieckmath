Two archives, not one heap

The phrase “Grothendieck’s archive” conceals two principal corpora and several systems of measurement. At the University of Montpellier is a fonds covering mainly 1949 through 1991, estimated by the university at approximately 28,000 pages. When the digital portal opened in 2017, roughly 18,000 pages were accessible online. The later archive reveals that seclusion was not textual silence. Taken together, the corpora approach sixty thousand pages in rounded institutional and participant estimates, but even that sentence needs caution. The impressive number should therefore begin the explanation rather than end it. The count must identify authorially numbered pages, archival images, included notes and duplicates, the meaning of “volume,” and the portion open to readers. Each unit describes a different fact.

How paper becomes a fonds

The Montpellier finding aid makes visible the labor between private papers and an archive. Grothendieck’s files were not arranged according to one consistent thematic or chronological plan, though many folders and subfolders bore titles in his hand. Archivists preserved those units where possible and marked titles they supplied. They numbered sheets in pencil, cleaned them, rehoused them, and digitized selected sides. Reused paper often carried unrelated material on the reverse, so a mathematical draft could coexist physically with an administrative letter or another typescript. 44Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”.

This material history is not ancillary. A reused verso can supply a lower bound for an otherwise undated note. A folder title can reveal the author’s intended grouping. A rusted fastener or separated bundle can blur that grouping. Digitization converts a sheet into images but does not automatically restore chronology.

The portal’s 18,000 accessible pages are not a statement that 10,000 pages vanished. Rights account for a substantial part of the difference. Letters by correspondents cannot be treated as Grothendieck’s words alone, and the university could not make them public without authorization. The archive thus distinguishes preservation from online access. 55Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”.

This is especially relevant to a biography of withdrawal. A restricted page remains part of the historical record even when the reader cannot click it. The absence of online access is not proof of destruction, censorship, or sensational content. It is an access status with legal and ethical reasons.

The two transfers reported by the finding aid also produced an archive with temporal layers. Material associated with the reported 1990 transfer may have been supplemented, reorganized, or contextualized by the reported 1995 transfer. Maltsiniotis says a copy of Les Dérivateurs came through Malgoire and credits him with comparing it against the transcription; the editor’s account does not lock the transfer’s legal status, exact date, or manuscript completeness. 66Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”.

An archive therefore includes remembered knowledge held by custodians. That knowledge can identify handwriting, recover a title, or explain a folder. It is valuable testimony, not infallibility. The finding aid attributes Malgoire’s datings and observations when they are his. This account preserves the attribution so that later evidence can confirm or revise it.

Four ways to count a page

A “page” seems like the simplest archival unit. In these fonds it can mean at least four things. First is an author’s number written on a sheet. Second is an archivist’s folio or image number assigned during processing. Third is a page in a typescript or later transcription. Fourth is a page in a printed edition. The totals diverge because the units answer different questions.

Authorial pagination reveals intended sequence, but authors skip, duplicate, or restart numbers. The Montpellier finding aid notes repeated and missing page numbers within Les Dérivateurs. A chapter may have two versions of one numbered page. An appendix may use its own series. Counting only unique authorial numerals would erase variants.

Archival digitization counts what must be represented. A folder, separator, annotated reverse, or alternative state may receive an image even when it lies outside continuous pagination. Blank versos may be omitted while crossed-out drafts remain because they show process. The result is a faithful digital surrogate, not a facsimile of the author’s table of contents alone. 99Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”.

A transcription changes density. Handwriting spread across several sheets may occupy fewer typeset pages, or one dense sheet may require expansion. Editorial notes add pages that are not Grothendieck’s. Line breaks and equations change format. Maltsiniotis’s 1,976-page figure must therefore remain an editor-reported manuscript-pagination claim, not the established length of a complete work or eventual edition.

Printed pagination changes again. The 2024 edition of La Clef des Songes has its own format, front matter, and postface. Its page count cannot settle how many typescript leaves or note pages the BnF holds. 1010Centre for Grothendieckian Studies, “*La Clef Des Songes* Published by Éditions Du Sandre”; Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck.

Boxes and volumes are equally different. A box is a preservation container assigned by a repository. A volume in the BnF catalog reflects the structure of Réflexions sur la Vie et le Cosmos as received and arranged. Forty-one volumes happen to occupy catalog units 3 through 43, while two earlier units contain La Clef. Saying “forty-three volumes of late reflections” would absorb the separate dream manuscript and misname boxes as volumes.

Approximation signs carry knowledge. Montpellier writes “about 28,000” and “about 18,000.” The Grothendieck Institute writes “about 30,000” for the BnF handover. These are not defective numbers waiting for a biographer to make them exact. They reflect counting conventions, processing state, and aggregate scale. 1111Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”; Université de Montpellier, “28 000 pages d’Alexandre Grothendieck”; Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques, “Some of Grothendieck’s Archives Published Online by the University of Montpellier”; Istituto Grothendieck, “Handover of Unpublished Manuscripts of A. Grothendieck to the BnF”.

Adding the rounded figures can provide an order of magnitude—nearly sixty thousand pages across the two corpora—but the sum should never be presented as an audit total. Overlap is not indicated, rules may differ, and “page” may not match. The visual for this chapter therefore places the figures in separate columns with labels rather than one towering bar.

Scale is still meaningful. Twenty-eight thousand pages required years of conservation work. Thirty thousand late pages required forty-one physical units and a detailed component tree. Fifteen thousand pages on one subproject make clear that Le Problème du Mal was not a short digression. Accurate units allow magnitude to speak without spectacle.

A chronology of the invisible work

In August 1992, Géométrie élémentaire schématique reopened a recognizably mathematical register. The BnF describes auxiliary results connected with Riemannian-Lorentzian manifold structure and links the work to the author’s earlier geometry. This was not EGA resumed under another title. It was one component in a new manuscript environment.

By October, Structures de la Psyché had begun. The catalog describes combinatorial structures applied to manifestations of individual and cosmic psyche. A companion text, Psyché et Structure, pursued similar relations in literary prose. The juxtaposition demonstrates Grothendieck’s attempt to move between formal and discursive languages. It does not show that the psyche is a mathematical object in the evidentiary sense established by the manuscript.

During 1993 the work shifted toward Le Problème du Mal, which became the dominant investigation for years. Its parts carry theological titles and combine metaphysical speculation, autobiographical material, structural reflection, and accounts of good, evil, sacrifice, cosmos, and creation. The BnF catalog necessarily summarizes some of this vocabulary. The biography attributes the vocabulary to the manuscripts rather than presenting its entities as verified.

At intervals, Esprit et Matière returned to physical theory. One section treats conservation of energy; another sketches a doctrine of microphysics. The interleaving of physics and theology reveals the ambition of the whole title Reflections on Life and the Cosmos. Grothendieck was not organizing independent disciplinary monographs. He was attempting a common account.

The years 1994–1996 are represented by a nearly continuous succession of volumes. Chapter and part numbers give the papers an internal order. That order matters against the dismissive word “scribblings.” Even the authorial label “Gribouillis” belongs only to specific later units, not to the entire corpus. A catalog of forty-one volumes with tables and notes is evidence of sustained organization, though not of correctness.

From 1997 into 1999, preparatory texts, summaries, creation notes, and “Gribouillis” continued. The sequence appears to narrow after the main bulk of Le Problème du Mal, but page totals alone cannot reconstruct intensity or daily schedule. A box may contain a dense revision; a long interval may be represented by fewer leaves. The archive supports date ranges, not a graph of inner life.

Walking the component tree

The BnF’s component tree permits a closer chronology than the broad titles alone. Volume 3, Géométrie élémentaire schématique, runs from August to October 1992. Volumes 4 through 6, Structures de la Psyché, begin in October and continue through April 1993. The first book, on exchange, gift, and reception, expands across the three volumes; a second book begins but remains incomplete. Volume 7, Psyché et Structure, belongs to the same span. 1818Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck.

Volumes 8 through 13 contain the first four parts of Le Problème du Mal, written from April through October 1993. Their internal headings move from the question of evil on earth to questions about humanity, God, moral knowledge, conditioning, spiritual knowledge, and sacrifice. Listing these headings establishes the project’s architecture; it does not accept the answers or reproduce intimate confessions.

Volumes 14 and 15 interrupt the physical succession with sections A through D of Esprit et Matière, whose dates reach backward to August 1992 and forward to January 1994. One section is explicitly titled for conservation of energy. The overlapping dates show that the author’s volume order is thematic and developmental rather than a simple diary laid down one notebook after another.

Volumes 16 through 33 return to Le Problème du Mal. Part V, “La Semaille,” occupies three volumes from October 1993 to April 1994. Part VI, “Le Cosmos,” occupies six volumes into December 1994. Volume 34 returns to Esprit et Matière with a section described as a sketch of a doctrine of microphysics, written from September to November 1996. Again, a physical-theoretical strand appears between theological volumes. Its placement makes the common-project ambition visible more clearly than a list that separates “science” from “religion.”

Volumes 35 and 36 continue part X of Le Problème du Mal from November 1996 through August 1997. Volume 37 contains preparatory and complementary notes for parts XI and XII extending to August 1998. Volumes 38 and 39 collect tables of contents, summary notes, and general tables. The presence of these navigational tools shows an effort to master the accumulated scale.

That last unit prevents a false claim that all BnF writing starts cleanly in August 1992. The named forty-one-volume work is dated 1992–1999 as a whole, while associated dossiers preserve material beginning in 1991. Catalog ranges can overlap because a later-assembled dossier contains earlier notes. The biography should state the level at which each date applies.

The tree also shows incompletion at several scales. A book within Structures de la Psyché is unfinished. Later parts of Le Problème du Mal survive as preparatory texts and summaries. Réflexions sur la Création is represented by a first part. Tables imply a projected whole whose state differs from a final edition. The archive is organized but not finished.

The sequence should not be narrated as a steady descent into one subject. Geometry comes first; psyche, evil, physics, cosmos, and creation overlap; formal and autobiographical registers recur. “Dominant” is more accurate than “exclusive” for the theological material. Even in the most extensive period, the component tree preserves interruptions and returns.

The archive as an argument in itself

Organization conveys claims before a reader reaches any sentence. To place geometry, psyche, matter, and evil under one overarching title says that they belong to a common field of reflection. To divide the work into books, parts, chapters, tables, and summaries says that the field can be ordered. To continue for seven years says that the ordering remained a live task.

Yet the archive’s architecture may reflect aspiration more completely than achievement. A table can assign a place to an unwritten chapter. A volume title can claim unity among heterogeneous notes. The catalog describes the author’s arrangement; critical reading asks how successfully individual pages realize it.

The manuscript also argues through recurrence. A theme revisited across volumes may gain complexity, but repetition can also reinforce confidence without new evidence. Page count cannot decide which is happening. Sampling must compare early formulation, revision, objection, and later summary.

For Le Problème du Mal, this means resisting quotation by extremity. The BnF press release summarizes the work in language likely to attract attention, including the manuscript’s concern with Satan. A biography concerned with evidence should examine connections among parts, distinguish autobiographical claims, locate formal notation and cited sources, and trace definitions across time. Sensational extracts answer none of these questions.

For Esprit et Matière, expertise must be interdisciplinary in fact. A mathematician can check formal manipulations but not alone adjudicate historical interpretations of Maxwell or contemporary microphysics. A physicist can evaluate physical claims but may need an archivist to establish sequence. A historian of science can identify context without validating equations. No single aura of expertise should cover the whole.

For Structures de la Psyché, the privacy threshold rises. A theory may classify aspects of persons known to the author. Publishing examples could expose intimate judgments. Before quotation, reviewers must determine whether the subject is identifiable, whether the claim has independent relevance, and whether paraphrase can avoid harm.

These requirements explain why cataloging is only the first visibility. A box can be open to a qualified reader yet unready for broad quotation. The page must pass mathematical, factual, privacy, rights, and contextual review. The source-card chain is slow because the manuscript collapses domains that the review system must separate again.

What the catalog cannot answer

The component dates do not reveal daily routine. A range such as April to June 1993 may contain dates in the manuscript, archivists’ inference, or both. It cannot show how many hours Grothendieck wrote, whether he worked every day, or what interrupted him. No schedule should be reverse-engineered from page totals.

The catalog cannot reveal motive. It can show that a physics section follows theological volumes; it cannot tell why he changed subjects on a particular morning. It can show that a project expanded; it cannot decide whether expansion felt joyful, compulsory, burdensome, or calm. Those adjectives would require dated self-report and careful context.

It cannot supply reception during composition. The papers may contain references to letters, but the public catalog does not map who read which volume in the 1990s. “Written in total isolation” is therefore too strong unless circulation is audited. The safer statement is that the work remained outside ordinary publication and professional review.

It cannot prove originality. An unpublished note may anticipate later mathematics, repeat known material, or pursue an idiosyncratic dead end. Dating establishes priority only after the content is identified precisely and compared with independent work. Claims that the boxes contain undiscovered revolutions are promotional until specialists demonstrate them.

It cannot determine mental state. Handwriting density, repetition, theological subject, or volume do not constitute diagnosis. At most they describe features of documents. The leap from graphology or topic to clinical conclusion violates both method and privacy.

It cannot settle authorial intention toward every page. The BnF’s use of the term bequest does not establish intention or legal effect for this account; the 2010 declaration addresses unauthorized publication; foldering may suggest order; labels such as “gribouillis” may suggest provisional status. Different signals can apply to different objects. Only inspected provenance and legal records can specify restrictions.

Finally, it cannot tell the story of what is absent. The limited report of destroyed non-mathematical papers does not identify every lost item. Missing chapter numbers do not prove burning. The endpoint in 1999 does not prove cessation. An archive is the history of survival, not a transparent outline of everything once made.

Recognizing those limits makes the catalog more useful, not less. Within its scope, it is extraordinarily precise: titles, containers, date ranges, internal subdivisions, repository, access procedure, and provenance. The biographer’s discipline is to ask it only the questions it can answer.

Formal structure and empirical truth

The late corpus poses a recurring category error. Because Grothendieck was a mathematician and sometimes used formal structures, a reader may call the whole project mathematical. Because many claims concern God or evil, another reader may call the whole project religious. Neither classification is adequate.

A formal system can be mathematically analyzed without its intended application being true. One may define a combinatorial structure and prove properties about it. To claim that it describes human psyche, physical law, or spiritual entities requires an additional bridge: observational evidence, conceptual argument, and engagement with the relevant field. Mathematical consistency does not supply that bridge by itself.

The reverse error is to assume that metaphysical context makes every mathematical page meaningless. Géométrie élémentaire schématique and portions of Esprit et Matière may contain technical arguments assessable on their own terms. Only specialist reading can decide. Dismissal by association would be as careless as endorsement by reputation.

The archive should therefore be sampled by claim type. Definitions and proofs go to mathematical review. Assertions about physics require historical and scientific review. Autobiographical statements require corroboration and dating. Theological propositions are presented as belief and argument. Privacy review governs intimate material even when factually relevant.

This modular method resembles the source-card system of the book. One page can generate multiple cards because it contains different kinds of claim. The unit of preservation is not automatically the unit of verification.

The many meanings of unpublished

“Unpublished” describes Les Dérivateurs, La Clef, and Réflexions sur la Vie et le Cosmos differently. Les Dérivateurs circulated through copies and partial electronic transcription; specialists could study substantial sections, even without a conventional critical volume. La Clef circulated enough to be summarized and partly scanned, was removed from some sites at a family request, and eventually appeared as a book in 2024. Réflexions remained a much more restricted manuscript corpus.

The word can refer to the absence of a commercial edition, the absence of a peer-reviewed edition, the absence of authorized circulation, or the absence of any readership outside the author. Those conditions should not be assumed to coincide. A mimeographed text mailed to hundreds may be unpublished yet public in effect. A manuscript deposited under restriction may be preserved yet unavailable. A web transcription may be readable yet editorially provisional.

This vocabulary matters for influence. Pursuing Stacks affected higher category and homotopy theory through copies long before formal publication. Influence can be documented through citations, correspondence, and later work. It should not be inferred only from the claim that a text “circulated.” Who received it, when, and what they used must be shown.

The late BnF corpus presents the opposite danger: projecting influence from fame. Because Grothendieck’s earlier ideas transformed mathematics, commentators may suppose that tens of thousands of unread pages must contain future revolutions. The proposition is possible and empty until content is identified. Archives offer research questions, not guaranteed treasure.

Publication also changes a text. Editors choose a copy text, standardize or preserve spelling, decide how to mark corrections, build indexes, and explain references. A facsimile preserves visual evidence but may be difficult to search. A diplomatic transcription aids reading but introduces decisions. A critical edition compares states and documents variants. Calling any of these “the manuscript” without qualification erases mediation.

Maltsiniotis’s editorial note is exemplary because it states conventions. Obvious corrections and editorial comments are placed in brackets; uncertainty receives a question mark; original page numbering is marked; the table of contents is editorial. Such declarations allow a reader to separate Grothendieck’s hand from later assistance. 2020Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”.

The BnF catalog performs a different editorial act. It does not transcribe every line. It creates hierarchical description so that a researcher knows which box and volume to request. Its titles may quote the manuscript, supply dates, and summarize content. The authority is archival: identity, provenance, arrangement, access. It is not a substitute for the arguments inside.

The ideal illustrated biography will show this mediation visually. A manuscript facsimile should carry archive ID and authorial page number. A transcription should identify editor and convention. A data diagram should say whether it counts boxes, images, or numbered pages. Such captions prevent design from presenting an archive as a transparent window onto thought.

Editors and custodians enter the story

The conventional account places assistants after the main action: the principal author creates, while others preserve. Grothendieck’s papers make that hierarchy impossible. Without Malgoire’s custody, the Montpellier fonds might have been dispersed or damaged. Without archivists, its order and restrictions would remain obscure. Without Künzer and Maltsiniotis, much of Les Dérivateurs would remain difficult to read.

This does not make them coauthors of Grothendieck’s mathematical claims. It makes them authors of access. Their work determines which marks become searchable symbols, which pages are connected, which uncertainty is visible, and which source future scholarship cites. A bad transcription can create a false theorem; an undocumented correction can create a false authorial sentence.

Custodians also make ethical judgments. Malgoire accepted papers from a former teacher under circumstances not fully public. The university later balanced opening with correspondents’ rights. Grothendieck’s children participated in transferring late manuscripts to the BnF. The BnF imposed a specific communication procedure. Each act shaped the boundary between private object and collective inheritance. 2121Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck; Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”; Istituto Grothendieck, “Handover of Unpublished Manuscripts of A. Grothendieck to the BnF”.

The word “rescue” should be used carefully. It can honor preservation while simplifying contested agency. To say a custodian rescued papers may imply that the author’s wishes were an obstacle rather than part of the history. The bounded verbs are reported transferred, kept, deposited, and cataloged; donated, owned, and copyright transferred require instruments not inspected here.

Editors of mathematical texts face an additional responsibility: not to improve an argument invisibly. Grothendieck himself reportedly valued the presence of mistakes as part of discovery in some manuscripts. Whether or not an editor shares that view, corrections must be distinguished from transcription. The objective is a usable text whose layers remain auditable.

For the metaphysical corpus, editing will require even broader restraint. Normalizing a private term into familiar theological language can change meaning. Removing repetitions can erase the rhythm through which a concept developed. Publishing autobiographical passages can affect living families. A critical edition needs historians, language specialists, rights review, and privacy review alongside mathematics.

The sheer scale distributes authority. No one reader can responsibly master all forty-one volumes across mathematics, physics, psychology, autobiography, and theology. Claims about “what the archive says” should identify the sampled component. A collective reading project is not optional; it is the only credible response to the range.

This restores an old theme in a new form. Grothendieck often presented mathematics as collective adventure even when discovery required solitude. The late archive was produced under extreme limits on collaboration, but its future understanding necessarily becomes collective. The invisible work summons another invisible work around it: cataloging, checking, translating, and refusing premature conclusions.

Access is not comprehension

Digitization can create an illusion of completion. When 18,000 pages appear in a browser, the archive seems “open.” In fact, optical access is only the first layer. Handwriting must be deciphered, abbreviations expanded, references located, chronology compared, and mathematical arguments checked. Search tools are limited when scans lack reliable text recognition.

Language also creates barriers. Most late manuscripts are in French; some mathematical correspondence is in English or German. Grothendieck’s vocabulary can be ordinary in one sentence and idiosyncratic in the next. Translation must decide when to preserve a repeated key word and when literal English would mislead.

Context is distributed across corpora. A 1991 note may refer to Pursuing Stacks, a letter held by a recipient, or a concept from SGA. A 1995 theological section may revise terms introduced in La Clef. The relevant source may sit in another repository under different access rules. Digital pages do not eliminate archival travel.

Comprehension also requires negative knowledge: knowing what has not been checked. A reader may identify a striking sentence in one volume without knowing that Grothendieck modifies it two thousand pages later. Tables and summary notes can help, but the corpus’s incomplete state means they may not be definitive. Responsible citation records the local context and the state of broader review.

This is why the chapter does not announce discoveries from the late papers. It reports what repositories and editors have established. Any new mathematical claim would require transcription, independent proof-checking, comparison with literature, and attribution review. Any biographical claim would require corroboration outside the author’s retrospective account.

The public romance of an archive ends at the door; scholarship begins inside. Boxes are not revelations. They are invitations to slow work under conditions set by provenance, rights, and method.

Scale without awe

Thirty thousand late pages are difficult to imagine, which is why the number often functions as an adjective: immense, obsessive, secret, unfathomable. A quantitative fact then becomes a psychological claim. This chapter refuses that conversion.

Page count can establish duration and material commitment. It can suggest the editorial challenge. It cannot measure originality, truth, suffering, or genius. A repetitive page and a decisive definition each count as one; a densely corrected sheet may contain more work than ten fair copies. Counting is indispensable and insufficient.

The same principle applies to comparison with earlier work. EGA and SGA are also vast, but their pages emerged through collaboration, seminars, publication, and reuse. The Lasserre papers were created under different conditions and contain different genres. Saying that the late corpus is “larger” by one metric would not show that it has greater mathematical content.

The accurate achievement is documentary. From 1992 through 1999, Grothendieck produced and organized a forty-one-volume manuscript sequence spanning geometry, psyche, physics, autobiography, theology, and cosmology. One subwork alone is officially estimated at about 15,000 pages. The sequence survived well enough to be cataloged box by box. Those facts need no inflation.

Nor does the archive support a romantic image of work with no possible reader. Tables, chapter numbers, corrected typescript, and summaries provide forms of future legibility even when publication was refused. The later institutional term bequest is not used to infer Grothendieck’s intention.

The invisible work became visible only after layers of other work: custody by Malgoire, family decisions, archival description, conservation, cataloging, transcription, and rights management. That later history belongs fully to Part XII. At the close of Part XI’s archive chapter, the important temporal fact is that the cataloged production stops in 1999 while Grothendieck lived for fifteen more years.

The papers therefore lead not directly to death but to a boundary in evidence. After 1999, the public timeline thins. A 2004 scholarly profile can confirm continued seclusion. A 2010 declaration can confirm one deliberate intervention. Beyond such points, the biographer encounters the temptation to substitute village stories for record. The last boundary is where that substitution must stop.
