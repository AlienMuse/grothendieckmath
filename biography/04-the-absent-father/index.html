Berlin in 1933: after the studio

At the beginning of 1933, the household no longer had the stable commercial trace supplied by the earlier photographic studio. The directly inspected 1929 address book lists Hanka Grothendieck-Raddatz’s business at Brunnenstraße 165, while Scharlau found no continuation after 1930; the evidence supports the loss of a fixed directory entry without turning that silence into a documented eviction.11Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929, 1075; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

This reduced resolution defines the chapter’s opening conditions. Hanka’s surviving manuscript ends before Alexander’s birth, the address books cease to provide a stable point, and later testimony suggests renewed mobility without a continuous address sequence; by 1933 the family remains visible through work, photographs, and memory, while the rooms in which daily life occurred have largely disappeared from the record.22Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Economic instability did not mean that every day was destitute. Tanaroff could earn money with the instant camera, and a good day in a busy place might produce many small sales. The detailed account in Eine Frau belongs mainly to the earlier street-photography period, and its totals cannot be projected onto 1929–1933. It does show why income varied: plates and chemicals had to be purchased, work depended on crowds, and the price of each image was low. A fixed studio brought rent and storage as well as shelter from weather. Losing the listing therefore marks the disappearance of an economic platform, not proof that photographic work ceased. Scharlau reports that Tanaroff continued to be known as a street photographer in Berlin and later in Paris.33Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13, 15, 18.

The camera also complicates the meaning of presence. A worker who had to go where customers gathered could be away from the household without having left it. Scharlau says that in earlier summers Hanka and Tanaroff traveled to fairs and resort areas for work. No itinerary in the available record establishes comparable trips after Alexander’s birth. The fact should therefore remain background, not become a childhood scene. It is enough to recognize that a mobile occupation and unstable registration made the father’s location harder to recover even before political flight.44Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

The address book and the camera describe two different kinds of work. The directory fixes a proprietor, occupation, and business address at the moment its information was compiled, whereas the instant camera belonged to a trade that could move toward fairs, streets, and crowds; neither source by itself shows how many days the family shared the studio or how much income reached the household.55Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929, 1075; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

Scharlau’s account of the photographic practice, based partly on Hanka’s literary manuscript and surviving images, is strongest at the level of material action. Tanaroff operated an instant apparatus despite the loss of his left arm, processed small plates, and depended on direct sales, while Hanka participated in the enterprise and held the registered business role; the vivid dialogue and crowd choreography in the novel remain literary and do not enter this chapter.66Grothendieck, Eine Frau.

A fixed studio altered that mobile economy without making it secure. It offered an address, storage, indoor work, and a business identity under Hanka’s name, but it also required rent and placed the enterprise within licensing and directory systems; when the listing disappeared after 1930, the archive lost a stable point even if Tanaroff continued taking photographs elsewhere.77Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

This distinction guards against two opposite exaggerations. The studio should not be treated as proof that the family had achieved lasting middle-class stability, yet its disappearance should not be narrated as a documented eviction into homelessness; the supported movement is from a direct business trace to a less resolved period whose settings survive mainly through later testimony.88Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Photography also affected what later survived. A household organized partly around image-making naturally left portraits, but those pictures record moments of exposure rather than ordinary relations, and their existence cannot establish harmony, fear, neglect, or the amount of time Tanaroff spent at home. The same object may confirm presence while remaining silent about care.99Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The father’s work therefore belongs to the chapter not as picturesque atmosphere but as a condition of the household. It supplied irregular income, required mobility, depended on Hanka’s registered role, and gave Tanaroff a recognizable occupation that he could resume elsewhere; when political danger finally moved him out of Berlin, the family lost both an adult presence and part of its working arrangement.1010Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13, 15, 18.

Maidi, Alexander’s older half sister, adds another measure of the household’s changing boundaries. Scharlau places her within the four-person Berlin household formed by Hanka, Tanaroff, Maidi, and Alexander, while also acknowledging uncertainty about when she returned from extended stays with maternal relatives. Alf Raddatz remained part of her reported legal history but not of this daily household; neither the exact duration nor the domestic arrangement can be recovered continuously.1111Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Tanaroff’s relationship with Maidi resists a simple biological hierarchy, although the private object that bears on it has not been inspected. Scharlau reports a letter from Paris dated August 8 and characterizes its closing as paternal, but this chapter uses neither wording nor signature as direct evidence; it relies only on Scharlau’s date and location for the exit terminus. Grothendieck’s separate retrospective statement about receiving no paternal letters during the later foster years remains his testimony, not an explanation of Tanaroff’s conduct.1212Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15 note 51; Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 608.

A household on Berlin’s precarious edge

The studio belonged to a part of Berlin in which migrants, small trades, political networks, and crowded housing overlapped, but the address does not authorize a cinematic neighborhood. Scharlau places the family near Eastern European Jewish and émigré life around Brunnenstraße, while the directly inspected directory supplies only a business entry; no source identifies the view from the rooms, the neighbors on a particular day, or the path the children took through the district.1313Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

What can be recovered is a social position. Hanka and Tanaroff moved among writers, workers, anarchists, and refugees without occupying the prosperous cultural Berlin that later memory often treats as the whole city; their household depended on irregular work and informal relations, and its political commitments did not remove rent, food, childcare, or documentary requirements.1414Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

The parents’ literary ambitions formed part of that economy rather than an alternative to it. Scharlau reports collaborative writing, an unsuccessful application by Hanka for literary support, and plans that produced less completed work than the adults hoped; the lost application and Hanka’s incomplete manuscript show aspiration and interruption, but they cannot be used to diagnose talent, discipline, or parental commitment.1515Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Political generosity could also compete with household resources. Earlier records described by Scharlau include donations to persecuted anarchists, while the family narrative repeatedly mentions money owed for Maidi’s care; placing those facts together shows competing obligations, although no ledger allows the biographer to calculate what one payment prevented or which adult made each decision.1616Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

Maidi’s presence keeps the household from becoming merely a preface to her brother. Scharlau’s account places her with Hanka, Tanaroff, and Alexander during at least part of the Berlin period, and later memory suggests that Tanaroff sometimes acted in a paternal role toward her; her exact movements between maternal relatives and the Berlin home remain uncertain, so the chapter does not turn intermittent residence into a continuous domestic scene.1717Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

Alexander’s early childhood is even less continuously visible. Photographs and his adult recollections confirm a remembered family relation, but the available record does not contain a diary, school record for these years, or contemporaneous observer capable of narrating ordinary days; the absence of such material is why work, address, and later testimony must carry separate parts of the account.1818Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The household’s unorthodox structure should not be romanticized as freedom from institutions. Marriage law, licensing, surname usage, police registration, and youth welfare all appear in Scharlau’s narrative, while private choices governed partnership, work, and care; the family lived through both systems at once, accepting some relations and resisting others without gaining immunity from administrative power.1919Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

By the beginning of the 1930s, the studio no longer supplied a directory trace, and the family’s settings become correspondingly harder to follow. That reduced resolution matters because it precedes the political crisis: before Tanaroff left, the archive had already shifted from a directly locatable business toward later accounts of mobility and scarcity, making the final Berlin years less suited to scene and more dependent on carefully separated source layers.2020Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

A family remembered afterward

Grothendieck’s account of the Berlin years was written at a formidable distance. In Récoltes et Semailles he located his investigation of the family rupture in 1979–1980, after both parents had died, and described working from correspondence and a small residue of memory; his prose therefore records an adult inquiry into 1933 rather than a transcript of what he knew at five.2121Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

That distinction governs his recollection of the first years. He remembered unusually strong attachment to both parents and a particular identification with his father, but this later testimony neither proves a uniformly peaceful household nor licenses a scene of the child observing Tanaroff’s work or waiting for his return. It establishes how the mature writer understood the relation that preceded the break.2222Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Scharlau’s reported family photographs provide a different and narrower form of presence: they place people before a camera without demonstrating the quality of domestic relations. His interviews also preserve reports of parental quarrels and temporary absences, yet their source chain cannot establish a particular night, sequence, farewell, or childhood response; these remain attributed family history rather than a reconstructed evening.2323Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Grothendieck later interpreted the parents’ changing relation through a spiritual scheme of passivity and dominance. That vocabulary belongs to his adult analysis and cannot diagnose either parent, while Scharlau’s report of correspondence from the first months of separation merely shows the material on which part of the later inquiry rested. Because the letters were reportedly destroyed in 1991, the chapter draws no event detail from their contents.2424Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18.

The lost correspondence would have supplied dates, addresses, and contemporaneous language, but its absence leaves later readers with Grothendieck’s interpretation and Scharlau’s source history. The difference matters because an adult meditation can establish what the rupture came to mean without resolving the departure’s exact circumstances or either parent’s motive.

Before the break

The remembered Berlin family was neither a conventional household nor a political commune free of ordinary obligations. Hanka and Tanaroff worked, wrote, argued, and cared for children within arrangements shaped by poverty and unstable documentation. Grothendieck’s later account nevertheless remembered the first five years as a distinct family period.2525Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4 and 607–608.

Tanaroff’s missing arm made his photographic work conspicuous, but it did not make him dependent in every practical task. Scharlau’s strongest evidence shows an experienced worker adapting an instant camera and continuing a trade across cities. Hanka’s later claim that he needed her help must therefore remain part of a conflicting family narrative.2626Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 17.

Hanka’s registered role in the studio also resists a simple story of dependence. Her name appeared in the directory, while Scharlau credited Tanaroff with much of the camera work and described their joint travel for business. The enterprise depended on cooperation even when the adults disagreed about work, money, and gendered authority.2727Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

Writing occupied a parallel place in their ambitions. Hanka pursued prose shaped by autobiography, and Tanaroff was remembered as planning an account of revolutionary experience that did not survive. Their projects demonstrate aspiration, although incompletion cannot be assigned to one motive or defect.2828Grothendieck, Eine Frau.

Maidi’s position exposed the household’s layered forms of parenthood. Alf remained part of her reported civil history, while Tanaroff appears in later family evidence as someone who sometimes assumed a paternal role. The available materials show relation without allowing a complete account of residence, support, or feeling.2929Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Alexander’s early relation to Tanaroff reaches us chiefly through his adult writing. He remembered admiration and identification rather than fear as the defining terms of those first years. That memory cannot erase conflict, but it prevents conflict from being projected across every day before departure.3030Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4.

The parents’ quarrels are similarly difficult to scale. Scharlau reports temporary absences after disputes, yet no record counts them or identifies a single occasion. They establish a remembered pattern, not a timetable of domestic instability.3131Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The distinction becomes important when Tanaroff finally left Germany. A later narrator might connect the final migration to earlier nights away, but the child’s recognition of finality remains unknown. The biography can describe repeated absence only at the level Scharlau’s source supports.3232Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Political identity entered daily life through associations, donations, and the history Tanaroff carried from Russia. Scharlau found no evidence of new German organizational activity during the final Berlin years, which limits claims about immediate police interest. His anarchism remains biographical context rather than proof of a specific underground act.3333Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

Hanka’s oppositional history was different in form. Earlier work around a stigmatized newspaper and avant-garde theater placed her outside respectable convention, but Scharlau found no party membership that would define her 1933 position. Those facts complicate danger without yielding a motive for departure.3434Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 11 and 17.

The household therefore entered 1933 with several kinds of fragility already present. Its fixed business trace had ended, its income remained irregular, and one adult’s refugee history exposed him to renewed administrative danger. Nazi rule transformed those vulnerabilities into a crisis that private improvisation could not resolve.

No evidence shows the adults predicting the full course of persecution that followed. Tanaroff had reasons to recognize danger, but the available record does not preserve his words about the decision. Historical consequence must not be substituted for contemporaneous foresight.3535Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The same rule applies to the children. Later separation gave the first family period a sharp boundary in Grothendieck’s memory, although neither child left a contemporaneous account of the father’s departure. Their silence in the archive is a limit on scene, not proof of indifference.3636Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

By August 8, the one adopted chronological statement is Scharlau’s report that Tanaroff was in Paris. Competing departure months remain outside the narrative, and the route remains unrecorded. This restraint allows the chapter to close without turning disagreement into false precision.3737Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18.

The break altered the household before it explained itself. Hanka and the children remained in Berlin, reported correspondence connected the adults, and the loss of Tanaroff’s labor created immediate material consequences. Chapter 5 begins with those consequences rather than repeating the evidence for departure.3838Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 18.

The pages of Récoltes et Semailles remain primary evidence for that later meaning. Grothendieck identified the first family as a decisive early relation and returned to its dissolution while reflecting on patterns he believed he had discovered in 1979–1980; those claims belong securely to the history of his self-understanding even when they cannot reconstruct a child’s contemporaneous knowledge.3939Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4 and 607–608.

His retrospective attachment to both parents prevents a simple portrait of Tanaroff as always absent. The adult son remembered the father inside the household before migration and later described prolonged silence, which makes chronology indispensable: early identification, departure, and foster-year correspondence are three different periods rather than evidence for one timeless paternal character.4040Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4 and 608.

Scharlau’s interviews and documents add external structure without resolving the private relation. They place photographic work, a changing business trace, reported quarrels, and migration around the family memory, but the biographer cannot combine those fragments into continuous domestic observation; the gaps are part of the historical form rather than defects to be hidden with scenes.4141Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

This layered reading also preserves Hanka as a separate actor. Grothendieck’s later analysis of the parents’ bond, Scharlau’s judgments about their conduct, and Hanka’s earlier literary manuscript arise from different purposes, so none should silently become Hanka’s contemporaneous explanation of why she remained, quarreled, or later followed Tanaroff.4242Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Grothendieck, Eine Frau.

The chapter consequently treats remembered love and remembered injury as evidence of the adult writer’s relation to the past, not as licenses to rank the parents’ affection. That restraint does not diminish the rupture; it locates the claim in the person who made it and allows the migration record to carry its own, narrower weight.

The political meaning of an insecure paper

By 1933, Tanaroff’s established biography placed him in several categories targeted by the new regime: Scharlau identifies him as Jewish, anarchist, and a refugee from revolutionary Russia, while official museum chronologies document the rapid destruction of civil protections and the expansion of political and anti-Jewish persecution after January. These national developments establish the field of danger without proving an individual warrant, summons, or police action against him.4343Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17; United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates,” Holocaust Encyclopedia, https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/1933-key-dates; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’,” Hitler and the Germans: Nation and Crime, https://www.dhm.de/archiv/ausstellungen/hitler-und-die-deutschen/en/machtuebertragung.html.

Scharlau is the principal biographical source for the conclusion that this danger preceded Tanaroff’s move to France. Chai and Oort repeat the broad early-life chronology while relying substantially on Scharlau, so their survey cannot be counted as independent corroboration; the only exit terminus used here is Scharlau’s report that Tanaroff was in Paris by August 8.4444Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18 note 51; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” secs. 1.3–1.4.

The available record provides no basis for naming a single enforcement act as the cause of departure. Dagmar later remembered Hanka connecting the move to persecution after the Reichstag fire, but the memoir is mediated, chronologically unreliable in places, and unsupported by a Tanaroff-specific police file; the national decree supplies context rather than confirmation of that remembered explanation.4545United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”, February 27–28 entries; Scharlau, Anarchy.

Scharlau also found no tangible evidence of Tanaroff’s participation in a German anarchist organization during the final Berlin years. Earlier political commitments and associations remain part of his biography, yet the chapter does not promote them into a claim of current underground activity, because persecution could attach to an attributed history without a newly documented act.4646Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Hanka’s documented position differed without yielding a practical counterfactual. Scharlau describes German identity documents, non-Jewish ancestry, oppositional cultural associations, and no known Communist or Social Democratic membership; those facts show different kinds and degrees of danger but leave her feasible courses of action and reasons for following Tanaroff unresolved.4747Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17; Koordinierungsstelle Stolpersteine Berlin, “Johanna ‘Hanka’ Grothendieck,” https://www.stolpersteine-berlin.de/en/brunnenstrasse/165/johanna-hanka-grothendieck.

The children’s legal positions are not inferred from Tanaroff’s papers. The mediated civil record describes different paternity and surname histories, while Alexander’s nationality remains unresolved and Maidi’s status cannot be derived from household membership; the immediate supported consequence is material, because the departure removed Tanaroff’s labor and changed the conditions under which the Berlin household could continue.4848Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

The national chronology helps explain why an already fragile documentary position acquired new urgency. Hitler became chancellor on January 30, 1933, the decree issued after the Reichstag fire suspended core civil protections on February 28, and the measures of March and April rapidly expanded dictatorship, political detention, and anti-Jewish exclusion; these dates come from official historical chronologies and describe Germany as a whole, not an enforcement file for Tanaroff.4949United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”.

For a Jewish refugee known in anarchist circles, the distinction between general policy and individual danger was practical rather than academic. The available record does not need to invent a personal arrest order to show that categories attached to his origin, political history, and uncertain papers had become more threatening, although it still cannot state which warning, encounter, or calculation determined the day he left.5050Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17; United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”.

The timing conflict among later chronologies is kept outside the event claim. This chapter selects none of the proposed departure months; Scharlau’s report of presence in Paris by August 8 supplies only the latest point needed for the chapter boundary.5151Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18 note 51; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” secs. 1.3–1.4.

Hanka’s position cannot be measured simply by subtracting Tanaroff’s risks. Her documented ancestry and political history differed, yet she shared his household, economic uncertainty, and oppositional milieu, and the sources do not inventory the practical options available to her after he left; this is why different exposure does not become a claim that remaining was safe or feasible.5252Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17; Koordinierungsstelle Stolpersteine Berlin, “Johanna ‘Hanka’ Grothendieck”.

The children’s situation also changed before any legal conclusion can be drawn. Tanaroff’s departure removed a worker and parent from the Berlin household, leaving Hanka to organize daily life while she considered her own departure; Chapter 5 follows the later care arrangements, but the material pressure begins here as a consequence of the first migration.5353Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Political context, documentary insecurity, and family economy thus meet without collapsing into one cause. The available evidence supports a dangerous environment, an adult who left Germany, and a household altered by his absence; it does not support a precise motive hierarchy among fear, politics, partnership, work, and parental obligation.

A crisis measured at two scales

At the national scale, the change was rapid and documented. The appointment of Hitler, the emergency decree after the Reichstag fire, the dismantling of constitutional limits, political detention, and anti-Jewish measures all occurred within the first months of 1933, transforming the meaning of a political history or Jewish origin even when no new act had been committed.5454United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”.

At the family scale, the evidence is fragmentary. Scharlau reports that Tanaroff left, that Hanka and the children remained for a time, and that correspondence crossed the new distance; the archive does not preserve a meeting at which the adults set out a plan, nor does it identify which danger, warning, or practical opening determined the departure.5555Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15, 17, 18.

The difference between those scales prevents a familiar error in political biography. General persecution can make flight intelligible without proving an individualized pursuit, while the absence of a personal warrant does not make the broader danger unreal; narrative accuracy depends on holding the public chronology and the private source gap together.5656United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”.

Tanaroff’s earlier experience of clandestinity and expulsion may appear to offer a psychological explanation for quick action, but no contemporaneous statement in the available record records his calculation. Scharlau sometimes interprets his history as giving him an unusual sensitivity to danger, and that remains the biographer’s inference rather than a motive this chapter adopts.5757Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The household consequences are more directly visible than the decision process. An adult and income-producing worker was gone, Hanka remained with two children and an intention to leave, and the fixed studio had already disappeared from the directories; these facts explain why the existing arrangement could not simply continue without requiring the narrator to rank love, politics, fear, or ideology.5858Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 17.

The children’s perspectives remain absent from this moment. Grothendieck’s later description gives the rupture long-term importance but does not reproduce what he understood before the household dispersed, while no contemporaneous statement from Maidi survives; the political crisis changed their circumstances before the sources reveal their knowledge.5959Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8.

This chapter therefore ends the father’s Berlin chronology at a reported destination rather than an imagined decision. The next chapter begins with the care arrangements that followed, preserving event ownership and preventing the same private letter or departure conflict from being explained twice.

From January to August 1933

The year opened with a household already lacking the fixed studio that had once anchored its work. Tanaroff could still practice street photography, but income depended on movement and crowds, while Hanka’s literary ambitions and the children’s care continued within an address history that had become difficult to trace.6060Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13 and 15.

On January 30 Hitler became chancellor, and the transfer of power rapidly changed the environment around political opponents, refugees, and Jews. The shift was national before it became visible in any family file, yet Tanaroff’s origin and political history placed him within categories made newly dangerous.6161United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Deutsches Historisches Museum, “Transfer of Power and ‘National Revolution’”.

The emergency decree of February 28 suspended protections and widened detention after the Reichstag fire. Hanka later connected Tanaroff’s departure to that moment in Dagmar’s recollection, although the chapter treats the family explanation as memory rather than an individual police record.6262United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”; Scharlau, Anarchy.

Measures in March and April consolidated dictatorship and expanded political and anti-Jewish exclusion. They did not write the family’s private decisions, but they narrowed the setting in which a Jewish anarchist refugee with insecure papers could continue to work and reside.6363United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”.

Scharlau found no new German anarchist activity that would explain departure through a specific recent offense. Tanaroff’s established commitments and earlier networks nevertheless remained visible parts of his biography, allowing persecution to attach to a history rather than to a fresh act.6464Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The family economy made waiting consequential. Tanaroff’s photographic labor contributed to a household whose stable business address had disappeared, and his movement out of Germany removed both an adult presence and a source of income; Hanka and the children remained together for the moment.

Scharlau’s chronology next places Tanaroff in Paris by August 8. The private letter that supplies that endpoint remains unquoted and uninspected here, but the reported date and location are enough to mark the completion of the father’s Berlin departure.6565Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18 note 51.

Correspondence reportedly connected the adults across the new distance. The letters have not survived for inspection, so they provide source history rather than dialogue, yet their reported existence shows that physical separation did not end communication between Hanka and Tanaroff.6666Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

By the chapter boundary, then, the father was in France and the remaining household faced the consequences of his absence. Hanka’s arrangements for Maidi and Alexander had not yet been narrated; those actions, and Hanka’s own journey, belong to Chapter 5.

Berlin closes around the household

The political calendar after January moved faster than the family archive. The Reichstag fire on February 27 was followed by the emergency decree on February 28, which suspended protections and enabled detention, and the March 23 Enabling Act gave the government broad legislative power outside normal parliamentary control.6767United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”.

Political repression acquired institutions and places as well as laws. Dachau opened for political prisoners in March, Communist and Social Democratic organizations were attacked, and the new government converted opposition into a category of detention; Tanaroff’s anarchist history entered a state environment increasingly hostile to every independent left movement.6868United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”.

Anti-Jewish measures became public in April through the boycott and exclusions from public employment. Tanaroff’s Jewish origin was already part of family knowledge, while the assumed surname in western records offered no reliable shield against a regime organizing persecution around ancestry and political history.6969United States Holocaust Memorial Museum, “1933: Key Dates”.

These developments did not occur in a separate world from photographic work. Street trade depended on public space, licensing, and tolerance from police, and a refugee photographer could encounter the new state through ordinary attempts to earn money; the household’s economy was exposed to the same institutions that governed residence.7070Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 13.

The disappearance of the studio listing made that exposure harder to document rather than less real. A fixed business had once given Hanka and Tanaroff an address and commercial identity, while the later return to mobile work placed more of the household’s livelihood outside durable records.7171Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Tanaroff’s earlier migrations offered practical precedent for leaving. He had traveled through Berlin, Paris, and Belgium, worked with a portable camera, and relied on political or émigré contacts; by 1933 Paris was a known destination rather than an abstract symbol of freedom.7272Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 18.

The same history also made documents part of every move. Scharlau’s account follows assumed papers, short permits, expulsion threats, and an identification card, showing a life repeatedly negotiated through administrative encounters even though the individual objects await authentication.7373Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 13.

Family history supplied another reason the move mattered. Grothendieck later remembered Tanaroff as a central parent in the first household, and Maidi’s later evidence suggests a paternal social relation despite their different legal history; departure removed one adult from both children’s daily world.7474Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Hanka remained the adult organizing the Berlin household after Tanaroff left. Her work, documents, and political associations differed from his, yet she shared the economic consequences of the migration and the task of caring for two children while considering her own journey.7575Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The children’s positions differed in age and prior residence. Maidi’s history included stays with maternal relatives, while Alexander’s remembered early place centered on the four-person Berlin home; this contrast shaped the practical setting without granting access to either child’s immediate response.7676Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Both children still depended on adult arrangements after Tanaroff left. Their differing histories explain why one shared label for the coming placements would obscure rather than clarify the family chronology.

Correspondence became the first bridge across the new geography. Scharlau reports letters between the parents during the separation, and Grothendieck later used family correspondence while reflecting on the rupture; the exchange connected Berlin and Paris before the family’s other routes were settled.7777Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

The care problem emerged from that divided geography. One adult had reached France, another remained with the children, and the household no longer had its old work structure; Chapter 5 begins when Hanka turns those conditions into separate arrangements for Maidi and Alexander.7878Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Seen in sequence, the departure was neither a single dramatic night nor a complete family flight. It was the first movement in a staggered dissolution, beginning with political danger and a father’s migration and continuing through decisions made after he had left Berlin.

The exact month inside that sequence remains outside the adopted chronology. The reported August 8 Paris terminus gives the chapter a firm exit while preserving the source conflict for future archival review.

This chronology also fixes narrative ownership. Chapter 4 carries Tanaroff from the changing Berlin household to the reported Paris endpoint; Chapter 5 receives Hanka and the children after that move and narrates the arrangements that followed.

Care before the household divided

Before any placement, care still belonged to the Berlin household. Hanka remained with Maidi and Alexander after Tanaroff’s move, managing ordinary needs within a family economy already altered by the loss of his work and presence.7979Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

The children occupied different positions within that household. Maidi’s earlier residence with maternal relatives contrasted with Alexander’s remembered place in the Berlin home; their histories shaped practical responsibilities without revealing private responses.

Tanaroff had participated in the household as photographer, partner, and father. His departure changed income and daily presence together, making care an economic question as well as a relation among adults and children.

Hanka’s own work history offered no simple replacement for the studio economy. Her writing remained incomplete, the photographic business had lost its fixed listing, and Scharlau’s account supplies no stable new employment for the first months of separation.

Reported correspondence linked Berlin and Paris while Hanka remained with the children. The exchange shows that the adults’ relation continued across distance, although its unavailable contents leave the division of planning and responsibility unresolved.8080Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

Formal care language enters only through mediated records. Scharlau reports earlier youth-welfare involvement, but the missing file supplies no basis for describing who held authority during these months; daily care and legal guardianship remain separate questions.8181Scharlau, Anarchy.

The political crisis intensified this practical pressure. A return by Tanaroff offered no stable assumption under Nazi rule, while Hanka’s own departure would require another arrangement for each child.

Nothing in this sequence makes the later placements inevitable in their exact form. It establishes the problem Hanka faced after the first migration: two children still in Germany, one adult already in France, and a household whose economic structure had weakened.

That is the care chronology Chapter 4 can own. It ends before a receiving household or institution is selected, leaving Chapter 5 to narrate the arrangements rather than previewing them.

The end of this chapter also keeps the siblings as separate subjects. Maidi’s next residence requires evidence different from Alexander’s, and neither route should be inferred from the other child’s later account. Chapter 5 begins with that divergence.

The father’s departure therefore changes care without completing its reorganization. At the August boundary, Hanka still carries daily responsibility in Berlin, Tanaroff is reported in Paris, and the children’s later destinations remain ahead. This is a complete transition rather than an incomplete preview.

A departure without a recorded scene

The departure itself has no recoverable scene. Scharlau marks the reported nighttime exit and the child’s alleged immediate hurt as hearsay, while no station, route, companion, farewell, or contemporary family letter fixes the sequence; Grothendieck’s later writing establishes only the lasting importance he assigned to the rupture.8282Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

What survives is a change of location. Scharlau reports Tanaroff in Paris by August 8, Hanka and the children still in Germany, and parental correspondence during the first months apart; Paris was also a familiar émigré center where Tanaroff had previously lived and worked, although no 1933 lodging or employment record is available.8383Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7, 15, 18.

The private August letter is used only through Scharlau’s report of its date and place. Its wording, signature, language, postmark, holder, and rights were not checked, so the chapter neither quotes nor interprets it as evidence of affection, advice, or paternal conduct.8484Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15 note 51.

Grothendieck’s later account of paternal silence begins with the subsequent foster years and cannot be projected backward into the months before December 1933. It shows how the adult son remembered that later interval, whereas the departure chapter can conclude only that Tanaroff had become geographically absent from the Berlin household while remaining present in family memory and reported correspondence.8585Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4 and 608.

Chapter 5 follows what came next: Hanka still had to arrange care for both children before leaving Germany.8686Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.

Paris as a reported endpoint

Paris was not an arbitrary destination in Tanaroff’s biography. Scharlau had already followed him there during the 1920s, when Russian émigré and anarchist networks offered contacts and street photography offered a portable way to earn money; the earlier history makes a later return intelligible without proving who received him, where he stayed, or what work began immediately in 1933.8787Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 18.

The city’s meaning was therefore both practical and limited. It lay outside the regime that had transformed Tanaroff’s position in Berlin, contained people and occupations he knew, and provided the address from which Scharlau reports the August letter; none of those facts establishes secure asylum, permanent residence, or a replacement household.8888Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 7 and 18.

That distinction prevents the destination from becoming a retrospective refuge myth. Later internment and deportation show that France did not supply enduring safety, but those later events should not be used to make every 1933 decision tragic in advance; at this point the evidence supports movement out of immediate German danger and into another uncertain documentary setting.8989Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 18.

The Paris endpoint also reveals how an archive often records migration backward. The departure itself leaves no opened ticket or border file, while the destination appears through a biographer’s description of a private letter; the result is a firm-enough change of city for narrative sequence and an unresolved route that must remain invisible.9090Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18.

For the family, that asymmetry mattered because the father became knowable at a distance before the circumstances of leaving became knowable. Hanka and the children remained in Berlin at the chapter boundary, parental correspondence reportedly crossed the new distance, and the next practical decisions arose from a separation whose first journey cannot be reconstructed.9191Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 607–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 17 and 18.

The title thus names a change in household relation rather than an essence assigned to Tanaroff. He had been present as worker, partner, photographed family member, and acknowledged father; after migration he was physically elsewhere, yet correspondence, memory, and political history kept him inside the family narrative.9292Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4; Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

Absence and memory

The departure survives as a change of city rather than a scene: Scharlau reports Tanaroff in Paris, while station, route, companion, farewell, and sequence at home remain outside the record. Grothendieck’s later memory joins intense early attachment to paternal silence during the foster years, establishing the rupture’s enduring importance without revealing Tanaroff’s motive.9393Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4 and 607–608; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18.

Fatherhood appears through different source forms. Scharlau reports civil acknowledgment, photographs place Tanaroff within the household, and a private letter is described as paternal toward Maidi; these materials establish relation at particular moments while leaving payment, daily care, and emotional consistency unresolved.9494Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 15.

The archive preserves those relations unevenly. Grothendieck’s French meditation and the 1929 address-book page are directly inspectable, whereas civil files, private correspondence, and much family testimony reach the chapter through Scharlau; Chai and Oort repeat an early-life chronology substantially dependent on his work.9595Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–8; Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 13–18; Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” secs. 1.3–1.4; Berliner Adreßbuch für das Jahr 1929.

That source hierarchy supports consequence rather than verdict. Grothendieck’s adult testimony can establish that the rupture remained painful, yet it supplies neither Maidi’s experience nor either parent’s private calculation, and nothing in the record turns childhood papers or departure into an origin of later mathematical style.

Once Tanaroff is reported in Paris, Hanka’s arrangements belong to Chapter 5. Respecting that boundary prevents the departure from absorbing every later consequence.

What 1933 left open

The exit state contains one dated terminus and several open questions. Scharlau places Tanaroff in Paris by August 8, while the departure day, route, and individual trigger remain unknown. Chapter 5 need not reopen that conflict.

When this chapter ends, Hanka and the children were still in Germany. Their later movements require different evidence and belong to the next chapter. This separation keeps chronology from becoming repetition.

The family relation also remains unresolved rather than erased. Grothendieck remembered early attachment, later silence, and enduring significance, while Scharlau reported continued correspondence among adults. Those sources establish persistence and break together.

No legal status passes across the boundary. The paternity, guardianship, and nationality chains remain mediated or unknown, so the next chapter receives people and places rather than adjudicated classifications.

The political context does pass forward. Nazi repression had transformed the danger around Tanaroff and oppositional networks, although no individual enforcement file explains the exact departure. Later chapters may inherit the danger without inventing the missing order.

This limited ending completes the chapter’s task. The father is absent from Berlin, present in a reported Paris source, and still central to the adult son’s memory. The care arrangements begin only after that point.

The chapter also leaves a recoverable research path. A private-letter inspection could test the terminus and paternal wording, while civil files could test Scharlau’s paternity narrative. Neither task requires preserving unsupported detail in the meantime.

Until those objects are obtained, the strongest account remains layered. Direct sources establish Berlin context and Grothendieck’s later interpretation, while Scharlau supplies attributed documentary findings.

Nothing else is handed forward as settled fact. Motive, route, immediate childhood response, and legal status remain outside the exit state.

A line drawn to Paris

At the chapter’s exit, Scharlau’s report places Tanaroff in Paris by August 8 while Hanka, Maidi, and Alexander remained in Germany; no route or exact departure date is asserted, and the private letter contributes no quoted language.9696Scharlau, Anarchy, chs. 15 and 18 note 51.

The migration made Tanaroff geographically absent without erasing earlier work, shared residence, reported correspondence, or the adult son’s attachment. That distinction prevents both total condemnation and sentimental repair, because the sources establish a broken household relation more readily than they establish motive.9797Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 603–4 and 607–608.

Hanka’s remaining arrangements begin the next chapter and receive no preview here.9898Scharlau, Anarchy, ch. 17.
