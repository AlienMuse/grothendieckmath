Open chapters

EGA I’s 1960 general plan listed thirteen open chapters; Chapter 25 preserves the full sequence, while this chapter tracks publication and migration.11Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

At the end of 1961, three large instalments existed: EGA I, The Language of Schemes, published in 1960; EGA II, Global Elementary Study of Some Classes of Morphisms, published in 1961; and the first part of EGA III, Cohomological Study of Coherent Sheaves, also published in 1961. Together they had already changed the available grammar.22Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

What “elements” meant

The title Éléments de géométrie algébrique echoed Euclid and Bourbaki. It did not promise elementary exercises. “Elements” meant foundational components from which a field could be constructed: definitions, operations, exact dependencies, and theorems reusable across problems.33Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

This explains the density. EGA is not a chronological record of discoveries. It suppresses false starts and arranges results by logical need. A definition may appear hundreds of pages before its most dramatic use. A proposition proves stability under base change because later chapters will use the fact without stopping.44Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

The human problem was to produce a reliable reference while the research program was still growing. If the authors waited for completion, nothing might appear. If they published disconnected articles, the language might fragment. Serial “elements” offered a third path.

An operating-system analogy is useful with limits. EGA supplied common operations on which later mathematical applications could run: fiber products, relative spectra, coherent sheaves, proper morphisms, cohomology. The analogy fails if it implies central design controlled every later theorem or that users merely executed predefined commands. Algebraic geometers modified the language, found counterexamples, and built new categories.

The title also declared scale. A paper can solve a problem; elements aim to make many papers possible. The ambition attracted readers and collaborators, but it imposed a steep entry cost and placed unusual authority in the authors’ notation.

The first volume: language

EGA I runs from pages 5 to 228 of Publications Mathématiques de l’IHÉS number 4. Its legal deposit records the third quarter of 1960. On the title page, Grothendieck appears as author and Dieudonné under a separate line crediting collaboration in redaction.55Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

The chapter builds affine schemes from spectra, defines preschemes and schemes in the historical terminology, develops products and separation, treats finiteness conditions and rational maps, and gives an extended account of sheaves of modules, quasi-coherence, coherence, and formal schemes.66Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

The order begins with spaces but moves quickly to sheaves. A scheme’s structure sheaf makes local algebra geometric; modules over that sheaf make linear and ideal-theoretic data geometric. An ideal sheaf defines a closed subscheme. An invertible sheaf encodes a line bundle. A coherent sheaf can describe a subvariety, divisor, vector bundle, or more singular object.

On an affine scheme X=Spec⁡AX=\operatorname{Spec}A, an AA-module MM gives a quasi-coherent sheaf M̃\widetilde M. Localization agrees with restriction:

M̃(D(f))≅Mf. \widetilde M(D(f))\cong M_f.

This dictionary lets module calculations glue across affine opens. Coherence adds finite generation under Noetherian hypotheses and supplies the finiteness needed for cohomology.

Serre’s FAC is the direct predecessor EGA names. FAC developed coherent sheaves and cohomology on algebraic varieties. EGA I extends the local module–sheaf architecture to schemes over general rings. The historical sentence is not “Grothendieck invented coherent sheaves.” It is that he and Dieudonné made them structural in the scheme foundation, building on Serre.77Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

The second volume: maps

EGA II appeared as number 8 in 1961 and occupied pages 5–222. Its title identifies a global elementary study of classes of morphisms. Chapter 27 gives the definitions and examples; the publication consequence here is that relative morphism language had become a numbered reference layer before the coherent-cohomology chapter appeared.88Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes”.

The volume’s position made later construction and cohomology arguments shorter to state. Authors could cite established classes and stability results instead of rebuilding them inside every exposé. The title’s word elementary marks the volume’s place before the cohomological methods of Chapter III, not an assessment of difficulty.

The third volume opens the cohomological chapter

EGA III.1 appeared as number 11 in 1961, pages 5–167. It continues the general preliminaries with a section on representable functors and then begins the chapter on coherent cohomology. Chapter 28 treats hXh_X, Yoneda, and full faithfulness; this chapter records only the placement and publication date.99Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique III: Étude cohomologique des faisceaux cohérents, première partie”.

The first part did not complete Chapter III; the second part appeared in 1963. That open edge is the dependency consequence: by the end of 1961 a substantial coherent-cohomology layer was public, while its continuation and many later formulations remained future work. The exact modern theorem statements are not reassigned to this fascicle without proposition-level locators.

Dieudonné’s pages

The title page continued to identify Dieudonné’s role as collaboration in redaction. The correspondence gives snapshots of the work: Grothendieck sent notes and commentaries; Dieudonné drafted chapters; Serre read and corrected; chapters went to press; Grothendieck developed material farther ahead.1010Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck and Serre, Correspondance Grothendieck–Serre.

It is common to say Grothendieck supplied vision and Dieudonné details. The contrast is useful only if “details” receives its mathematical weight. Turning a new theory into thousands of linked statements requires selecting lemmas, ordering dependence, checking edge cases, and enforcing notation. A gap in a detail can collapse a later theorem.

At the same time, the asymmetry should not be blurred. EGA identifies Grothendieck as the author and Dieudonné as collaborator in redaction. The research program, principal new concepts, and direction belonged to Grothendieck. The precise sentence-level division cannot be reconstructed from the printed title alone.

Serre’s contribution remains external and direct: foundational earlier papers, correspondence, draft criticism, examples, and corrections. He declined or never completed proposed historical notes and joint pieces for EGA. The book must not use “Grothendieck–Serre school” to dissolve authorship.

The treatise was thus neither solitary nor anonymously collective. It was a directed collaboration embedded in a critical network.

The future named too early

At that point, the desired étale cohomology had not been constructed.1313Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck, Revêtements étales et groupe fondamental (SGA 1); Grothendieck, “Technique de descente et théorèmes d’existence en géométrie algébrique. It is tempting to read the chapter title as proof that the future was already known. It was a research target. The program identified properties a cohomology should have and arranged foundations thought necessary to reach it. The route would require étale maps, sites, topoi, derived methods, and collective work.

Similarly, naming Picard schemes did not prove representability in all cases. The 1961 exposés set up construction; Picard existence lectures followed in February and May 1962 under hypotheses. Naming motives later would not construct their full category.1414Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck, Revêtements étales et groupe fondamental (SGA 1); Grothendieck, “Technique de descente et théorèmes d’existence en géométrie algébrique. Grothendieck’s named research destinations coordinated work and exposed missing bridges. Historical writing should preserve the subjunctive: would, should, hoped, projected.

The difference between program and theorem does not diminish vision. It makes vision legible as a force acting before proof.

The architecture of cross-reference

EGA’s numbered propositions create a map through the corpus. A later proof can cite (I, 5.5.8) or (II, 4.5.2) rather than restating a theorem. Chapter, section, and proposition numbers make dependencies inspectable.1515Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique III: Étude cohomologique des faisceaux cohérents, première partie”.

This system also reveals the modular ideal. Definitions and results should be reusable outside their first motivation. A lemma about quasi-coherent algebras supports relative Spec; relative Spec supports affine morphisms; affine morphisms enter descent and construction.

The cost is that reading becomes non-linear. A page may refer backward through several fascicles and forward to promised material. Changes to early chapters propagate. The authors’ declaration that chapters remain open acknowledges this versioning problem.

Serial publication gave each fascicle a stable page range but not an eternally final text. EGA I was later revised in a new edition. Modern readers often use electronic retypings or summaries whose numbering follows one version. Exact quotations and claims must cite the 1960 fascicle used here.

The publication architecture belongs to the intellectual history. It made a growing theory navigable enough to become communal infrastructure.

An unfinished monument

EGA’s scale and endurance can make the text look like a finished stone object. Its publication policy instead treated chapters as revisable and open to supplementary fascicles.1616Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique III: Étude cohomologique des faisceaux cohérents, première partie”.

The published corpus is incomplete relative to the plan, yet individual fascicles are complete mathematical works. The distinction prevents two errors. One is to say EGA was abandoned and therefore failed. The other is to speak as if all thirteen planned chapters exist somewhere in the eight fascicles.

Incompleteness also had creative effects. Seminars took over open regions. Students and collaborators became authors. Later books reorganized the foundations pedagogically. The absence of a completed canonical treatment left room for alternative expositions.

There were costs too. Important results remained dispersed or difficult to access. SGA volumes appeared years after seminars. Readers needed French, extensive prerequisites, and patience with numbering. A universal language did not mean universal accessibility.

The biography should allow both judgments. EGA was a transformative achievement and an unfinished project. These are not opposing verdicts.

Chapter I as a dependency tree

EGA I begins from prime spectra and builds outward. Basic opens and localizations establish the affine topology. The structure sheaf turns rings into locally ringed spaces. Morphisms reverse ring homomorphisms. Affine pieces then glue into preschemes.1717Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

Products appear early because every relative construction needs them. A fiber of X→SX\to S is a fiber product. The diagonal defining separatedness maps into a fiber product. Base change is a fiber product. Without existence and associativity up to canonical isomorphism, later notation would have no foundation.

Subschemes and immersions follow. Quotient rings define closed affine subschemes; quasi-coherent ideal sheaves globalize them. Open subschemes arise from restriction. Locally closed immersions combine the two.

Finiteness conditions then classify manageable objects. Quasi-compactness, Noetherian conditions, finite type, and finite presentation measure different kinds of size. The distinctions become essential once base rings are arbitrary.

Rational maps preserve contact with birational geometry. A rational map is not a morphism defined at generic points by magic; it is an equivalence class of morphisms on dense opens, with hypotheses ensuring useful domains. Integral schemes and function fields recover classical language inside schemes.

The long treatment of modules provides the linear layer. Quasi-coherent sheaves localize like modules. Coherent sheaves add finite presentation in the Noetherian setting. Tensor products, Hom, pullback, and pushforward prepare cohomology.

Formal schemes close the volume by passing from powers of ideals to completed local structures. This ending points directly toward the 1959 formal-geometry lecture and the existence theorems.

The sequence shows a dependency tree rather than a list. Remove products and relative geometry fails. Remove quasi-coherent sheaves and relative Spec fails. Remove coherence and finiteness theorems lose their objects. Remove formal completion and one route to representability closes.

This architecture is part of Grothendieck’s conceptual contribution and Dieudonné’s redactional achievement. A definition’s place determines which later proof can cite it.

Historical terminology inside the tree

EGA’s préschéma and schéma distinction places separation inside the noun. Modern convention calls both schemes and adds “separated” to the latter. A reader using a modern translation must track this shift.1818Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”.

Other terms moved too. A morphism called simple in early correspondence became smooth. “Complete” in older algebraic geometry is often rendered proper in the relative language. Universal domains and geometric points received more exact treatment.

Terminology is not cosmetic. If a quoted theorem says “scheme” in EGA’s restricted sense, translating it as modern “scheme” may drop separatedness. If a letter says “unramified” where the author later distinguishes étale and unramified, a silent update can distort chronology.

Main prose uses current vocabulary for comprehension and flags the historical difference at first use.

Notation also evolved between editions. EGA I’s first edition was later revised. Cross-references in EGA II and III can target the original Chapter I numbering. A digital typesetting based on a revision may not match the 1960 page.

This account treats the Numdam fascicles as primary objects. Modern references verify interpretation but do not replace exact locators.

This textual discipline is one reason the corpus cannot be summarized solely from later textbooks. Textbooks present the successful mature language; EGA’s first editions preserve the language becoming stable.

Relative Proj in the volume sequence

EGA II’s sections on homogeneous spectra, projective bundles, and ample sheaves show how the relative language absorbed classical projective geometry. Chapter 27 has already explained the relative map and base change; Chapter 28 has shown why projectivity matters for Hilbert representability. Here the documentary point is the order in which EGA made those later uses citable.1919Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes,” secs. 2–5.

The volume begins with affine morphisms and then moves through relative projective constructions before reaching proper and projective morphisms. That sequence turns coordinate methods into reusable statements over a base. It also keeps distinctions visible: projective implies proper under the stated hypotheses, while proper need not mean projective.

For the future geometry, the important consequence was a shared reference layer. A later exposé could invoke a projective bundle, an ample invertible sheaf, or a projective morphism without rebuilding the definitions. EGA II therefore supplied infrastructure for the Hilbert construction rather than a second independent route to it.

Blow-ups as evidence of an operative language

EGA II closes with blow-ups, cones, and projective closure. Their presence matters because the scheme language was being tested by transformations, not only by classification. The chapter does not repeat the Rees-algebra construction already available in standard algebraic geometry; it records what the construction demonstrated in the EGA sequence.2020Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes,” sec. 8.

A blow-up modifies a scheme along a specified center and produces a projective morphism back to the original scheme. This allowed local and birational questions to be stated over general bases while retaining the center’s scheme structure. The construction therefore linked the inherited geometry of modification with the new relative vocabulary.

Its historical role is bounded. Blow-ups did not solve resolution of singularities in every setting, and their universal property did not make every modification canonical. They showed that the new foundations could carry a classical operation into a broader category without reducing it to a picture.

Serre’s projective results as load-bearing precedents

Serre’s projective cohomology and vanishing results predated EGA and supplied essential control over coherent sheaves after sufficiently positive twists. The exact theorem and its hypotheses belong to Serre’s work; EGA used and extended that infrastructure in a relative setting.2121Serre, “Faisceaux algébriques cohérents”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique III: Étude cohomologique des faisceaux cohérents, première partie”.

The consequence for the construction program was finite presentation. High twists can make coherent sheaves globally generated and suppress higher cohomology under the standard hypotheses, allowing families to be encoded by finite linear data. Chapter 28 has explained how this enters the Grassmannian route to the Hilbert scheme.

This placement keeps attribution and chronology separate. Serre’s theorem is not retroactively an EGA invention, and EGA’s relative organization is not reduced to a citation of Serre. The later construction depends on both the prior theorem and the new framework.

EGA as a machine for hypotheses

The repeated adjectives in EGA—Noetherian, quasi-compact, separated, finite type, proper, flat, coherent—can look like barriers between reader and idea. Their function is diagnostic.2222Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique III: Étude cohomologique des faisceaux cohérents, première partie”.

Each adjective supports an operation. Noetherian conditions give finiteness. Separation controls diagonals and intersections of affines. Properness controls limits and cohomology. Flatness controls variation. Coherence keeps modules finite.

A proof’s hypothesis list records which mechanisms it uses. Removing an adjective becomes a research question. Counterexamples identify whether the mechanism was essential or merely convenient.

This style made later generalization systematic. One could extend a theorem from Noetherian to quasi-compact and quasi-separated schemes by replacing the finiteness argument, rather than redefining the whole subject.

It also encouraged overstatement in summary. Saying “Grothendieck proved cohomology behaves in families” hides the hypotheses that give the phrase meaning. This chapter keeps the adjectives even at a cost to pace.

A plan revised by its own success

No single cause for the treatise stopping during Chapter IV should be asserted without broader archival evidence. The observable facts include expansion of material, growth of seminars, competing research directions, and the immense labor of writing.2424Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck and Serre, Correspondance Grothendieck–Serre; Grothendieck, Revêtements étales et groupe fondamental (SGA 1).

Success itself enlarged the task. Schemes generated new questions; representability required quotients; cohomology required sites; local study of morphisms became hundreds of pages. A concise foundation was no longer plausible.

The seminar form offered faster collective development. It also dispersed the corpus and made later publication dependent on editors. Some results no longer fit the linear EGA order.

Grothendieck’s published work continued to move into new problems and seminar forms while the planned EGA sequence remained incomplete. The record establishes that trajectory; it does not support a general claim about his temperament or ability to finish a program.

Dieudonné’s finite capacity also mattered in the plain sense that one collaborator could write only so much. The chapter does not speculate about conflict or fatigue without dated sources.

The unfinished plan is therefore evidence of scale and changing method, not a psychological diagnosis.

What publication fixed by the end of 1961

The three fascicles fixed different layers of the new geometry. EGA I established a public language and a numbered system of reference. EGA II organized major classes of morphisms and projective constructions. EGA III.1 opened the coherent-cohomology chapter and carried representable-functor preliminaries into the treatise. Theorem, method, and “yoga”

Grothendieck and later algebraic geometers often used the informal word yoga for a coherent body of methods, analogies, and expected compatibilities. A yoga is not one theorem. It guides theorems and suggests proof patterns.2727Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique III: Étude cohomologique des faisceaux cohérents, première partie”.

The scheme yoga includes localization, gluing, base change, and functoriality. The proper-cohomology yoga includes finiteness, formal functions, and semicontinuity. The representability yoga joins deformation, descent, and algebraization.

The word can help describe mathematical practice between formal statements. It can also become a substitute for explanation. This book uses it sparingly and always names the operations involved.

A historical yoga can be stronger than its proved range. Mathematicians may expect a statement before hypotheses are settled. Letters capture this prospective level. EGA records the proved level.

Confusing the two makes Grothendieck either infallible or vague. Keeping them distinct reveals how a broad expectation generated precise work.

Toward a cohomology for Weil

Weil’s conjectures required a cohomology theory for varieties over finite fields with features analogous to singular cohomology: finite-dimensional groups, functoriality, cycle classes, a Lefschetz trace formula, duality, and an action of Frobenius.3131Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck, “The Cohomology Theory of Abstract Algebraic Varieties”; Grothendieck, Revêtements étales et groupe fondamental (SGA 1).

Zariski cohomology of coherent sheaves did not supply that Weil cohomology. EGA III was essential infrastructure but not the final theory.

Étale cohomology would use torsion sheaves on the étale site and later ℓ\ell-adic limits. Building it required homological algebra, sites, enough points or substitute arguments, derived functors, and finiteness theorems.

The original EGA plan’s Chapter XIII names the destination. The next seminar years construct roads. Deligne’s proof of the final Weil conjecture in 1974 lies far beyond this Part and has its own authorship.

It is historically important to say both sentences: Grothendieck designed a program centrally aimed at Weil’s conjectures; he did not prove all of them. Program and proof are different achievements.

Reading the open sentence

The sentence used for the proposed epigraph can sound like a declaration of endless creation. The relative world in one view

The Part’s mathematics can be arranged around the map X→SX\to S. At the left, rings and spectra construct XX. Above, base change sends it to XTX_T. Below, quasi-coherent sheaves attach algebraic data. At the right, cohomology pushes data toward SS. A functor F(T)F(T) asks whether a parameter scheme represents families. Descent tests whether local objects return from a cover.

No arrow in that arrangement belongs to one person without qualification. Spectra and local rings have predecessors. Sheaf cohomology carries Serre, Cartan, and others. Yoneda supplies the categorical lemma. Weil and Cartier precede general descent.

The arrangement also has a temporal edge. Beyond it lie sites, topoi, and étale cohomology. They belong after 1961.

Such an arrangement cannot show the institute, journal, correspondence, and seminars that produced the pages. A chronology can. Mathematical and material architectures should meet without one being presented as proof of the other.

The result summarizes this Part: a network with a directing intelligence, inherited nodes, named collaborators, and open outgoing arrows.

What the elements made possible

After EGA I, a mathematician could begin with a scheme over an arbitrary base without redefining its local functions. After EGA II, the map could be classified as affine, projective, finite, or proper with known stability properties. EGA III began supplying coherent cohomology and direct images.3636Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique I: Le langage des schémas”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique II: Étude globale élémentaire de quelques classes de morphismes”; Grothendieck and Dieudonné, “Éléments de géométrie algébrique III: Étude cohomologique des faisceaux cohérents, première partie”.

FGA added construction methods. Formal geometry approached an object through every infinitesimal order. Descent asked whether local objects came from the base. Hilbert schemes turned embedded families into a representing scheme.

These tools compose. A projective family gives coherent sheaves. Their cohomology supplies finite modules. Formal completion probes a parameter near a point. Descent glues local constructions. Representability identifies the result through a universal family.

Composition is the measure of a foundation. An isolated definition can be elegant; a system lets one theorem become input to another.

The system also multiplies failure modes. A family may not be flat. A quotient may not be a scheme. Formal data may not algebraize. Cohomology may not commute with base change. The elements make those failures statements rather than surprises.

This precision opened research to correction. Artin could broaden representability. Mumford could address quotients. Giraud could systematize nonabelian descent. Later derived geometry could retain homological intersections lost by ordinary schemes.

Their work is not an appendix to an already complete vision. It shows how a powerful foundation generates questions its original authors do not finish.

The future geometry of the title is therefore plural. Grothendieck supplied an organizing architecture of exceptional scope. Dieudonné made much of it a durable text. Serre and predecessors supplied indispensable beams. Subsequent mathematicians redesigned rooms and added wings.

The architectural metaphor ends where proof begins. Every addition has its own definitions, hypotheses, and authors. The book’s final responsibility is to keep those names attached.

By December 1961 the elements were sufficient for a new collective phase. They were not sufficient for the final conjectures. That distance is the narrative space of Part VI.

A future organized, not owned

Grothendieck’s program gave future algebraic geometry a vocabulary of tasks. Build a cohomology. Represent a functor. Descend an object. Study a morphism relative to its base. The program did not own every answer. Michael Artin would transform representability and algebraic spaces. David Mumford would develop geometric invariant theory. Historical balance is not achieved by appending these names to Grothendieck’s vision as executors. Their results have independent creativity. The program’s power can be measured by the number of strong mathematicians who could enter it and alter it.

Dieudonné’s independent mathematical identity also persists beyond his collaboration. Serre’s work was never contained by EGA. Motchane’s institute hosted physics and other mathematics. The future geometry grew in a community broader than one program even when that program dominated.

The philosophical theme is therefore openness with boundaries. EGA opened chapters because research was alive, not because every later idea was already latent and attributable to its author.

Foundations become a research program

Part V began with a demand for foundations and ends with foundations becoming a research machine. The scheme retained prime, generic, arithmetic, and infinitesimal information. No one element explains the others. Schemes without descent would be a broad category with fewer construction tools. Representability without formal existence would remain a question. The joining is the achievement. It is also where myth tends to concentrate. One name comes to stand for a language built from many sources; one estate for work begun elsewhere; one completed-sounding acronym for open chapters. This Part has kept the seams visible because the seams carried the work.

The last printed destination in EGA I’s plan was Weil cohomology. Reaching it would require changing what “open set,” “cover,” and even “space” could mean. The relative world had made the base variable. The next architecture would make topology variable too.
