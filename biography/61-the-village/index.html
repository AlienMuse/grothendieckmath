The last letters and the first folder

Two letters written at Les Aumettes close the documented life there. On April 2, 1991, Grothendieck sent Robert Thomason a long mathematical letter on derivators, the subject of Chapter 59, which the editors of Les Dérivateurs later called “an excellent introduction to the theory of derivators”; on June 24 a letter to A. Y. still located him at Les Aumettes.11Grothendieck, Lettre d’Alexandre Grothendieck à Robert Thomason; Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”; Grothendieck, Lettre à A. Y. Technical work, in other words, was continuing in the months before the departure.

The first dated object of the village years is a folder rather than a letter. In the BnF catalog of the late manuscripts, the last unit gathers notes and what the catalog, in quotation marks, calls « gribouillis », connected with Le Problème du Mal and Esprit et Matière; its dates run from August 1991 to February 1997.22Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck (n.d.), https://archivesetmanuscrits.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cc126317b. The month places the earliest of those leaves within weeks of the departure that closes Part X. The catalog does not say where the first of them was written, and the day of arrival in Lasserre is not known. Institutional records fix the year, 1991, and not the day.33Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF,” September 29, 2023, https://www.bnf.fr/fr/les-manuscrits-du-mathematicien-alexandre-grothendieck-entrent-la-bnf; Aline Hartemann et al., “Les mille et une pages de Grothendieck,” Chroniques de la BnF, 2025, https://www.bnf.fr/fr/les-mille-et-une-pages-de-grothendieck; CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques; Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”.

The Montpellier finding aid and the editors of Les Dérivateurs associate a large derivator manuscript with the same period, though its dates and scale rest on their description rather than on a dated title page.44Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”; Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”. What can be said securely is narrower and sufficient: Grothendieck did not stop working in order to leave.

What the derivator pages carry

The derivator manuscript is the largest object that joins the last months at Les Aumettes to the years that followed, and the Montpellier finding aid describes it closely enough to show how its date was arrived at. The manuscript is catalogued as five units of handwritten notes, grouped by chapter from I to XIX, followed by a sixth unit of notes “around” the derivators. Two of the five units carry the year 1990 beside undated leaves, and the finding aid observes that the date of November 14, 1990, appears in the notes of one of them; the other three are undated.55Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”.

The date of the whole is therefore a report. According to Jean Malgoire, the finding aid says, the Dérivateurs were written between October 1990 and the first half of 1991. It adds that the Grothendieck Circle gives 1987 instead, and the Circle’s timeline does list the work under that year.66Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”; Schneps, “A Brief Timeline of Grothendieck’s Life”. The editors’ page gives Malgoire’s span, from October 1990 to the middle of 1991.77Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”. The finding aid records the disagreement without resolving it.

The paper offers a second kind of evidence. Much of the manuscript was written on the backs of sheets that had served other purposes: printouts from 1982, extracts of Récoltes et Semailles, letters. The unit containing chapters IX to XII is written partly on the back of a copy of an article from 1988. The notes around the derivators are written on letterhead paper dated December 1990, on letters from 1990 and 1991, and on an announcement, dated 1991, of the Séminaire Méditerranéen d’Algèbre et Topologie.88Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”. A verso does not date the writing on the other side of the sheet, but it sets a lower bound: a page cannot have been written before its paper reached Grothendieck. Those leaves, at least, cannot belong to 1987, and the paper agrees with Malgoire’s span rather than with the earlier date.

What the sources consulted do not give is the manuscript’s itinerary. Jackson reports that Grothendieck gave Les Dérivateurs to Malgoire in 1995, and the manuscript now belongs to the Montpellier fonds.99Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1206; Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”. Where it was kept between the summer of 1991 and that gift is not stated.

How the destination entered the record

The name of the village entered the public record slowly, and the order in which it entered matters. Allyn Jackson, writing in 2004 while Grothendieck was alive, said that after entrusting his mathematical papers to Jean Malgoire he disappeared into the Pyrenees; she named no commune.1010Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1211. The Grothendieck Circle’s brief timeline said that in August 1991 he left his home for an unknown location, and that he spent his time writing “an enormous work on physics and philosophical meditations” on free choice, determinism, and the existence of evil.1111Schneps, “A Brief Timeline of Grothendieck’s Life”. Ching-Li Chai and Frans Oort, summarizing the move in 2017, wrote that in 1991 he left, that it was unclear where he was living, and that he moved to Lasserre “at some time.”1212Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck,” sec. 1. Writing as correspondents, they added that for many periods between 1970 and 2014 it had been unclear to them how to reach him, “personally and also at his changing locations.”1313Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck”.

Jackson’s profile closed with the words of Yves Ladegaillerie, the student whose thesis Chapter 50 followed: “In his solitary retirement in the Pyrenées, Alexandre Grothendieck has the right to rest after all he has been through.” Ladegaillerie added that, “in thinking of what we owe him, we must leave him in peace.”1414Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1212. The account that named no commune ended by asking its readers to let him be.

Accounts written after his death are more definite. CNRS Le Journal, announcing it in November 2014, said that he had lived secretly withdrawn from the world since the beginning of the 1990s in a village of Ariège.1515CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques. The University of Montpellier’s notice on its Grothendieck archive says that three years after his retirement he settled in Ariège. The BnF, writing in 2025, said that he withdrew in 1991 to Lasserre.1616Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”; Hartemann et al., “Les mille et une pages de Grothendieck”.

These statements do not contradict one another, and the most precise of them is not simply the best. Each records what was known, or what its author chose to say, at its date. The vaguer formulations of the earlier years are themselves evidence: they show that the destination did not belong to ordinary circulation while Grothendieck was alive. The Circle’s timeline named the subjects of the late work, physics and evil, which the BnF catalog would later give as titles, but not the place.1717Schneps, “A Brief Timeline of Grothendieck’s Life”; Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck. The later precision follows his death, the settlement of the papers, and the entry of the late manuscripts into the BnF in 2023. A biography written afterward can name the commune. It should not project that knowledge back onto the 1990s, as though the mathematical world had known where he was.1818Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF”.

What “seclusion” establishes

Contemporary institutional accounts say that Grothendieck withdrew to an Ariège village at the beginning of the 1990s. Allyn Jackson’s 2004 profile, written while he was alive, described over a decade of seclusion in a remote southern hamlet. The word is warranted if it means withdrawal from public mathematical life, an address kept out of ordinary circulation, and strong limits on visitors and correspondence. It becomes unreliable when it is made to mean that no person ever saw him, that no letter left the house, or that the biographer possesses an uninterrupted record of every day. 1919CNRS Le Journal, Disparition d’un génie des mathématiques; Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, I,” nos. 9–10; Alexandre Grothendieck, Lettre à L. Illusie, 2010, https://csg.igrothendieck.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/L155u.pdf.

The later letters and transfers demonstrate why literal total isolation cannot be sustained. The 1995 archive transfer happened. The 2010 covering letter to Luc Illusie happened. The manuscripts themselves, dated across the 1990s, happened. Each implies material exchanges: paper acquired, documents stored, messages delivered, or boxes moved. None restores a public social life. Together they prevent a picturesque falsehood—the image of a person existing outside all human infrastructure.

The distinction between privacy and unknowability is equally important. A fact may be knowable to neighbors or family and still be inappropriate for a biography. A historian must determine whether a detail can be verified and what explanatory work it performs. The precise position of the house, household routines, medical episodes, disputes with local officials, and family contacts have been reported in varying forms. Much of that material comes through press accounts built around the allure of the reclusive figure. Unless a detail is needed to establish a consequential event and meets a high corroboration threshold, it remains outside this narrative.

There is no evidentiary warrant for retrospective diagnosis here. Religious and metaphysical writing does not itself establish a clinical condition. A clinical label would require a different kind of evidence: records created by qualified practitioners, adequate context, and an ethical reason to publish. The absence of such evidence is not an invitation to diagnose from prose. It is a boundary.

This restraint does not idealize seclusion. Refusal can injure people; withdrawal can leave practical burdens to others; an author’s control over papers can conflict with the labor and rights of correspondents, editors, and institutions. The farewell letter as reported by Scharlau acknowledges ordinary obligations precisely because departure had consequences. But consequence is not motive. The record allows the biographer to describe what had to be managed without claiming to know the private necessity Grothendieck attached to leaving.

How the boundary was kept

The documents that describe the arrangement are few, and most are secondhand. Jackson, drawing on Jean Malgoire, reported that a small number of people knew where Grothendieck was and that he instructed them not to forward mail that arrived for him at the university. Malgoire told her that, close to fifteen years later, the university still received a great deal of correspondence addressed to him.2020Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1211. Scharlau’s summary of the letter left at Les Aumettes adds that Grothendieck had decided never to return to the region, had informed only the landlady, and had paid the rent and the utilities some time in advance. Relying on private testimony, Scharlau also writes that acquaintances had probably been asked to help him look, discreetly, for new lodgings, and that at least one had done so.2121Scharlau, Spirituality, chap. 30.

These reports describe a boundary kept by practice rather than a disappearance. Letters continued to arrive at an institutional address; someone decided not to send them on; a few people held information that others lacked. Such an arrangement depends on other people’s cooperation, and it leaves an asymmetry in the record. The unforwarded letters are known only in aggregate, as “a great deal of correspondence.” The people who knew the address have, for the most part, not described it in public.

Jackson’s own paragraph holds both descriptions side by side. In one sentence Grothendieck “disappeared into the Pyrenées to live in complete isolation”; in the next, a small number of people knew where he was.2222Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1211. The juxtaposition is accurate rather than careless. “Complete isolation” describes the experience of the mathematical community and of the many correspondents whose letters stopped at Montpellier. The second sentence describes the narrower circle through which the arrangement worked. Both are true at once, and neither should be quoted without the other.

A portrait and a département

Among the instructions that Scharlau summarizes from the farewell letter is one concerning a single object: the oil portrait of Grothendieck’s father, painted in the internment camp of Le Vernet. The letter, as he reports it, named the person who was to receive it; a note in his account records conflicting later testimony about what became of the painting.2323Scharlau, Spirituality, chap. 30. The letter itself has not been examined for this account, and the conflict is left as Scharlau reports it.

Chapter 11 described the portrait as colleagues remembered it, hanging for years in Grothendieck’s office: a man with a shaved head and, in Pierre Cartier’s words, a “fiery expression” in the eyes.2424Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, I,” 1047. Its reappearance in 1991 adds one fact to that description. When books, furniture, and other objects were mostly left to another person’s judgment, the portrait was one of the things the letter disposed of by name.

The object matters here for a reason of geography. Le Vernet lies in Ariège. Alexander Schapiro was held there during the first years of the war, while Hanka and her son were interned at Rieucros in Lozère; Grothendieck later wrote that his father disappeared at Auschwitz in 1942.2525Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, I,” 1040; Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”; Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), Promenade, §1. In 1991 the son settled in the same département. The Montpellier notice on his archive states both facts in a few lines of biography without connecting them, and none of the sources consulted for this account makes a connection either.2626Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”.

A biographer is tempted to supply one. The father’s camp and the son’s last village in a single département would make an elegant symmetry, and symmetry persuades. Nothing in the documents says that Grothendieck chose Ariège for this reason, that he went to Le Vernet, or that he ever remarked on the coincidence. Lasserre and Le Vernet are different places with different histories, and the record supports a fact of geography, not a motive. The coincidence is stated once, here, and no explanation is built on it.

A blank in public time

Over a year separates the last Les Aumettes letter in June 1991 from the first securely dated BnF manuscript unit in August 1992. A dramatic biography might fill that span with adaptation to the new house, encounters, crisis, or revelation. The sources used here do not. The blank is not proof that nothing happened. It is a measure of what the present archive and public record do not establish.

This kind of blank differs from silence in a correspondence that is known to be complete. The late papers may preserve drafts dated August 1991 through February 1997 in BnF unit 43, but that container gathers notes associated with larger projects and does not offer a diary of settlement. The catalog names content and range; it does not authorize a daily chronology. 2727Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck.

The safest bridge is intellectual rather than scenic. The April and June letters show Grothendieck thinking about categorical foundations, physics, mathematical models, and their limits. The August 1992 manuscript Géométrie élémentaire schématique treats geometry in connection with Riemannian-Lorentzian structures according to the BnF description. This is not proof of uninterrupted work on one project, but it shows that mathematical and physical questions present before departure reappeared in the first major dated work after it. 2828Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck; Grothendieck, Lettre à A. Y.

At the same time, the container titled Réflexions sur la Vie et le Cosmos announces an enlarged horizon. “Life” and “cosmos” are not technical subdivisions of algebraic geometry. The later catalog branches into psyche, matter, evil, creation, and drafts. The work beginning in 1992 would therefore preserve mathematical continuities while placing them inside a system of reflection that no university department could comfortably own.

That enlarged horizon is one reason the village became alluring to outsiders. It appeared to offer a physical explanation for an intellectual crossing: in isolation, the story suggests, mathematics became cosmology. But geography cannot perform that causal work. La Clef des Songes and other late writing predate Lasserre. The registers were already coexisting. The move changed conditions of contact and circulation; it did not create every idea that followed.

To leave the blank unfilled is not a failure of narrative. It is a positive account of evidence. The year 1991 contains hard points: dated letters, a reported departure, a named destination, papers transferred before and after. Between them are spaces where only conjecture would create continuity. A truthful biography can move across those spaces by saying exactly what carries: documents, projects, and a changed boundary around the author.

Physics as a standing question

One intellectual thread can be followed across the move without reconstructing any scene. In June 1991, still at Les Aumettes, Grothendieck wrote to A. Y. about physics and its mathematical models.2929Grothendieck, Lettre à A. Y. In August 1992 two manuscripts were begun in the same month. The BnF describes Géométrie élémentaire schématique as a collection of auxiliary mathematical results; the other, Esprit et Matière – ou l’Esprit des Lois naturelles, continued in sections until January 1994, and its last section is titled “Conservation de l’énergie.”3030Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck. Its subtitle names the laws of nature as its object.

The same interest appears in the testimony of the few mathematicians who saw him in the 1990s. Leila Schneps and Pierre Lochak told Jackson that he had developed a strong interest in physics and was frustrated by what he felt was a lack of rigor in the field. They sent him books on physics that he had asked for. In one letter, Jackson reports, he asked a “disarmingly simple question”: what is a meter?3131Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1211.

The question invites comparison with an earlier one. As a student in Montpellier after the war, Grothendieck had set out to supply what his school textbooks lacked, a serious definition of length, area, and volume, and Chapters 13 and 14 followed that question into his own theory of measure.3232Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, I,” 1041. A question about the meter asks, in a similar spirit, what a unit of length is before anyone uses it. The resemblance is a comparison made by this account. The sources do not say that Grothendieck made it, and the physical manuscripts of the 1990s have not been read here closely enough to say how, or whether, he answered the question in them.

Two crossings in 1995

Two dated events of 1995 show the arrangement from outside. On July 28, 1995, according to the University of Montpellier’s account of its archive, Grothendieck completed the second stage of his gift of mathematical papers to Jean Malgoire; the first stage had taken place at the end of June or the beginning of July 1990.3333Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”. Jackson reported that in 1995 he formally conferred on Malgoire the legal rights to his mathematical papers, and that Les Dérivateurs, written during those years, was given to Malgoire the same year.3434Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1206, 1211. A gift completed in stages, by a man who no longer lived in the region, required contact, transport, and agreement. Chapter 64 follows what the papers became.

The second crossing had a history that ran parallel to the village years. In 1991, the year Grothendieck left Les Aumettes, Leila Schneps read his Esquisse d’un programme in Paris. “It was a wild expression of mathematical imagination,” she later told Jackson.3535Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1205–6. She edited a volume on dessins d’enfants that appeared in 1994, and with Pierre Lochak she organized a conference around the Esquisse, whose proceedings printed the text for the first time.3636Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1206; Schneps, The Grothendieck Theory of Dessins d’Enfants; Schneps and Lochak, Geometric Galois Actions 1.

On September 2, 1995, Schneps and Lochak met him. The date comes from Grothendieck himself, in a letter he wrote from Lasserre to Frans Oort in February 2010. The conference at Luminy had run from August 27 to September 1. In 2010 Grothendieck wrote that the editors had met him “the very next day of the conference” without ever mentioning the conference or the project of a book.3737Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck”, letter of February 3, 2010. The visitors’ account, as Jackson reported it in 2004, emphasizes something else. They told him about the progress made on the program he had outlined in the Esquisse, and he was surprised to learn that people were still interested in his work.3838Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1211.

The two accounts were set down at different dates and for different purposes. One, given by the visitors, concerns what Grothendieck learned; the other, written to a colleague whose contribution he had just discovered, concerns what he was not told. They need not contradict each other, and this account does not adjudicate between them. Together they establish a meeting, its date, and its subject.

What followed, in Jackson’s account, was an exchange of letters that “began to swing between warm friendliness and cold suspicion,” and then an end to contact.3939Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1211. The description comes from the correspondents and is not a diagnosis. For the years after 1991 it establishes something plainer: the boundary was neither absolute nor fixed. Grothendieck opened it for particular people and purposes, and closed it again.

Papers that moved without him

The years in which Grothendieck’s address was known to a few people were also the years in which several of his unpublished mathematical texts first reached print or transcription. Set side by side, the dates show how far the history of the man and the history of his papers had separated.

In 1995 the Université Montpellier II issued a 253-page transcription of the first thirty-seven paragraphs of La Longue Marche à travers la théorie de Galois, edited by Jean Malgoire with a foreword.4040Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1205, 1212; Université de Montpellier, “Archives Grothendieck”. By 2004, Jackson reported, Schneps and Lochak had typed a large part of the handwritten Longue Marche into TeX, and they had started the website of the Grothendieck Circle, which gathered material on his life and work.4141Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1211. The Esquisse d’un programme, written in 1984 for an application to the CNRS, reached print in the Luminy proceedings of 1997.4242Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1205–6; Schneps and Lochak, Geometric Galois Actions 1. Les Dérivateurs, given to Malgoire in 1995, was being made available on the web by 2004, in an edition by Matthias Künzer, Malgoire, and Georges Maltsiniotis; the editors’ page credits Künzer with the deciphering and typing on which it rests.4343Jackson, “As If Summoned from the Void, II,” 1206; Maltsiniotis, “Les Dérivateurs (The Derivators)”. Chapters 53, 55, and 64 follow these texts as texts. Here only their dates matter.

None of these undertakings is described in the sources consulted as initiated by Grothendieck, and the documents in which he responded to them date from 2010. In his letter of February 3, 2010, he told Frans Oort that he had learned only the previous month, from the editors, of the existence of Geometric Galois Actions 1 and of the 1995 conference whose proceedings it began.4444Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck”, letter of February 3, 2010. The book had then been in print for thirteen years.

The sequence corrects two opposite impressions. The village years were not years in which his mathematics lay untouched while he wrote about other things: other people were transcribing, editing, and printing it. Nor were they years in which he took part in that work. The sources show him, at most, receiving occasional news of it. Chapter 63 follows his declaration of January 2010 on the circulation of his writings.

One exchange by post, 2010

A month after that declaration, Grothendieck wrote a letter of another kind. Its recipient, Frans Oort, later published it with Ching-Li Chai in facsimile and transcript, and it shows how the boundary worked in both directions. It is dated “Lasserre February 3, 2010.” Grothendieck wrote that he had found in Oort’s expository paper in Geometric Galois Actions 1 “an earnestness, a warmth of tone, highly unusual nowadays in mathematical spheres,” that he had first thought of writing on January 12, and that other matters had occurred to him since. He asked whether the letter had reached Oort at his former address, asked for Oort’s personal address, and promised to “write more extensively about matters close to my heart.” He also asked whether Oort had received the copy of Récoltes et Semailles he had sent him, with a dedication, “by September 1985.”4545Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck”, letter of February 3, 2010.

The comments printed after the transcript record what happened next. The copy of Récoltes et Semailles had never reached Oort. His answer to the letter was opened, resealed, and returned to the sender; his note adds, in brackets, that the personal address was missing. A letter of admiration from an audience at the University of Pennsylvania came back unopened. The comments end: “Alas — further attempts for communication were in vain.”4646Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck”.

The exchange does not show why the replies came back, and this account does not guess. It shows the mechanics. A letter could leave the village and arrive; a reply could be opened, resealed, and sent back. Chai and Oort called the letter “gentle” and wrote that in it “the need of contact and communication was obvious.”4747Chai and Oort, “Life and Work of Alexander Grothendieck”. The judgment is theirs, and it stands beside the returned envelopes without contradicting them. The pattern resembles the exchange with Schneps and Lochak in the 1990s: contact began, and then it stopped.

Why “the village” is enough

The chapter title names a scale of belonging without claiming intimacy. “The house” would make a private structure the narrative center. “The hermitage” would import religious meaning. “Disappearance” would deny the letters, transfers, and later manuscripts. “The village” records an inhabited public place and the limit at which description stops.

It also corrects the fantasy that Grothendieck left society for unpeopled nature. A village has roads, property, services, local government, and neighbors even when a resident limits contact. The regional map shows this ordinary infrastructure. Seclusion occurred within society, not beyond it.

The title does not claim that Lasserre accepted or understood Grothendieck in one collective way. No village has one mind. It avoids making residents into a chorus of witnesses and leaves their private relations unmined.

Finally, “the village” marks a contrast in scale with the universal languages associated with Grothendieck. The contrast may be suggestive, but it is not causal. Small place and large thought can coexist without the first explaining the second. The archive, not landscape symbolism, establishes what was written there.

The village as working place

The BnF dates the large Réflexions sur la Vie et le Cosmos sequence from 1992 to 1999. Its first cataloged unit, Géométrie élémentaire schématique, begins in August 1992. This creates a documentary interval after the 1991 move, not a scene. The catalog does not establish how the new house was arranged, which page was first written there, or what daily schedule produced the manuscript. It does establish that a dated, sustained sequence had begun within roughly a year of the move. 4848Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck; Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF”.

The phrase “invisible work” names this condition better than “silence.” To most of the mathematical world, Grothendieck was absent. The absence was real: no seminars, no conference circuit, no new journal papers, no ordinary circulation of results. At the writing table, however, the production of text continued at extraordinary length. Invisibility describes a relation between work and audience. It does not mean nonexistence.

Nor did the work retain a stable disciplinary boundary. The 1992 material included geometry, formal structures applied by Grothendieck to the psyche, reflections on physics, cosmology, creation, and the problem of evil. Those projects will require different standards of reading. A page containing equations does not automatically become accepted physics; a formal vocabulary does not validate a claim about spiritual reality; an autobiographical assertion does not prove the motives of another person. Yet the mixing of registers is itself a fact about the archive. The village was the place where the boundaries Grothendieck drew between mathematics, self-examination, theology, and cosmology became increasingly unlike the boundaries maintained by academic disciplines.

This was not the export of La Clef des Songes into a new setting. La Clef had been written in 1987–1988, before Lasserre. But it supplied questions, terms, and methods that the later corpus extended: dream interpretation, inner listening, creation, the relation between psyche and world, and the claim that personal experience could disclose a larger order. The chronology has to move backward before it can move forward. The dream manuscript is a prehistory to the village archive and a warning about how to read it.

By the end of 1991, then, several boundaries coincided without becoming identical. Grothendieck had crossed a geographic boundary from the Vaucluse to Ariège. He had strengthened a social boundary around access to himself. He had placed a material boundary between papers given to Malgoire and papers kept for the work ahead. And he had carried across an intellectual boundary questions that no longer fit securely within mathematics.

The village did not explain those decisions. It held them. The next documentary object is therefore not a glimpse through a window or a recollection by a curious visitor. It is a typescript already several years old, corrected, layered with notes, and later preserved in two boxes at the BnF: La Clef des Songes ou Dialogue avec le bon Dieu.
