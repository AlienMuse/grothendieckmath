Where omniscience enters

Biography becomes least trustworthy at the instant it feels most intimate. A date and place give way to weather; a recorded act gives way to motive; a letter becomes a transparent confession; silence acquires an emotion according to the writer’s design. The reader may not notice the crossing because grammar supplies no border between evidence and invention. A sentence can move from “he wrote” to “he knew” without changing its confident tone.

Grothendieck’s life invites such crossings. Childhood separation, internment, his father’s murder at Auschwitz, statelessness, mathematical eminence, rupture with IHES, ecological activism, disputes with colleagues, spiritual writing, family distance, and withdrawal to Lasserre offer the materials of several powerful plots. One plot makes adversity the source of mathematical eminence. Another makes eminence the cause of isolation. A third reads later spiritual conviction as the hidden destination of earlier mathematics. Each can arrange true events and still become false by converting sequence into necessity.

The biographer’s limit begins with a refusal: no invented dialogue, private thought, motive, or destiny. This is not a ban on narrative. It requires narrative to emerge from documented action and acknowledged interpretation. A private recollection excluded from the reader layer cannot support an independently established scene, chronology, exchanged words, or emotional verdict; only documented public acts can carry that burden.

The limit also applies to the author of this book. Stating that the record permits no answer is sometimes correct, but repeated as a ritual it can become evasive. The task is to say exactly what is known, through which source, and what narrower inference is reasonable. A dated manuscript can establish that a phrase existed by a given time. A revision can show that the writer reconsidered wording. Neither alone reveals whether the revision came from doubt, tactical concern, fatigue, or advice. The boundary must be drawn claim by claim.

Five levels of assertion

A responsible chapter can distinguish five levels. The first is the documentary fact: a page, event record, appointment, publication, or date preserved in a reliable source. The second is attributed testimony: a named person reports an experience or recollection. The third is corroborated reconstruction: independent sources converge on a sequence, with discrepancies stated. The fourth is interpretation: the biographer proposes a pattern and identifies it as a reading. The fifth is speculation. Speculation can guide research; it should almost never appear as narrative fact.

The levels are not a hierarchy in which documents always defeat memory. Documents have purposes and omissions; memory can preserve what institutions ignored. A contract establishes terms, not the whole relationship. A memoir reveals the author’s retrospective understanding, not a camera recording the past. The advantage of levels is transparency. Readers can disagree with an interpretation without having to distrust the dates beneath it.

Grothendieck’s own late writings intensify the problem because genres mingle. Récoltes et Semailles is autobiography, meditation, mathematical history, moral indictment, and self-analysis. La Clef des Songes presents dreams within a spiritual interpretation. Réflexions sur la Vie et le Cosmos moves through mathematics, physics, psyche, metaphysics, and theology. A sentence that functions as evidence for Grothendieck’s belief at the time may be weak evidence for another person’s conduct or for an external historical event. 11Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF,” 1–2; Grothendieck, Récoltes et semailles; Centre for Grothendieckian Studies, “*La Clef Des Songes* Published by Éditions Du Sandre”.

The words testimony, claim, interpretation, and demonstration must therefore retain distinct meanings. A text may call an argument a demonstration outside mathematics; the biographer cannot transfer the certainty associated with proof merely because the author was a mathematician. Nor may a mathematical diagram be used to suggest that a psychological classification has been mathematically validated. Competence in mathematics helps identify formal structure and missing proof. It does not confer clinical or theological authority.

The seductions of diagnosis

Readers of Grothendieck have sometimes used psychiatric language to characterize his late polemics, suspicions, spiritual convictions, or isolation. Pierre Schapira’s review, for example, describes part of Récoltes et Semailles with a clinical-sounding term while disputing its account of mathematical credit. The review is a named critic’s judgment. It cannot be promoted by this biography into a retrospective diagnosis. 22Schapira, “A Truncated Manuscript”.

Several reasons converge. A diagnosis requires appropriate expertise, evidence, and usually direct assessment; a biographer reading selected writings has none of those in adequate form. Historical psychiatric categories change. Polemic, error, religious experience, interpersonal grievance, and mental illness are not synonyms. Medical privacy and the dignity of the subject remain relevant after death, while casual labels can also harm living relatives and people with similar conditions.

Restraint does not require the biographer to call every late claim sound. Statements about credit can be checked against publications, correspondence, seminar records, and the testimony of other participants. Contradictions can be displayed. Grandiose self-comparisons can be quoted with context if necessary and legally cleared. Accusations can be attributed and tested. The proper alternative to diagnosis is not reverence; it is evidence.

The same rule governs spiritual writing. The biography can say that Grothendieck interpreted dreams as communications, wrote about God, or pursued the problem of evil over thousands of pages when those facts are documented. It cannot announce that the experiences were revelation or pathological delusion. Those conclusions belong to theological or clinical frameworks not established by the archive. The historian can analyze vocabulary, sequence, reading, and action without adjudicating the transcendent object.

The Lasserre shorthand

The word hermit appears in institutional and journalistic accounts because it communicates severe withdrawal quickly. It also imports an ancient role: the holy or eccentric solitary who rejects society and acquires truth in the wilderness. Applied without care, it turns Lasserre into a stage set and Grothendieck’s neighbors into scenery. It can erase continued writing, necessary contacts, material dependence, illness, and the fact that withdrawal had different intensities over two decades.

The corrective is not to ban the word but to attach it to observed conduct. Grothendieck moved to Lasserre in 1991, while the BnF catalog preserves manuscripts dated through 1999. These anchors establish relocation and continued writing. They do not establish a private communication chronology, degree of isolation, or single motive, and this chapter draws no such conclusion from gaps alone in the available public record. 33Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF,” 1–2; Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck.

Nor should the biography manufacture a final photograph of contentment or despair. The deathbed is especially attractive to biographical closure because it appears to make a life legible from its end. The sources used here establish the date of death and broad circumstances reported elsewhere; they do not authorize a scene in a hospital room, a last thought, or a reconciliation. Chapter 66 ended the life. Part XII concerns what institutions and readers did afterward, not an imagined postscript spoken by the dead.

Maronne and Toën’s exhibition preface offers a useful editorial challenge. They note how easily ecological militancy, provincial teaching, meditation, and disappearance could be assembled into an anecdotal portrait. For their exhibition, they chose instead to recover the mathematician at work from 1973 to 1991. That selective purpose is explicit. A full biography must restore activism, family, and spiritual writing around the mathematics, but it should inherit the curators’ resistance to picturesque simplification. 44Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF,” 1–2; Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck.

The archive is not the person

An archive is material selected by survival. Grothendieck kept some pages and discarded others. He entrusted one corpus to Malgoire, left another in an ordered library, and made provisions that helped bring late work to the BnF. These acts are meaningful, but they did not preserve every conversation, daily routine, friendship, quarrel, or abandoned scrap. The archive is exceptionally large and still radically incomplete as a life.

Size can worsen the illusion of totality. Thirty thousand pages seem sufficient to fill every silence. In fact, repetitive writing can give one concern disproportionate documentary mass. A person mentioned once may have occupied years of life; an idea analyzed for thousands of pages may have dominated writing without dominating every day. Page count measures paper. It does not directly measure importance, sincerity, duration, or causal force.

Arrangement adds another mediation. The author’s sequence, where recoverable, shows one relation among pages. The archivist’s hierarchy makes the relation serviceable. A digital interface adds search and thumbnails. The biographer adds a narrative order. Each layer can disclose and distort. The SAA code’s statement that archival practice is not neutral is not an accusation against archivists; it is a reason to document appraisal, description, and access decisions. 55Society of American Archivists, “SAA Core Values Statement and Code of Ethics”, Judgment, Privacy.

A strong biography therefore cites the archival object and the path to it. It says “in volume X, leaf Y, dated Z,” not “in the archive.” It notes whether the date is authorial or assigned, whether the text is draft or fair copy, whether cancellation is visible, whether surrounding pages continue the thought, and whether quotation comes from a scan, transcription, or edition. If access was mediated by a catalog or exhibition, that mediation is named.

The same discipline prevents the archive from silencing people less well preserved. Grothendieck wrote extensively about colleagues. His account cannot become their only voice merely because his papers are voluminous. Correspondence, institutional minutes, publications, interviews, and archives of others may correct or complicate him. Absence of a reply in his fonds does not prove that no reply existed. A biography built from one giant archive can reproduce its subject’s asymmetries unless it actively looks outward.

Living people inside a dead man’s papers

The ethical center of late biography often lies with third parties. Grothendieck died in 2014; many people he taught, criticized, loved, avoided, or described lived beyond him, as did descendants of others. A private letter can expose health, belief, sexuality, family conflict, or an allegation unrelated to the public significance that justified research. The fact that a celebrated mathematician wrote the sentence does not make every named person a public subject.

The Research Ethics Library’s guidance on biography asks what purpose is served by including private or compromising information and whether it enlarges understanding rather than satisfies curiosity. It advises weighing knowledge and public interest against privacy, harm, and the position of relatives and other third parties. It also emphasizes trust when access depends on private archive owners. 66Norwegian National Research Ethics Committees, “Biography”.

That balance cannot be reduced to consent alone. An heir cannot necessarily consent on behalf of every correspondent. A public figure may be subject to legitimate scrutiny even when relatives object. Conversely, a legally publishable detail may remain ethically irrelevant. The editorial record should state the decision: included because it bears directly on a documented public act; paraphrased to minimize unnecessary exposure; anonymized where identity adds nothing; withheld pending corroboration; or excluded as gratuitous.

Interviews require a parallel protocol. The interviewee’s memory is identified as memory. Dates and factual claims are cross-checked when possible. The interviewer does not promise control incompatible with independent scholarship, and any agreed conditions are recorded. Silence, hesitation, or refusal is not narrated as confession. A family member’s affection or anger is part of testimony’s context, not a defect that disqualifies the voice.

Mathematical biography needs mathematical limits

Grothendieck’s technical work creates another temptation: to substitute authority for explanation. A biography can call him revolutionary, quote medal citations, and move on. That form of praise is inaccessible to general readers and unhelpful to mathematicians. The alternative is layered explanation: begin with the human problem, give a faithful intuition, then offer a precision window with definitions and references.

The intuition must name its limit. A scheme is not simply a shape made of equations. A topos is not an ordinary space with more open sets. A motive is not one hidden object already constructed in every desired category. A dessin’s childlike appearance does not make Galois action easy. The limit is where intellectual honesty appears; without it, metaphor becomes a claim that experts know is false.

Attribution needs the same granularity as archival citation. “Grothendieck proved the Weil conjectures” is false; Deligne proved the remaining conjecture using a landscape transformed by Grothendieck and many collaborators. “Grothendieck invented modern derived algebraic geometry” is false; his ideas and late programs are antecedents to theories built by later mathematicians. A biographer should explain the relation rather than choose between worship and erasure.

Mathematical review must be external to literary confidence. Elegant prose can conceal a wrong hypothesis, a reversed implication, or a premature attribution. Specialists should check both the equations and the narrative verbs.

Quotations and the pressure to sound authentic

Direct quotation supplies voice, and voice supplies the strongest illusion of presence. For that reason quotations need stricter, not looser, control. Every French sentence must be checked against a named edition or archival image. Ellipses must not remove a temporal qualification. Translation must not intensify certainty. Page or section locators must allow another reader to recover the context.

The 2010 declaration shows the stakes. Isolating its broad opening can suggest an absolute refusal that extends in one unbroken grammatical form beyond death. Isolating only its lifetime qualification can suggest that Grothendieck welcomed posthumous publication. The document contains both an expansive statement of non-intention and a later sentence with a temporal phrase. The biography’s responsibility is to fully preserve the tension rather than deploy a fragment as a verdict. 77Grothendieck, “Déclaration d’intention de non-publication,” 1.

Epigraphs deserve the same discipline. Their isolated position invites the reader to treat them as the author’s last word on a chapter. The chapter-title metaphor is traceable through Colin McLarty’s translation and locator, but this account has not checked the full French passage against an authorized primary edition. The quotation is therefore not used, even though the chapter title can remain. A visible gap is preferable to a beautiful, unstable sentence. 88Colin McLarty, “The Rising Sea: Grothendieck on Simplicity and Generality,” in Episodes in the History of Modern Algebra (1800–1950), ed. Jeremy J. Gray and Karen Hunger Parshall (American Mathematical Society, 2007), 1–2.

Translation adds authorship. McLarty discusses multiple plausible English renderings, each with different rhythms and implications. A new translation should identify its translator and reasons; it should not be printed as if English words were Grothendieck’s own. Short quotation rights and translation rights must also be cleared independently of textual accuracy.

The boundary as narrative form

Limits need not appear only in endnotes. They can shape chapters. A disputed date can remain a fork in the sentence. An attributed recollection can carry the speaker’s name before the claim. A mathematical program can close with what remained unproved. An archive can be described as current to a stated date. A scene can end where the record ends.

This form makes room for interpretive argument. The biography may argue that belonging, creation, and refusal recur across Grothendieck’s life, provided it shows the documents and counterexamples from which the pattern is drawn. It may compare the relative viewpoint in mathematics with his suspicion of fixed institutions, provided it labels the comparison an analogy and refuses to make a theorem cause a political decision. Mathematical style is not a psychiatric key.

It may also make moral judgments. Leaving students without guidance had consequences; accusations of theft can be tested; institutional military funding can be documented; ecological warnings can be compared with their historical moment. Ethical seriousness does not require neutral adjectives. It requires that judgment address actions and evidence rather than imagined essence.

The exhibition preface says that complex analysis respects readers by refusing formulas made simple enough to mislead. That principle extends beyond an exhibition. Complexity here does not mean maximal detail or indecision. It means preserving distinctions that the evidence requires: custody and copyright, belief and event, program and proof, influence and credit, privacy and secrecy, solitude and inactivity. 99Maronne and Toën, “Préface”.

What the book cannot finish

This biography cannot finish the archive. New catalog descriptions, permissions, editions, and mathematical readings will come after it. It cannot finish Grothendieck’s reputation; different communities will continue to argue about activism, pedagogy, credit, spirituality, and withdrawal. It cannot assign one meaning to an oeuvre that changed both its objects and its intended readers.

Nor can it reconcile the people whom Grothendieck accused or left. Narrative closure is not restitution. A balanced paragraph does not repair a relationship, and a posthumous book cannot obtain consent from its subject. The most it can do is avoid adding false scenes, misquotation, careless exposure, and inherited caricature to the record.

The limit is not ignorance celebrated. It is knowledge kept within its warrant. It allows the archive to remain larger than the book, other witnesses to remain independent of the protagonist, and later mathematicians to own their results. It allows readers to see where interpretation begins and to make another one.

At the edge of the final chapter, one methodological comparison remains: conceptual enlargement can turn a resistant problem into a consequence of wider theory. Biography cannot use that method without alteration. A larger context may soften a false opposition, but it cannot dissolve a person into theory. The life is over; the archive and mathematics continue; the writer’s task is to approach both without pretending that additional context removes every boundary.

Adversity is not a master key

Any attempt to connect childhood adversity to adult mathematics, politics, or relationships carries a high evidentiary burden. The relevant wartime and family facts belong in earlier chapters supported by their own primary records. Part XII does not reuse them as a shortcut to motive or diagnosis.

A master-key narrative is attractive because it creates unity. It can turn displacement into an explanation for relative mathematics, danger into a complete explanation for pacifism, or early loss into a complete explanation for later withdrawal. Each line contains a causal leap. Similar experiences produce unlike lives, and mathematical concepts arise within technical problems and communities as well as biographies.

Responsible comparison states its level. It may observe that questions of home, foundation, and belonging recur in documents from different periods. It may place a political statement beside an earlier experience if Grothendieck himself made the connection. It may ask whether relative mathematics offers a metaphor for a life across bases. It may not say that childhood “caused” schemes without evidence capable of carrying that verb.

Adversity narratives can also reduce relatives and communities to functions in a famous subject’s development. Every person mentioned has a history not exhausted by an effect on Grothendieck. The biography owes those lives independent sourcing and should not insert communities as background figures used to prove resilience.

The ethical limit is thus also historical breadth. The more consequential the causal claim, the more independent evidence it requires. Where Grothendieck’s own retrospective account interprets childhood, it is cited as retrospective self-understanding. Where the book proposes a thematic echo, it names it as an echo. It does not translate suffering into destiny.

Silence has several causes

Archives create visible speech and invisible silence. A missing reply may never have been written, may have been lost, may remain in another archive, or may be restricted. A gap in a notebook may mark interruption, discarded pages, or a shift to another file. A person’s refusal to answer can be deliberate without revealing the reason.

Biographical prose often turns silence into evidence because it provides dramatic economy: “He never replied, confirming the break.” Unless the source records receipt, intention, and context, the final clause is too strong. The factual sentence may be “No reply has been located in the archive consulted.” The interpretation can follow as one possibility rather than a concealed certainty.

Grothendieck’s withdrawal leaves gaps in the record. This account does not convert absence, sparse correspondence, or later testimony into a private chronology, degree, or cause. It confines direct statements to the documented move, dated manuscripts, institutional custody, publication events, and other public acts whose sources can be recovered. 1010Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF,” 1–2; Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck.

Institutional silence requires equal care. An absent minute may mean that a discussion was informal, not that it never occurred. A delayed publication may reflect rights, cost, editing, or disagreement. A restricted archival series may protect privacy rather than conceal scandal. Before assigning motive, the biographer should seek policies, correspondence, and the testimony of decision-makers.

Silence can be respected without being romanticized. A subject’s refusal of publicity is historical action. It does not command the historian to suppress public facts, and it does not authorize invasion in retaliation. The boundary lies in relevance, warrant, and the rights of affected people.

Contradictions are not clues to a hidden essence

Grothendieck could seek collective mathematical work and later accuse the community of betrayal. He could circulate vast manuscripts and oppose republication. He could leave papers to institutions and seek withdrawal from readers. He could reject honors and care intensely about recognition. A neat biography treats each contradiction as a puzzle whose solution is a stable personality.

Contradictions may instead arise from time, genre, audience, and changing judgment. The person who wrote in 1984 need not hold exactly the position of 2010. A letter to one recipient may emphasize what a public declaration omits. An intention about preservation can coexist uneasily with an intention against publication. The historian’s first task is chronological: place each statement in its documentary situation.

The second task is lexical. “Publication,” “circulation,” “archive,” “gift,” and “withdrawal” may not designate the same acts across documents. Grothendieck’s declaration lists print, electronic forms, excerpts, letters, and translations with unusual breadth, while its next sentence contains a lifetime qualification. The complexity is in the text, not an excuse invented by later readers. 1111Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF,” 1–2; Bibliothèque nationale de France, NAF 29093. Fonds Alexandre Grothendieck.

The third task is to resist retrospective harmonization. A will may clarify legal intention without making emotional motives transparent. An heir’s decision may resolve custody without explaining every family relation. A formal edition may establish a base text without resolving the ethics of publication. Some tensions remain tensions after all available sources are read.

Preserving contradiction does not make the biography shapeless. Chronology supplies form. Consequences supply stakes. The reader can see positions change and actions collide without being handed a diagnostic key that makes the subject simpler than the evidence.

The source that is wrong in one place

A source does not become wholly useless because it contains an error. The University of Montpellier’s announcement can be reliable about its own digitization and promotional about possible discoveries. A CNRS obituary can accurately record institutional reception while repeating a heroic anecdote that requires separate checking. A family interview can be primary evidence of the speaker’s experience and secondary evidence of an older event.

Source evaluation therefore occurs at claim level. Institutional sources have special authority for their dates, programs, holdings, and procedures. They may not be neutral about prestige. A publisher is authoritative about ISBN and release date, not automatically about a work’s historical significance. A mathematician may be authoritative about a proof and partisan about a dispute. An archive catalog is authoritative about its current description and capable of revision.

This approach prevents the false choice between credulity and cynicism. The BnF press release can anchor the contents and dates of NAF 29093 while its Einstein comparison remains publicity. Schapira’s review can identify a concrete editorial omission claim while his psychological language remains his interpretation. The parts are cited differently. 1212Bibliothèque nationale de France, “Les manuscrits du mathématicien Alexandre Grothendieck entrent à la BnF,” 1–2; Schapira, “A Truncated Manuscript”.

Conflicting official sources deserve an issue card, not a covert preference. The 1990 versus 1990-and-1995 transfer dates are a model. Both formulations appear in institutional pages. The biography uses the shared fact and states the difference. A later deposit instrument can close the issue without requiring either institution to be treated as generally unreliable.

The source inventory records access quality as well. A page blocked to direct retrieval can still have a stable identifier corroborated elsewhere, but no unviewed detail should be attributed to it. A search snippet can lead to a source; it should not become the final source when the underlying record is inaccessible.

Revision as an ethical obligation

The boundary of knowledge moves when evidence changes. A biography built as modular chapters, fact cards, and issue records can revise a date without quietly rewriting history. The old claim, new source, decision, and affected passages should be logged. This procedure treats correction as part of authorship rather than embarrassment.

Revision is particularly important for living archives. A catalog can add a component description. Rights holders can clarify authorization. An interviewee can correct a transcript. A mathematical reviewer can find that an intuition overstates a theorem. Each correction may alter narrative rhythm, but rhythm cannot be the reason to retain error.

There are limits to correction too. A family memory revised decades later does not erase the earlier account; both have dates and contexts. A new edition does not make circulating versions vanish from reception history. A legal permission obtained today does not retroactively authorize yesterday’s act. Versioned records preserve sequence.

A biographer should disclose consequential changes between editions. If an image was removed because provenance collapsed, the credit list should not conceal the attempt. Transparency builds confidence more effectively than a surface without visible uncertainty.

A practical test for every intimate sentence

Before retaining an intimate sentence, the biographer should identify its exact source; whether the statement was public, private, or inferred; whether the person discussed is living or represented by living relatives; whether the detail explains a consequential action or only adds color; whether independent corroboration exists; whether less exposure can convey the same knowledge; and what omission would lose.

The questions do not produce automatic answers. A private fact can be essential to understanding a public decision. A relative’s objection can coexist with strong historical justification. An unflattering fact can be included without sensationalism. The test makes the purpose explicit and leaves a record for editorial review.

Language supplies a second test. “He claimed” can sound dismissive; “he wrote” is often narrower. “He confessed” imports guilt; “he described” may fit the document. “He became paranoid” is diagnosis; “he accused former colleagues of…” identifies the action and permits verification. “He vanished” creates legend; “he moved to Lasserre and sharply restricted contact” names conduct.

Scene detail receives a third test. If time, weather, clothing, room, gesture, or spoken words are not in a source, they do not enter as fact. A documented photograph can support visible details within its frame, not thoughts outside it. A map can establish distance, not the traveller’s feeling. Restraint in scene construction keeps attention on the details that evidence does give.

These tests are demanding because intimacy is commercially valuable. The book’s subject is entitled neither to hagiography nor to punishment for having been famous. The reader is entitled to know which kind of sentence is being read.

The value of an unfinished portrait

A finished portrait in biography often means that contradictions have been arranged into a face the author can summarize. Grothendieck’s record resists that closure, and the resistance should remain visible. The mathematician of collective seminars and the writer of solitary indictments are the same person across time, not two masks awaiting a revelation of the “real” one.

An unfinished portrait can still be exact. It can establish dates, institutions, works, relationships, and consequences. It can interpret recurring themes. It can make moral distinctions. What it refuses is the sentence that claims total access to motive.

This refusal gives other people room. Colleagues retain versions of events not reduced to Grothendieck’s account. Children are not cast only as heirs. Archivists are not invisible custodians. Later mathematicians are not disciples by default. Readers are not required to accept the biographer’s synthesis as the life itself.

The limit is finally a form of proportion. Grothendieck’s archive is vast; a life was larger. His mathematics is vast; mathematics is larger. His moral criticism was severe; the history of scientific ethics is larger. A biography can honor scale without confusing its subject with the world around him.

That proportion carries into the ending. The last chapter will not discover a final meaning in a late manuscript or turn its governing metaphor into fate. It will state what continues, what remains unsettled, and which practices make responsible continuation possible. The boundary drawn here is what allows that ending to remain open without becoming vague.

There is also a limit to declared limitation. Excessive caveat can make a writer appear rigorous while avoiding judgment. Where records converge, this biography should speak plainly. Where an action harmed others, it can describe consequences. Where mathematics is correct, it can say so after review. Uncertainty must be specific: the transfer date conflicts, the primary epigraph page is unchecked, the rights chain is undocumented. Precision about doubt permits confidence elsewhere. The aim is neither omniscience nor permanent suspension, but proportion between sentence and source.

Proportion also governs emphasis. A spectacular but weakly sourced anecdote should not occupy more narrative space than a documented decade of work. The archive’s abundance should not crowd out teaching, activism, family, or collaborators. Selection is unavoidable; the notes make its rationale available.

The same proportion applies to praise and blame. Neither should outrun the sources. A documented achievement can be described without superlatives; a documented injury can be described without inventing cruelty. Moral clarity depends on accurately named acts, affected people, and consequences. The sentence remains forceful because its evidence is visible and its limits have names.

The family material used here remains attributed testimony from one interview chain, not an independently verified private chronology. The prose therefore refuses diagnosis, motive, and unsupported continuity while omitting unnecessary identifying detail. Attribution marks the boundary between memory and established fact, keeping evidentiary weakness visible beneath polished narrative.

This omission is substantive rather than cosmetic. Provenance remains available through institutional records; the single family testimony is not reproduced. The public-facing narrative receives no health detail, reconstructed contact sequence, diagnosis, motive, or emotional inference from that source chain in this Part.
