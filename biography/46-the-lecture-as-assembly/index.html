Wednesday, room 8

The notice named a mathematical course. Théories de Dieudonné des groupes de Barsotti-Tate: Wednesdays from ten until half past twelve, room 8, beginning November 3, 1971. Beneath that title, Alexander Grothendieck announced that the first meetings would discuss science and technology in the contemporary evolutionary crisis and ask whether scientific research should continue. The number of such sessions would depend on the audience before the course moved to its technical subject.

The notice also described the audience. The Collège de France required no registration and no diploma. Grothendieck listed nationality, sex, religion, age, qualifications, and even haircut among the distinctions that would not bar entry. His notice welcomed interested participants whether or not they were mathematicians. The invitation was concrete: a day, hour, room, address, and telephone number. It transformed the openness of the Collège from an institutional custom into a political instrument.11Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France,” 1.

There was a qualification printed in the first lines. He would conduct the nontechnical discussions on his own responsibility, without official sanction and without their title on the Collège posters. The institution had approved the mathematical course and rejected the other part of his proposal. The notice therefore occupied an ambiguous position: it used a public chair to stage an event that the assembly of professors had refused to recognize as the chair’s business.

This ambiguity is the subject of the chapter. Grothendieck did not abandon the lecture hall for a street outside knowledge. He contested what the hall could contain and how authority should operate within it. His mathematical reputation obtained the invitation. He then tried to make the invitation answer questions that reputation usually excluded. The result was neither an ordinary course nor an assembly without hierarchy.22Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France”.

What a lecture did

The academic lecture concentrated asymmetries. An institution selected a speaker, announced a subject, arranged a room, and assembled listeners around the expectation that one person knew. In advanced mathematics the asymmetry was intensified by years of prerequisite study. A visiting star could speak to fifty or a hundred people while only a few followed the argument; the others attended from professional obligation, hope, or fear of appearing ignorant. Grothendieck’s career placed him on both sides of this form, as auditor and invited lecturer.33Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 9–10.

His first activist adaptation did not abolish the form. He attached a second lecture. A university inviting him to speak on schemes, cohomology, or Riemann–Roch would also organize a public meeting about science, war, and survival. The arrangement treated scientific prestige as a transferable resource. Organizers who wanted the mathematics had to make room for the politics. A mailing list could seed a local group; an audience attracted by a great mathematician could encounter a critique of the system that produced great mathematicians.44Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 9–10.

The method carried obvious risks. Listeners might accept his political claims because they trusted his theorems. A department might advertise controversy as spectacle. A nontechnical meeting attached to a specialist visit could remain secondary, the unusual sermon after the real intellectual event. Grothendieck increasingly tried to disturb that ordering by making critical comments inside the mathematical lecture itself and by inviting discussion about the ritual of incomprehension.

This was not anti-intellectual populism in its simplest form. He did not claim that an untrained listener could understand every proof immediately. He asked why the institution required people to perform attendance at a talk they could not use, and why the social purpose of the work remained beyond discussion. The difference between difficulty and domination mattered. A subject may be difficult in itself; a profession can still exploit difficulty to rank people and close questions.

Kingston

From January through March 1971 Grothendieck visited Queen’s University in Kingston, Ontario. He lectured on group schemes, a subject continuous with his recent mathematical work. The university’s Papers in Pure and Applied Mathematics published Gordon Edwards’s English translation of his responsibility essay. Its preface praised his mathematical originality and modesty before introducing the movement he had founded.

The setting embodies the double use of academic hospitality. A mathematics department gave him an audience and publication venue. He gave technical lectures and used the same visit to disseminate an argument for refusing the military and questioning research. The translation could reach readers who had not attended a meeting and identified Edwards’s labor in carrying the movement across languages. It also placed an urgent political address inside an academic series, where it could be read at a distance from collective action.55Grothendieck, “The Responsibility of the Scientist Today,” iv–v, 84–87.

Grothendieck did not yet insist that the two parts merge entirely. The mathematical visitor and the activist shared an itinerary. He was learning that the separation itself conveyed a hierarchy: mathematics was the reason for the invitation; survival was the supplementary concern. His later campus practice would make acceptance conditional on a public debate and introduce critical remarks before the technical talk.

The Queen’s stay also connected him with Edwards and the North American edition of Survival. Translation, editing, and movement business occupied time that a visiting professor would ordinarily reserve for research. The university was one node in an international network whose people had their own arguments and projects.

Twenty campuses

In the spring of 1971 he crossed roughly twenty Canadian and American campuses in about three weeks. He recorded the number in his report to the Collège and described it again at CERN. The three weeks referred to an intensive tour, not the entirety of his months in North America. At each stop, he later said, the mathematical invitation became an opportunity to speak with other scientists, students, and people outside science.

He asked at least one mathematician in the host department to take responsibility for organizing the nontechnical event. The request prevented the public meeting from being outsourced entirely to an activist society. It forced a colleague to connect the visit’s two halves. At CERN he reported that no host had refused. That claim is his own measure of the tour, not proof that every department welcomed the discussion or that every audience agreed.

The tour changed his ideas, he said. This admission counters the image of a messenger carrying a finished doctrine. He encountered American radical science, ecological literature, antinuclear campaigners, and forms of counterculture more developed than their French equivalents. The campus offered a concentration of dissent as well as professional power. Students challenged war research, employment hierarchies, curriculum, and the relation between university and industry.

Travel also magnified contradiction. Aeroplanes, university funds, and international academic networks belonged to the technological civilization he criticized. Audience members would ask how he justified using them. The question was not answered by saying that a good end purified the means. His practice remained transitional: he used available infrastructure while calling for a way of life that might not reproduce it.

A proposal for two courses

Back in Paris, Grothendieck prepared his second Collège year. On May 22, 1971, he wrote to the administrator, Étienne Wolff, with a two-part proposal. One part concerned science and technology in the evolutionary crisis; the other concerned Dieudonné theory for Barsotti–Tate groups. His letter admits that he possesses no special competence over the crisis. That lack, it argues, could not be a reason to exclude vital questions from teaching, because no speciality owned them.66Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France,” 2–3.

He opposed two models at once. He would not speak ex cathedra as an expert on civilization. He also would not accept that only a recognized specialist could open the subject. Fragmentation into disciplines, he wrote, was tending toward a negation of knowledge if knowledge meant apprehending a complex reality and acting within it. Everyone had to begin with the means available, then improve those means with time and companions.

The proposal therefore made acknowledged incompleteness a qualification for discussion rather than a disqualification. This was bold and vulnerable. Professors could reply that urgency did not protect students from error and that institutional titles existed precisely to identify fields in which a teacher could be held accountable. Grothendieck’s answer was that excluding the question guaranteed another kind of error: expertise without examination of purpose.

The mathematical part was not camouflage. Barsotti–Tate groups and Dieudonné theory formed a serious research course connected to his last active mathematical program and to work by Luc Illusie and William Messing. He promised the minimum eighteen hours expected for the technical subject. The conflict concerned whether the associate chair’s freedom included beginning elsewhere.

Freedom at the Collège

The Collège de France was not an ordinary degree-granting university. Professors announced research in progress to any auditor who wished to enter; there was no syllabus leading to an examination and no student body admitted through a normal program. A mathematician could therefore choose a current, specialized subject without preparing a general course. This freedom made the institution a powerful platform for new knowledge and made its boundaries less obvious than those of a departmental curriculum.77Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France,” 1–2.

Grothendieck’s status introduced another boundary. He was not the permanent holder of a chair. The associate-professor position was new, intended for a visitor, and limited to two years. By his account he attended the assembly’s deliberations and could speak but not vote. The freedom traditionally exercised by permanent professors did not necessarily transfer without limit to a guest. Wolff’s objection can therefore be understood as institutional governance rather than merely fear of political speech.88Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France,” 2.

The novelty cut both ways. If a distinguished visitor was invited precisely for intellectual originality, close control of his topic undermined the purpose. If the first associate position carried less freedom than permanent chairs, that hierarchy deserved explicit defense. Grothendieck’s proposal forced the Collège to decide what kind of guest it had created.

Open doors did not themselves make the institution democratic. The building, schedule, language, and cultural confidence required to enter selected an audience even without registration. A worker could legally attend a Wednesday morning lecture and be unable to leave work. A person with no mathematical training could join the survival discussion and still experience the Collège as a place belonging to learned elites. Grothendieck’s playful list of haircuts and qualifications acknowledged some social exclusions while underestimating others.

The proposed discussion used the absence of examination to detach learning from certification. No participant needed to prove competence before speaking. That feature aligned with Survivre’s criticism of scientific hierarchy. Yet the room remained under the name of the professor, and the course’s official identity remained mathematics. The experiment depended on the very symbolic capital it sought to redistribute.

Academic freedom was the principle invoked on both sides without always being named. Grothendieck treated it as freedom to examine the ends and social role of one’s discipline. Colleagues could treat it as freedom secured by disciplinary competence and threatened when a professor used a scientific chair for personal politics. One version protected criticism from the field; the other protected inquiry by maintaining the field. Neither can be dismissed without losing part of what universities are for.

The argument also concerned institutional endorsement. Printing the title on the Collège poster did not prove its claims, but it declared the subject a legitimate use of the institution. The proposal sought that legitimacy partly to break the rule that social criticism belonged after hours. The majority withheld it while leaving him personal speech. The conflict was thus over the location of criticism: inside the recognized work of knowledge or beside it.

Survivre published the episode because a private compromise would have defeated the point. Grothendieck’s report states that professional acts, including the decisions of a professors’ assembly, should be public. Some colleagues could reasonably regard deliberation as confidential. His report converted their internal comments into movement evidence without their consent. Transparency and trust collided; his choice favored the public claim.

June 27

The assembly of professors debated the proposal on June 27, 1971. Grothendieck reported the meeting in no. 9 under the title Remous au Collège de France. His is the fullest contemporary account available in the available record, but it is not a neutral minute. He named colleagues, described gestures and moods, and interpreted their arguments through his critique of scientism. The exact vote can be separated from those interpretations; the portraits must remain his.

Wolff opened against the nontechnical part on the ground that it did not fall within the associate chair. Francis Perrin, a physicist and former high commissioner for atomic energy, worried that a mathematician was particularly ill placed to criticize science. Anatole Abragam argued that using mathematical authority to impose personal opinions on Vietnam or nuclear energy would be dishonest. Jean Leray urged an interdisciplinary seminar, because subjects so important should not be left to one person’s lights. These positions differed. An institutional-boundary argument was not the same as a defense of nuclear policy, and a proposed interdisciplinary forum was not simple censorship.

Grothendieck answered that his stated form was discussion, not imposition. He explicitly rejected the authority of the expert over the crisis. The course announcement would distinguish the two parts, so no auditor would be lured to mathematics and surprised by politics. An interdisciplinary seminar might be useful, but it would not accomplish his purpose: integrating critical reflection into mathematical teaching rather than creating a new speciality elsewhere.

Jean-Claude Pecker supported the importance of discussing the questions and also proposed an interdisciplinary setting. Grothendieck remembered Jacques Monod and probably François Jacob and Raymond Aron as favorable. Jean-Pierre Serre, who had proposed Grothendieck’s appointment and renewal, appeared in the report embarrassed by the consequences. Here the account is most personal. Serre’s institutional support had made the experiment possible; Grothendieck presented his discomfort as evidence of the limits of that support.

The cases made against him

The objection from competence had more force than Grothendieck’s caricature of specialization allowed. Scientific authority is fallible, but it can be earned through methods that expose claims to correction. A mathematician speaking on ecology, medicine, nuclear energy, or social psychology might misstate evidence with the persuasive confidence developed in another field. The higher his prestige, the greater the danger. Abragam’s warning about borrowed authority anticipated a problem visible in Grothendieck’s later claims about China and medicine.

Grothendieck’s reply did not remove the problem; it changed the proposed safeguard. He would not ask listeners to accept his conclusions as a mathematician. They would discuss, challenge, and contribute experience. Authority would be checked through an open form rather than through prior credential. This safeguard worked only if the discussion could actually resist his charisma and if reliable knowledge remained available. The CERN exchange would show both possibilities.

Leray’s interdisciplinary proposal offered a different safeguard. Several professors could bring relevant knowledge and correct one another. To Grothendieck, that solution risked creating a new specialist domain and leaving each ordinary teacher free to ignore purpose. The disagreement was not simply interdisciplinary openness versus reaction. It concerned whether responsibility belonged in every course or in a designated forum.

Perrin’s reported claim that mathematics was not a science in the observational sense and that a mathematician was particularly ill placed to criticize science appears, in Grothendieck’s account, almost self-refuting. But it contained a question about standpoint. Grothendieck had limited direct knowledge of experiment, engineering, and laboratory organization. He answered that distance from one apparatus could help reveal the ideology surrounding it. Both claims can be true: an outsider may see assumptions and miss constraints.

Wolff’s argument about the scope of the chair protected an agreement between institution and appointee. An institution unable to define any scope could become a collection of personal platforms. Grothendieck’s position treated the crisis as making the inherited scope morally obsolete. Urgency often produces this collision between office and conscience. The fact that his cause was serious did not automatically entitle him to redefine the office; the fact that an office had rules did not settle whether obedience was responsible.

Serre had proposed the appointment and renewal. The later course dispute placed sponsor and appointee on different sides of an institutional decision. The vote and Grothendieck’s published account record their public positions; they do not establish either man’s feelings or a change in their friendship.99Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France,” 2–7.

Supporters also had distinct reasons. Pecker welcomed discussion of urgent subjects but sought an interdisciplinary arrangement. Monod and Jacob had their own histories of scientific public engagement and could favor critical reflection without accepting Grothendieck’s program. Nine affirmative votes did not constitute nine converts to Survivre. The vote joined temporary agreement on a title, not a movement.

Representing these cases fairly does not make the decision neutral. The assembly approved highly specialized knowledge almost by routine and demanded special justification for asking what that knowledge did in the world. Its standards placed burden on criticism rather than continuation. Grothendieck’s intervention made that asymmetry visible even where his alternative remained incomplete.

The count

The technical title was approved by twenty-five votes to twelve, with six abstentions and two invalid ballots. The nontechnical title was rejected by thirty-two votes to nine, with one abstention and one invalid ballot. In his published report, Grothendieck described the institution as conservative and treated the nine affirmative votes as a sign of movement. He treated the negative and abstaining votes on the technical subject as evidence of disarray, since most professors could not judge its mathematical content.1010Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France,” 2–7.

The counts permit other readings. Some voters may have opposed the entire two-part arrangement and expressed that opposition in both ballots. Some may have objected to the title’s wording, its prediction of crisis, or the use of a visiting chair for political discussion. A vote records a decision, not every motive. Grothendieck’s report is evidence of how he understood the institution and how he wished readers of Survivre to understand it.

The assembly did not forbid him to speak outside the official program. It refused to place the survival subject on the course posters. He announced that the first sessions would proceed under his own responsibility and that the technical minimum would still be met. The difference between prohibition and denial of sanction mattered to Wolff and to the institution. It mattered less to Grothendieck, who saw official exclusion as a demonstration that science protected its boundaries.

The episode cannot be reduced to brave speech against cowardly silence. Professors had a legitimate responsibility for what the Collège advertised. They also inhabited a tradition in which chairs selected new research topics with exceptional freedom. The unusual scrutiny of this proposal exposed the point at which that freedom ceased: not necessarily at falsehood, but at the boundary between a discipline and criticism of its social role.

The course that was and was not advertised

The November notice makes the vote public. It tells readers of an activist journal how to enter the Collège, locate room 8, and join discussion. The technical title remained the institutional cover, but Grothendieck refused to hide the difference. This was an act of procedural disobedience within the terms of his appointment rather than occupation from outside.

Attendance is difficult to reconstruct. A later student remembered only three people at a mathematical session in January 1972, but one sparse meeting cannot describe the whole course. The public discussions and technical lectures may have drawn different audiences. The absence of formal enrollment, one of the Collège’s democratic attractions, also left a thinner administrative record.

The course’s two subjects enacted the contradiction rather than solving it. A listener could spend one week asking whether research should continue and another studying a sophisticated theory inaccessible without years of training. Grothendieck did not pretend that the technical subject had become popular knowledge. He placed its continuation under a question and allowed the audience’s reaction to determine how quickly he moved to it.

The arrangement could be read as holding mathematics hostage to politics or as making mathematics responsible to politics. It could also be read as a teacher openly displaying his divided labor. The course did not stage a final renunciation. It made refusal and continuation occupy the same timetable.

A method called cultural subversion

By June 1972 the journal’s collective editorial had developed practical guidance for public debate. The aim was not to deliver correct doctrine more effectively. It was to create relations in which participants could speak from experience, question the visitor, and hear one another. Several facilitators were preferable to one. A meeting might begin without a formal presentation. Silence should not be filled from panic. Smaller conversations after the official end might contain the real work.

The editorial treats an unforced silence as a condition in which truthful speech might emerge. In a lecture, silence usually confirms that the speaker possesses the floor. In the proposed debate, a pause could release someone who did not compete easily with practiced orators. The visitor’s task was catalytic: encourage a reaction without determining it.1111Survivre … et vivre, no. 12, p. 9.

The editorial also acknowledged that large meetings resisted this ideal. With hundreds present, exchange became fragmented or chaotic; seasoned debaters could monopolize the microphone; theoretical language displaced lived experience. An invited authority could receive questions like a judge and answer until the event again revolved around him. The text urged speakers to listen more than talk and to give up the desire to convince at all costs.

These observations came partly from failure. Survivre meetings did not automatically free speech. Grothendieck’s own brilliance and conviction could overwhelm. Men accustomed to argument dominated rooms. A non-directive form could conceal informal power. The journal’s procedural writing is valuable because it did not assume that good content made good relations.

Orsay, late January

Issue 11 reported a debate at the Faculty of Sciences in Orsay in late January 1972. The event had barely been advertised beyond a small notice at the university restaurant, yet the amphitheatre was full. The report interprets the turnout as evidence that researchers and students experienced the question as immediate and disturbing. It did not provide a complete attendance number or an exact date. A movement itinerary gives January 28, the day after CERN; the prose retains the issue’s safer precision.

Grothendieck began by recounting his route to the question. Discussion quickly became a contest among experienced speakers, but the anonymous reporter noticed intense attention among those who remained silent. One position held that science was corrupted because it served bourgeois power and would be used well after political change. Another connected use, ideology, and scientific method so closely that none could be transformed alone. The critique of method drew the most attention because many researchers recognized its effects in their work.

The scheduled ending passed by three or four hours. The report says the participants agreed to another meeting. A week later about a hundred returned, but the earlier excitement encountered the difficulty of practice. The report accused the second discussion of reproducing the very scientific method it criticized: historical exposition, demonstration, organized ideological positions, and the assumption that saying produced knowledge. A further meeting was planned.1212Survivre … et vivre, no. 11, pp. 21–23.

The self-criticism is more important than a claim that the first meeting succeeded. The report identified the return of performance and specialization inside its alternative. It had no technique guaranteed to produce equality. The repeated meeting was itself the method: expose the failure, return, and try again.

A citadel that invited criticism

CERN was an apt and misleading symbol. It was the European Organization for Nuclear Research, created for fundamental particle physics through cooperation among states. Its accelerators, detectors, engineering, and international staff represented big science at a scale far from Grothendieck’s proposed fields and workshops. The word nuclear and the history of twentieth-century physics connected it in public imagination to weapons and reactors, while its staff could accurately distinguish their research from bomb design and power production.1313Alexandre Grothendieck, Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique? (audio recording), CERN, January 27, 1972, https://cds.cern.ch/record/912518; Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 1–3.

Grothendieck exploited the symbolic connection and admitted his factual distance. The opening joke about a nuclear center that did not do nuclear research drew force from the name, not a classified dossier. Later, when asked about military relations, he explicitly said he had no secret information. A summary that calls CERN a weapons laboratory would therefore falsify the event.1414Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 2, 29.

The center’s internationalism also complicated his critique. Scientists from different countries worked together across national borders—the kind of cooperation he had long defended and that Survivre sought to mobilize. Particle physics could be pursued for knowledge rather than an immediate device. If any institution represented the defense of pure research, CERN offered a strong case. Grothendieck chose a difficult audience rather than an obvious military contractor.

At the same time, scale, cost, and specialization made CERN a case for his question about social purpose. Large public investment was justified through knowledge, technology, prestige, and cooperation. Most citizens could not evaluate the experiments technically. Asking what purpose the research served was therefore legitimate even if no military use could be shown. The answer might include curiosity and international peace; he could then ask how those goods should be weighed against other needs.

The invitation itself matters. CERN maintained a public lecture series on science and responsibility and invited a severe critic. The host’s introduction acknowledged the danger of institutional self-satisfaction. This does not make CERN immune to critique, but it contradicts a picture of a closed fortress breached solely by Grothendieck’s courage. People inside had created the occasion and would use it to oppose him.

The preserved audio makes the event unusual in his biography. Many accounts of his activism pass through memory or a journal written by allies. Here tone, interruption, laughter, applause, and extended objections remain available, even though the later transcript marks inaudible passages. The record does not provide an objective view from nowhere; it preserves a public performance well enough to resist a one-voice legend.

CERN’s catalog supplies the event date and institutional provenance for the audio. The Rennes transcript supplies pagination and marks passages that could not be heard. Because every transcribed sentence has not yet been realigned against the complete recording, the chapter uses the transcript for argument and sequence, avoids inaudible passages, and does not infer tone where the written record supplies only words or an applause marker.1515Grothendieck, Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique? (audio recording); Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?”

The room also materialized the technological society under debate. Simultaneous interpretation, microphones, buttons, recording equipment, and an international center enabled people of different languages to exchange. Technology here enabled the argument to occur and survive. Any categorical indictment had to pass through that fact, whether or not the speakers named it.

CERN, January 27, 1972

The CERN recording begins with a host explaining why the center had invited Grothendieck. For a decade, its public lecture series had periodically asked scientists to reflect on science and responsibility. People at CERN, the host said, could become satisfied with the belief that they did theoretical, harmless work inside an exceptional European collaboration. A speaker from outside that confidence might push the questions further. The introduction did not endorse Grothendieck’s answers. It made the institution’s self-image part of the subject.

Grothendieck opened with pleasure at being there and an irony about the name. He had thought CERN a citadel of advanced nuclear research; he had been corrected that day, he said, because apparently the European Center for Nuclear Research did not do nuclear research. The joke was not a factual analysis of CERN’s particle physics. It exposed the public resonance of the institution’s name and the inability of technical distinctions to remove the atom’s military and civil associations.

He then made the broad claim that current scientific development was an important cause in every major danger to human survival. He did not say it was the sole cause. Speaking personally, he described decades in pure mathematics, long indifference to social consequences, and his decision to abandon research except what earning a living required. The introduction moved from nuclear institutions to his own profession so that critique would not be directed only at the hosts.1616Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 1–4.

The central question asked what social purpose scientific research served. He told the audience that most colleagues could not answer because the question sounded foreign to their training. Pleasure and livelihood were the answers he most often received. He accepted both as real and asked why society accorded scientific pleasure resources and honor unavailable to many other skilled activities.1717Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 4.

Constraint inside the vocation

Grothendieck challenged the claim that research gave scientists pleasure by describing the career pressures he had only recently begun to hear. Publication was necessary to obtain a job, advance, and remain visible. Productivity could be counted in papers or pages while content receded. People lower in the hierarchy did not tell a celebrated professor how constrained they felt. At CERN, Grothendieck said that apparently equal mathematical discussion had contained a rank he had failed to perceive.1818Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 4–7.

He widened the hierarchy beyond scientists. The surrounding ideology treated scientific knowledge as the only real knowledge, leaving most people to experience themselves as ignorant. A tiny fraction possessed the recognized languages; everyone else became a receiver. The critique joined employment pressure inside research to dispossession outside it. Both arose, he argued, when technical performance became the measure of human value.

An audience defends science

After the introductory talk, the host announced questions and reminded the audience to use desk buttons so simultaneous interpreters could hear. He hesitated between calling what followed questions or a debate. Grothendieck preferred the latter term, and the exchange then occupied most of the transcript.1919Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 12.

One questioner asked what made life worth living. Grothendieck contrasted the intense but narrow fulfillment of his mathematical past with the richer range of his current activity. Another asked about science in China, prompting an extended and overly trusting account of barefoot medicine and popular invention. A later participant challenged the entire direction. Many people owed their lives to science, the speaker argued; medicine and penicillin could not be dismissed. Art and mathematics might be pursued for pleasure. Human greed, hierarchy, war, and failed communication were not caused by science alone. The accusation was explicit: Grothendieck’s total rejection approached obscurantism.

The room applauded the challenge. This response matters. The lecture was not a sermon accepted through awe, even if some heard it that way. CERN participants defended work they knew and insisted on benefits his indictment minimized. One questioner argued that people owed their lives to science, which crystallized the objection.2020Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?” 27.

Grothendieck did not withdraw his position. He said that contemporary science was one of society’s principal negative forces and expected many fields to disappear. But he denied advocating a decree against research and imagined a deeply transformed science. He accepted that competition and professional distress were not unique to scientists. He accepted that science was not the only cause of war. He distinguished his knowledge of scientific life as the reason for discussing it rather than every profession.

Answering in the hostile room

The strongest part of the exchange was not agreement but qualification under pressure. When challenged about medicine, Grothendieck did not say antibiotics must vanish. He criticized routine use, then allowed that post-industrial societies might preserve antibiotics and other techniques judged useful case by case. The selection, he argued, should occur through ordinary needs rather than a specialist plan made in advance. This concession limited the apparent totality of his opening without resolving who could make safe judgments.

When asked about military relations at CERN, he admitted possessing no secret information. His criticism concerned public image and the general form of advanced research, not an allegation that a hidden CERN weapons program had been disclosed to him. That distinction is easily lost when the event is summarized as a mathematician denouncing a nuclear weapons laboratory.

When a participant separated ancient war from modern science, Grothendieck agreed that war preceded contemporary research. He answered with the technical refinement of antipersonnel weapons he had discussed in North Vietnam. Science did not invent domination; it altered its capacity and methods. The response maintained the causal claim in narrower form.

He also distinguished accessible art from esoteric mathematics. A person could respond to music without five or ten years of specialist preparation, whereas only a handful in the world might understand his recent work. The comparison idealized the accessibility of art and underplayed forms of training, but it exposed a problem of audience no general claim about beauty could solve. A mathematical creation could be profound and socially restricted at once.

The machinery of a debate

The transcript preserves more than propositions. Applause follows opposing speakers as well as Grothendieck. Inaudible passages are marked rather than invented. Simultaneous interpretation makes the event bilingual but requires participants to use a technical system. The host manages turns. The hall’s architecture and CERN’s equipment organize who can be heard. An assembly does not arise outside institutions; it uses their machinery.

The question-and-answer sequence also retains hierarchy. Participants address Grothendieck, and he answers at length. He remains the axis even when the room disagrees. He accepted objections and answered them at length, but that did not turn the event into a conversation among equals. The format he was trying to leave persists through the grammar of the transcript: question, Alexander Grothendieck, answer.

Yet the public challenge altered what could be said. He had to distinguish science as sole cause from science as one cause, current science from a future practice, useful techniques from the institution of research, and criticism of CERN’s image from knowledge of its contracts. These distinctions make the record more valuable than a polished manifesto. A hostile room forced precision that sympathetic prose did not.

The exchange also gave CERN staff a public space to hear internal doubts without requiring the institution to adopt them. The host’s introduction suggested self-questioning already existed. Grothendieck’s fame made the occasion possible, but the audience’s resistance prevented fame from settling it. The event worked as an assembly to the extent that its outcome remained open.

Five tensions in the exchange

First was the tension between benefit and system. Questioners named medicine and lives saved. Grothendieck answered that a beneficial technique did not vindicate the entire organization of research and that the same knowledge could be directed toward harm. This was logically fair but politically incomplete. Institutions are judged partly by benefits and harms that cannot be reduced to one another. His later allowance for retaining useful techniques was a more discriminating response.

Second was curiosity and allocation. At CERN he described discovery as joyful rather than fraudulent and asked why society financed this joy at high levels while neglecting other needs. An audience member compared science with art. The question exposed a danger in purely utilitarian judgment: knowledge and art can matter without an immediate function. Grothendieck’s answer concerned access and scale, but it never fully established why restricted audience made mathematical creation less worthy.

Third was method and use. Some participants could accept that science was badly used while defending experimental and rational methods. Grothendieck argued that analytical division, separation of reason from other faculties, and hierarchy were embedded in the method itself. His examples showed how methods operate socially, but they did not prove that deduction or experiment necessarily creates domination. The exchange left open whether reform required new ends, new institutions, or a different conception of knowledge.

Fourth was universality and position. Grothendieck spoke about civilization while acknowledging no special competence. He appealed to his experience of mathematics and encounters across campuses. Questioners spoke from physics, medicine, and their work at CERN. No one represented humanity as a whole. The debate’s democratic promise lay not in finding a universal speaker but in allowing positions to confront one another. Its limitation lay in who was present: mostly people able to enter a European research center.

Fifth was urgency and patience. If survival was at stake, gradual reform could appear indefensible. If claims were uncertain, urgent certainty could cause harm. Grothendieck’s deadlines for civilisational collapse gave his choices force and compressed deliberation. The audience’s resistance slowed the argument without making the danger vanish. A useful assembly had to preserve the ability to act and the time to correct.

These tensions explain why the transcript cannot be reduced to a set of quotable declarations. Its historical value lies in movement between them. Grothendieck sometimes answered an objection, sometimes shifted scale, sometimes conceded, and sometimes repeated the premise. The audience was not only hostile; it supplied distinctions his own movement needed.

The exchange also complicates the later language of silence. Grothendieck’s account distinguishes scientists who considered their work beneficial, those who separated research from use, and those who feared employment consequences; other participants created venues for criticism. His claim that colleagues could not answer why they researched captured a professional habit, not every person’s interior life.

Conversely, the existence of articulate objections did not prove institutions healthy. People at CERN could debate publicly and remain unable to influence strategic decisions. A lecture series could absorb criticism as evidence of openness. An assembly must be judged by speech inside it and by the power connected to that speech. The transcript cannot tell how, if at all, the discussion changed CERN.

An itinerary of rooms

Scharlau reconstructs a dense itinerary from the movement’s liaison bulletins. From January 16 to 23, Grothendieck traveled through Brittany and made contacts in Le Mans. CERN followed on January 27, with Orsay later that month. Scharlau places a Quaker International Center discussion on February 10 and a tour through Clermont-Ferrand, Limoges, and Bordeaux from February 28 to March 4. Not every underlying bulletin scan has been read for this account, so the sequence remains an attributed reconstruction.

For March and April, the same reconstruction places meetings in schools or towns including Meudon, Clichy, Tours, Toulon, Marseille, Montpellier, and Lodève, often with one or two companions. Some planned journeys cannot be confirmed and are not treated as completed. The variety of sites is more important than an exact total: university, research center, school, youth center, regional group, and commune.

The prestige mechanism changed with the room. At CERN the host and apparatus were formidable. In a school, pupils might be silenced by the very fame meant to attract them. In a small group, personal speech might emerge more readily. In a village, the visitor risked importing an urban intellectual agenda. Survivre’s reports compared these conditions and increasingly valued a meeting by what continued after the visitors left.

Constant touring also centralized the movement around an overextended speaker. Requests multiplied because his name drew audiences. A politics of decentralization had acquired a traveling center. The liaison bulletins encouraged provincial participants to organize their own discussions so that Paris could become one group rather than the source of every intervention.

The cost of the traveling center

Movement itineraries can make activity look like pure energy: one town after another, each date evidence of commitment. Travel also consumed attention, created missed correspondence, delayed issues, and placed domestic and editorial burdens on people who stayed. During an early North American trip, mail was not properly redirected and a journal number was delayed. The incident encouraged some division of work, but dependence on Grothendieck remained.

His capacity for sustained labor had shaped the mathematical program at IHÉS. Redirected toward activism, it could inspire collaborators and set a pace they had not chosen. Someone with care responsibilities, another job, or limited time could not spend every day answering letters and traveling. Difference in time became difference in moral appearance: the full-time activist could seem more serious, and everyone else could seem compromised.

The tours also depended on local labor. Organizers secured rooms, accommodations, notices, and follow-up contacts. A biography focused on the lecturer can repeat the archival omission by moving him effortlessly between cities. Every arrival required organizers, posters, a room, transport, and someone to continue local contact.

Grothendieck often traveled with one or two companions. Their presence could reduce the great-man format and supply another voice, as Guedj did in several debates. It could also extend the Paris group’s language into a place before local participants had defined their own question. The collective editorial in no. 12 increasingly advised visitors to begin from concerns in the room rather than deliver a standard exposition.

Academic invitations provided an itinerary that a less famous participant could not reproduce. The success of the tour therefore delayed the creation of means available without him.

The physical performance mattered as well. Accounts remember his appearance, speed, and ability to continue discussion for hours. Such memories can turn politics into charisma. They also record why rooms responded. Arguments do not circulate as disembodied propositions; voice, humour, impatience, listening, and endurance shape them. The movement’s challenge was to use these capacities without making them a constitution.

By mid-1972 Grothendieck’s regular activity in the group began to ebb. The change had several contexts and no securely dated single cause. The traveling center could not become a permanent model. Local groups, the journal, and other editors continued, demonstrating both the movement’s independence and the disruption caused by his withdrawal.

Authority cannot resign by declaration

Grothendieck’s experiment was unusually self-conscious about authority. He declared no expert competence over civilization. He invited non-mathematicians. He published objections. He urged facilitators to listen and to withdraw. These actions altered the ordinary lecture. They did not erase the authority accumulated through a Fields Medal, IHÉS, the Collège, and a body of work known even to people unable to read it.

Charisma is not an office one can leave on a specified date. It exists in other people’s expectations and in the distribution of attention. Some participants found Grothendieck warm and liberating; others experienced his conviction as pressure. Both could be responding accurately to the same capacity. A stated refusal to dominate did not remove domination’s effects.

Nor was every exercise of authority illegitimate. Convening a discussion, correcting a false mathematical claim, choosing a sequence, or protecting a quiet participant can require asymmetrical action. The problem was accountability: could the authority be questioned, limited, and transferred? The CERN transcript passes that test better than a one-way address because opposition becomes part of the record.

The Collège dispute passes it differently. Grothendieck challenged an institutional decision and proceeded without its sanction. The professors also exercised collective authority over the program. Neither side stood outside power. The event asks which authority better served the Collège’s public purpose, not whether authority as such existed.

The public that was missing

Open attendance can create the impression that humanity has entered the room. The actual audiences were socially specific. At the Collège, an auditor needed a free Wednesday morning and confidence to enter a learned institution. At Orsay, students and researchers dominated. At CERN, scientists, technicians, and people connected to the center possessed microphones and simultaneous interpretation. The population about whom everyone spoke was mostly elsewhere.2121Grothendieck, “Remous au Collège de France,” 1; Survivre … et vivre, no. 11, pp. 21–23; Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?”

Workers in factories making instruments, cleaners maintaining laboratories, families near nuclear sites, farmers, patients, and people in countries affected by military technology did not acquire representation because entry was legally free. Some appeared through reports or questions; others were objects in speakers’ arguments. The lecture-as-assembly redistributed speech within a selected public before it addressed selection itself.2222Grothendieck, “Allons-nous continuer la recherche scientifique ?”; Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 11–20.

Gender was another boundary. The movement’s rooms contained women, but men held most recognized scientific posts and most public speaking authority. Grothendieck’s course notice explicitly welcomed all sexes, a meaningful statement in a male institution. Welcome did not supply equal time, credentials, childcare, or freedom from the habits that made some voices sound authoritative.2323Pessis, “Les années 1968 et la science: Survivre … et Vivre, des mathématiciens critiques à l’origine de l’écologisme,” 11–20.

Translation at CERN widened language access and introduced another mediation. Interpreters had to hear, choose words, and keep pace with long, philosophically unstable arguments. A participant’s nuance could change across languages. The transcript mainly preserves French and marks some inaudible material; the English reactions of listeners who relied on interpretation are not fully recoverable.

Meeting scale selected which speakers entered the record. A person who challenged a famous lecturer before hundreds appears in the archive. Someone who spoke only in a small group afterward may vanish. Survivre’s collective text understood this and valued the conversations after formal closure. Historians must do the same while admitting that most of those conversations were not recorded.

The missing public limits Grothendieck’s claim to discuss universal survival. It does not make the meetings meaningless. Institutions often change when a partial public contests the authority available to it and builds connections outward. The accurate word is not the people assembled but an assembly opened inside a professional world.

This limit also changes the measure of listening. A lecturer can listen carefully to everyone present and still remain deaf to people absent by structure. Correcting that absence requires outreach, different venues, material support, local organization, and a better manner at the microphone. The provincial tours and school visits were attempts, unevenly documented, to alter the map.

What remained after the visitor

The movement’s theory judged a debate partly by its aftermath. Did people form a group, meet again, investigate a local institution, or change relations at work. The alternative was an event ending with applause, outrage, and the departure of a famous visitor. The Orsay follow-up meetings offered one answer, though they quickly reproduced old habits. Provincial contacts and distribution networks offered another. Many outcomes remain undocumented.

This criterion protects biography from confusing a memorable performance with political change. Grothendieck could fill a hall and still fail to create collective capacity. A poorly attended meeting might matter deeply to the few who continued. The archive favors posters, transcripts, and famous names; the work after the meeting often survives only in a bulletin line or later testimony.

The lecture as assembly was therefore an unfinished form, not a method he perfected. It joined expertise to self-criticism, public access to prestige, and speech to the discipline of listening. It could make institutional contradictions visible. It could also become another tour by an exceptional man. Survivre’s collective writing named the danger more clearly than many accounts celebrating his courage.

Inside those rooms, another contradiction remained. The man asking whether research should continue was still teaching an advanced mathematical course, advising younger mathematicians, and publishing work generated by earlier seminars. The lecture had made refusal public; it had not made mathematics disappear. To understand what he refused, and what continued despite the refusal, the next chapter returns to the technical title on the notice.
