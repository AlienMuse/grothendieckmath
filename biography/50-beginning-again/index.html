Grothendieck gave the beginning a date. In the retrospective map of his recent work that became Esquisse d’un programme, he wrote that his interest in topological surfaces began to appear in 1974, when he proposed to Yves Ladegaillerie the isotopy study of embeddings of a topological one-complex in a compact surface. The sentence is unusually useful: it names a year, a student, an object, and a problem. It replaces the vague assertion that Grothendieck “eventually returned to mathematics” with a piece of research that could be stated on a page.11Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” sec. 3, p. 13; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 13–15, 22.

Ladegaillerie developed results beyond a routine execution of an assigned topic. In 1974 he announced a topological classification of embeddings of compact one-complexes in surfaces; in 1975 he announced an isotopy theorem; in 1976 he completed the state thesis Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques. A later article, “Classes d’isotopie de plongements de 1-complexes dans les surfaces,” published the work in Topology in 1984 and cited those earlier notes and the thesis. The documentary sequence identifies him as the author of the results.22Ladegaillerie, “Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques”; Ladegaillerie, “Classes d’isotopie de plongements de 1-complexes dans les surfaces”.

Grothendieck’s later account called the resulting isotopy theorem remarkable and described it as a complete algebraic characterization of isotopy classes in the setting under study. He also said that he learned key facts of surface topology while directing the thesis. The direction of instruction therefore ran both ways. The famous adviser supplied questions and characteristic generalizations; the student supplied expertise, proofs, and results that changed the adviser’s field of vision.33Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” sec. 3, pp. 13–15; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 15.

“Beginning again” should be understood at that scale. It was not a reset of mathematical capacity and not an erasure of the years before 1970. Grothendieck brought to the project a lifetime of thinking about equivalence, descent, moduli, and fundamental groups. What began again was sustained attention to an object near the ground: a finite graph drawn on a surface, and the exact conditions under which one drawing could be moved to another.44Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” secs. 2–3.

An accessible problem that did not stay small

A topological one-complex can be pictured as a graph: vertices joined by edges, with loops and multiple edges allowed. A compact surface can be a sphere, a torus, a surface with several handles, or a nonorientable analogue. An embedding places the graph in the surface without crossings except where edges are meant to meet at vertices. The classification problem asks when two such placements differ only by a continuous motion of the surface.55Ladegaillerie, “Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques”; Ladegaillerie, “Classes d’isotopie de plongements de 1-complexes dans les surfaces”.

The phrase “continuous motion” hides a distinction. If two maps are homotopic without being isotopic, the graph may pass through itself during the deformation. If two embeddings are isotopic, every intermediate map remains an embedding. The latter relation captures the intuitive demand that a drawing be moved without cutting strands or pushing one through another. Isotopy is consequently sensitive to the way the graph sits as well as to the homotopy classes of individual cycles.

One begins with invariants. A homeomorphism between embedded graphs should preserve which vertices connect, how incident half-edges are cyclically ordered around each vertex in an oriented surface, and how the complementary regions are arranged. Loops in the graph determine elements of the surface’s fundamental group. But a list of invariants is not yet a classification theorem. One must prove that agreement of the correct algebraic data is sufficient to construct an isotopy. That sufficiency is where a visually evident question becomes difficult.

Cutting is a natural technique. If one cuts a surface along selected curves or along a regular neighborhood of the graph, the problem can be reduced to pieces with boundary. Their gluing data remembers the original embedding. Ladegaillerie’s thesis title joins the two operations—découpes and isotopies—because a controlled decomposition can turn a global motion problem into local and combinatorial ones.66Ladegaillerie, “Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques”; Ladegaillerie, “Classes d’isotopie de plongements de 1-complexes dans les surfaces”.

The precise theorem requires hypotheses and notation beyond this narrative window. The reliable historical claim is narrower: between 1974 and 1976 Ladegaillerie developed an algebraic description of isotopy classes for embedded one-complexes in surfaces, announced components in the Comptes rendus, and completed a state thesis under Grothendieck’s direction. It would be inaccurate to attribute every statement to the adviser or to claim that the entire subject began in Montpellier. Earlier work by Baer and Epstein formed part of the field, as Ladegaillerie’s later bibliography makes clear.77Ladegaillerie, “Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques”; Ladegaillerie, “Classes d’isotopie de plongements de 1-complexes dans les surfaces”.

Grothendieck’s contribution lay less in owning the result than in the questions that gathered around it: functorial translation from topology to combinatorics, the category and morphisms appropriate to embedded graphs, retention of automorphisms, and possible moduli of embeddings. Those extensions pointed beyond a single classification theorem.

They also suited the university. The first examples required little specialized algebraic geometry. Students could manipulate curves and drawings and discover counterexamples. Yet the work demanded exact definitions and proofs. It fulfilled the pedagogical hope that a shared visual stock could become rigorous mathematics without being reduced to an exercise whose answer the teacher already knew.88Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” sec. 2, p. 8; Grothendieck, “En guise de programme pour le cours de C4 « Introduction à la recherche » 1978/79”; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 13–15.

A graph, its neighborhood, and what the complement knows

A theta-shaped graph has two vertices joined by three distinct edges. On a sphere it can be embedded so that its complement consists of three disks. A small regular neighborhood of the graph is then a sphere with three open disks removed, often called a pair of pants. The boundary circles of that neighborhood record the three complementary regions.

Place the same abstract graph on a torus. One edge may run around a meridian and another around a longitude. The cyclic order of the three half-edges at each vertex may agree with the spherical drawing, yet the complement and the induced loops in the fundamental group differ. The graph alone does not determine the embedding. One needs data about the surface around it and about how cycles travel globally.

A regular neighborhood translates part of the embedding problem into a gluing problem. The neighborhood is a surface with boundary that deformation-retracts onto the graph. The closure of its complement is another surface with boundary. Reconstructing the original surface requires matching boundary components. If two embeddings supply equivalent neighborhood and complement data with compatible boundary identifications, one can ask whether the equivalence extends to an ambient isotopy.

This method explains why cutting appears in the thesis title. Cutting simplifies a picture and exposes the interface along which local classifications must be glued. The interface has automorphisms—boundary circles can be rotated, and components can sometimes be permuted—so a set of pieces without gluing maps is insufficient.

The mapping class group of a surface enters naturally. It consists, roughly, of homeomorphisms of the surface modulo isotopy. It acts on embeddings of a graph. Two drawings may be related by a homeomorphism of the surface but not by an isotopy starting from the identity. Thus “same up to a change of coordinates” and “same by continuous motion within the fixed surface” are different equivalence relations.99Ladegaillerie, “Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques”; Ladegaillerie, “Classes d’isotopie de plongements de 1-complexes dans les surfaces”.

This distinction later becomes important in the moduli of curves, where mapping class groups appear as fundamental groups of moduli spaces in an orbifold or stack sense. The 1974 project should not be credited with the later arithmetic program, but it placed mapping classes, combinatorial maps, and moduli in the same field of attention. That field was wide enough for an elementary drawing to touch a sophisticated group.

The example also sets a limit on visual reasoning. On the page, one may draw only a chosen polygonal model of the torus. An edge crossing the boundary of the polygon reappears on the opposite side. Different planar pictures can represent the same embedded graph, and similar pictures can represent different embeddings. Algebraic invariants are not an optional abstraction imposed on a clear image; they are what make the image independent of the drawing convention.

For teaching, this was productive friction. Students could begin by trusting a picture, discover that the picture lied or omitted global information, and then see why a fundamental group, cyclic order, or gluing invariant was needed. The language arose in response to a failure of intuition rather than arriving as a prefabricated prerequisite. That sequence closely matches the pedagogy Grothendieck later described, even though the surviving record does not assign each classroom example to him.

Adviser and student

The language of “Grothendieck’s students” can reproduce the hierarchy it describes. In the IHÉS period it often turns a large collaborative production into the work of a single source. In Montpellier it can do something different but equally misleading: measure each student by whether he or she became a carrier of Grothendieck’s whole vision. Ladegaillerie’s work should instead be read as a mathematical achievement with a documented relation to the adviser.

Grothendieck himself was capable of both perspectives. In Esquisse he named Ladegaillerie as one of two students whose substantial work in 1974–76 gave him pleasure. He described the theorem with admiration. Elsewhere in his retrospective writing he folded the student’s work into a larger account of ideas that he believed had not found adequate continuation. The first register recognizes another mathematician; the second makes continuation of the adviser’s program the standard. Both belong to his testimony, but a biography need not adopt the second standard.1010Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” secs. 3, 9; Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 221–23 and related notes.

The thesis had consequences for Grothendieck’s own research. The archive exhibition dates his emerging Galois–Teichmüller aspiration to 1977, in the wake of the surface-topology work connected with Ladegaillerie. That later development should not be pushed into 1974 as a finished plan. What can be seen already is the infrastructure: maps on surfaces, fundamental groups, isotopy, combinatorial encoding, and moduli. Those ingredients acquired an arithmetic charge only step by step.1111Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 22, 26–31.

The collaboration also offered a contrast with the standard image of Grothendieck as a lecturer who already knew the destination. Surface topology was not his established specialty. He could recognize broad structural possibilities while needing to learn the field’s theorems. This combination—outsider’s questions and student’s domain work—helps explain both the fertility and the friction of the Montpellier period. An outsider can ask why accepted boundaries exist; an outsider can also underestimate the knowledge already accumulated within them.

No surviving source authorizes a continuous scene-by-scene reconstruction of their meetings. We do not know which lemma was discussed on which afternoon or who first drew a decisive picture. The thesis, announcements, and later assessments define the responsible narrative boundary. They show a sustained project and mutual intellectual effect. They do not show dialogue that can be placed in quotation marks.

A second project: Jacobians made relative

Around the same mid-decade interval, Carlos Contou-Carrère worked with Grothendieck on local and global generalized Jacobians over general base schemes. Esquisse identifies this as the other substantial student project of roughly 1974–76, while noting that much of it remained unpublished at the time and that part was announced in the Comptes rendus. Later bibliographic records identify a 1979 note, “La jacobienne généralisée d’une courbe relative; construction et propriété universelle de factorisation,” and a 1981 note on relative geometric local class field theory.1212Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” sec. 9, p. 42; Carlos E. Contou-Carrère, “Jacobiennes généralisées globales relatives,” in The Grothendieck Festschrift, vol. 2, Progress in Mathematics 87 (Birkhäuser, 1990).

A Jacobian begins with a curve but produces a group-like geometric object. For a smooth projective curve over a field, the Jacobian organizes degree-zero divisor classes, or equivalently suitable line bundles. It converts questions about points and divisors on the curve into questions about an abelian variety. The adjective generalized extends the construction to curves with singularities or additional modulus data. The adjective relative asks for the construction not over one fixed field but in a family of curves varying over a base scheme.1313Contou-Carrère, “Jacobiennes généralisées globales relatives”; Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” sec. 3.

That last change is characteristic of Grothendieck’s earlier algebraic geometry. Instead of studying one curve CC, consider a morphism C→SC\to S, with each point of SS indexing a fiber. A satisfactory relative Jacobian should vary with the family, behave under change of base, and satisfy a universal property. These demands force delicate questions about representability, degeneration, and the distinction between local and global data.

The project therefore cuts against any claim that Montpellier meant an immediate abandonment of schemes. Surface topology supplied a new path, but the relative point of view remained active in student work. Grothendieck could move between a graph drawn on a real surface and a Jacobian defined over a general base scheme. The two projects occupied different technical worlds while sharing an interest in extracting algebraic organization from geometry.

The 1979 and 1981 announcements belonged to Contou-Carrère. The historical credit belongs to the author who developed and published the work. Grothendieck’s role is documented as direction and stimulation, not coauthorship.1414Contou-Carrère, “Jacobiennes généralisées globales relatives”.

Here the route from teacher to community was indirect. An idea discussed in a small Montpellier setting entered the literature under a student’s name and on the student’s schedule. That is not concealment or loss. It is one normal form of mathematical transmission. Grothendieck’s later dissatisfaction with reception should be reported separately from the content and authorship of Contou-Carrère’s work.

The relative setting deserves emphasis because it shows what had not changed. A classical geometer might attach one Jacobian to one curve. Grothendieck’s reflex was to ask for the construction in a varying family and over a sufficiently general base. Fibers could then specialize, acquire singularities, or live in positive characteristic. The central issue became the object’s behavior under every legitimate change of base.

That demand can reveal false definitions. Suppose a proposed relative Jacobian is built separately on each fiber. The fibers may have the right dimensions and group laws while failing to assemble into a geometric object over SS. A family can have monodromy; singular fibers can change the connected or affine parts; line bundles may require descent data. Fiberwise correctness is not the same as relative existence.

Functorial language makes the test exact. To each SS-scheme TT, associate a group of line bundles or divisor classes on the pulled-back curve CTC_T, subject to an equivalence. If this functor is represented by a group scheme or algebraic space JJ, then maps T→JT\to J correspond naturally to those classes. If automorphisms remain, a stack may be the more faithful object. The construction is successful when it represents the intended moduli problem and is compatible with base change under stated hypotheses.1515Contou-Carrère, “Jacobiennes généralisées globales relatives”.

This explanation is not a claim that Contou-Carrère’s exact construction took this simplified form in every case. It supplies the inherited problem around which “relative global generalized Jacobian” is intelligible. The local theory introduces formal curves and loop-like groups that require additional machinery.

A universal property

The phrase “universal property” recurs across Grothendieck’s mathematics because it defines an object by the maps it controls rather than by a chosen construction. In a simplified form, the Jacobian J(C)J(C) of a pointed smooth projective curve receives a map from the curve and has the property that suitable maps from CC to abelian varieties factor uniquely through J(C)J(C). The exact formulation depends on base points, degree, and the category in use.

For a family C→SC\to S, one seeks a relative object J→SJ\to S that performs an analogous task compatibly over every part of the base. The analogy illuminates the goal but has limits. Singular fibers may require generalized Jacobians; the relevant functor may be a sheaf or stack; representability can fail without hypotheses; and “unique” may mean unique up to a specified isomorphism.1616Contou-Carrère, “Jacobiennes généralisées globales relatives”.

The student project’s local/global language points toward another distinction. Global divisor data lives on the whole curve or family. Local symbols and local Jacobian constructions capture behavior near formal punctures or singular points. Class field theory relates local multiplicative information to abelian extensions; a geometric relative version seeks an algebro-geometric family form of those relations. This is technically far from the isotopy of graphs, but both ask how local pieces assemble into a global invariant.

The comparison should not be pressed into a theorem. It is a narrative parallel: cutting and gluing on surfaces, descent and factorization in relative geometry. The mathematical objects and proofs are different. What the parallel shows is why both projects could interest a mathematician trained to look for passage from local data to global form.

Three tempos of mathematical work

By 1975 three tempos coexisted. The university tempo was annual: courses, examinations, student projects, thesis deadlines. The correspondence tempo was intermittent: a long letter could open a program and wait years for a reply or rediscovery. The private tempo came in bursts: a question might occupy days or weeks and then be put aside. Grothendieck later described such episodes as sporadic and sometimes explosive periods of mathematical hunger.1818Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), 282–84; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 16.

No one tempo governed all three projects. Ladegaillerie’s thesis moved through successive announcements toward a 1976 defense. Contou-Carrère’s work extended into later announcements and publications. The Breen program remained largely epistolary until Brown’s letter in 1982 revived it. A biography that looks only for dated papers will see the first two incompletely and almost miss the third.

This unevenness also explains why labels such as “returned” and “abandoned” fail. Grothendieck could tell Brown in 1982 that his interest in mathematics had become sporadic, yet retrieve and discuss a technically ambitious 1975 program. He could later say that he thought the machine-building period was over, then write hundreds of pages on foundational machinery in 1983. These were not necessarily lies or conversions. They were statements made at different points in an intermittent practice.

The political and contemplative parts of his life likewise cannot be arranged as forces that displaced mathematics. His ecological activism had questioned whether scientific production deserved priority. From 1976, by his own later account, what he called méditation became another sustained practice. The archive exhibition notes that these activities competed for time, while insisting on continuous traces of mathematical research. Competition for attention is a sufficient historical statement; no pathology or private causal theory is needed.1919Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), “Une lettre, ” pp. L3–L4; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 5–16.

The phrase “sporadic work” can also mislead if it is heard as casual work. A concentrated interval of several weeks may produce hundreds of pages or reorganize a program. What was sporadic was recurrence across the calendar, not necessarily intensity within an episode. Grothendieck’s later metaphors of hunger and explosion emphasize that distinction, though their emotional force should not be converted into a clinical category.

The record documents interruptions, later retrieval, and notes preserved in boxes; it does not identify a private cause for stopping or resuming. Without a publication deadline, a line of thought could be set aside when teaching or another activity intervened. On return, notation, assumptions, and unresolved branches had to be recovered from the pages. Boxes preserved material but supplied neither an index nor a reader.2020Grothendieck and Brown, Correspondance Grothendieck–Brown, letters of March 4 and March 25, 1982; Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” sec. 1, p. 7.

Student work imposed a different discipline. A thesis had to stabilize terminology and reach a defensible conclusion. In that sense, Ladegaillerie’s and Contou-Carrère’s projects provided public edges to a much larger private field. Their documents provide evidence about Grothendieck and show where common mathematical standards acted on shared ideas.

What “outside fashion” meant in 1975

Surface topology itself was not unfashionable. Mathematicians had studied curves on surfaces, mapping class groups, and moduli for decades. Higher category ideas were not unique to Grothendieck; Benabou, Ehresmann, Boardman, Vogt, and others formed parts of a wider history. Relative Jacobians belonged to active algebraic geometry. The phrase “outside fashion” must therefore refer to the combination and the channel, not to an assertion that no one else cared about the constituent subjects.

The dominant public image of Grothendieck’s mathematics still came from schemes, topoi, cohomology, and the Weil conjectures. Deligne’s 1974 theorem and continuing SGA publication made the earlier program highly visible, unlike the private 1975 letter and local surface work.2121Deligne, “La conjecture de Weil I”; The Grothendieck Circle, “Bibliographie d’Alexandre Grothendieck”; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 10–13.

The new work also lacked an agreed public genre. A thesis could be cataloged. A Comptes rendus note could be cited. A forty-page letter full of conjectural foundations occupied a less stable position between source, private communication, program, and request for another mathematician’s work. Its mathematical importance now appears through later developments, but contemporary readers received it as correspondence.

Grothendieck’s style of problem choice intensified this ambiguity. He often treated the absence of the right concept as the central obstruction. To readers seeking a theorem, a program to invent several levels of categorical language could seem unbounded. To him, solving a technical result inside an inadequate framework could miss the point. The disagreement is mathematical and sociological before it is personal: different standards determine what counts as progress.

At Montpellier the shortage of advanced interlocutors made broad programs difficult to distribute locally. Esquisse later says that even minimal combinatorial language could be a barrier for many students, a judgment expressed in severe terms. Yet the same setting generated the surface questions that led him toward the programs. The university was both constraint and source.2222Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” secs. 2, 8–9.

The meaning of a successful thesis

Ladegaillerie’s 1976 thesis marks an outcome rather than a prologue to Grothendieck’s later fame. Its success can be measured without asking whether it produced dessins d’enfants. It formulated and proved results in surface topology, entered announcements into the literature, and supplied material for later publication. It also sustained a working relation across several years in the very institution Grothendieck would judge unable to host a collective research adventure.

The apparent contradiction disappears if one distinguishes a project from a milieu. A project can succeed between adviser and student. A milieu requires overlapping projects, continuing seminars, new entrants, shared language, and institutional succession. Grothendieck wanted the latter and obtained the former in several cases. His later negative assessment concerned the missing multiplication, not a total absence of results.2323Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme,” sec. 9; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 14–15.

The student’s role also challenged a central pedagogical temptation. A teacher who chooses a problem may silently know the shape of the answer and lead the student toward it. In a field the teacher is learning, control is less complete. Grothendieck could offer structural instincts and demand generality, but Ladegaillerie had to build the surface-topological proof. That partial reversal approached the active-active relation the later course program would praise.

It did not abolish asymmetry. The adviser controlled institutional recognition and could characterize the work in later memoirs. The student needed a defensible thesis. Their names did not carry equal power. A balanced account can recognize mutual learning without pretending equal position.

The same caution applies to Contou-Carrère. Grothendieck’s retrospective texts interpret the project through his own concerns about reception and credit. The mathematical bibliography demonstrates that Contou-Carrère pursued a distinct line of work over decades. A biography of the adviser must not make the student’s career vanish at the edge of the adviser’s grievance.

The other practice named in 1976

In his 1984–86 retrospective account, Grothendieck dated the emergence of another sustained practice to 1976. He called it méditation and warned that the word would invite misunderstanding. The archive exhibition places his reported beginning more specifically in October. The start date cannot be independently verified; October remains a curator’s reported refinement of his later chronology, in which he distinguished the practice from, while comparing it to, mathematical discovery.2424Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985), “Une lettre, ” pp. L3–L4; Maronne and Toën, Le temps des réflexions, 16.

This fact has often been used as a hinge between rational mathematics and an allegedly irrational later life. Such a hinge is both pathologizing and chronologically false. The surface program continued. The drawings and arithmetic questions intensified after 1976. The Long March came in 1981 and Pursuing Stacks in 1983. Meditation competed for time and altered his self-description; it did not switch mathematical capacity off.

Grothendieck’s own analogy between meditation and mathematics concerned discovery. In both, he understood work as attending to something not supplied by authority and allowing an initially unclear structure to become visible. A historian need not accept his philosophical claims in order to report that this comparison mattered to him. Nor should the comparison be used to infer private experiences for which no document exists.

The timing also cautions against explaining mathematical subjects through spirituality. His 1974 surface question preceded the date. The 1975 Breen program grew from cohomology and homotopy. Later vocabulary about children, dreams, or inner division cannot be projected backward as the cause of an isotopy theorem or a relative Jacobian. Mathematical claims require mathematical genealogies even when the mathematician places them inside a broader life.

The coexistence of practices affected form. A mathematical problem could be interrupted for reflection and later resumed. Conversely, writing about his life could expand until it displaced the mathematical introduction it was meant to serve, as happened with Récoltes et Semailles after 1983. That later outcome was not predetermined in 1976. At this stage the evidence shows alternation, not replacement.

There is a positive historical gain in that restraint. Once unsupported psychological explanation is removed, the documents themselves become sufficient. A thesis reached completion. A course entered the curriculum. A program traveled in a letter. A new reflective practice began. The year can contain all four facts without being forced into one secret cause.

Documents on different kinds of paper

Three documentary objects define these years. The first is the published announcement: short, dated, and designed to establish a result. Ladegaillerie’s 1974 and 1975 notes belong to this form. The second is the thesis: extended, examined, and institutionally deposited. Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques belongs to it. The third is the letter: addressed to one person, exploratory, and under no obligation to close every question. The 1975 Breen correspondence belongs to this form.

Their differences are part of the history. The note compresses. The thesis demonstrates. The letter speculates and recruits. Grothendieck’s mathematical life after 1970 increasingly spread across the third form and private scratchwork, while students still used the first two. That division helps explain how ideas could originate in a shared setting yet acquire radically different visibility.

The archive preserves another distinction: the document written then and the document remembering later. Esquisse in 1984 supplies the clearest chronology of 1974–76, but it was composed for a CNRS application and shaped by a decade of hindsight. Récoltes et Semailles supplies richer self-assessment, but it was written amid a broader moral reckoning. The earlier notes and thesis anchor those retrospections.2525Grothendieck, “Esquisse d’un Programme”; Grothendieck, Récoltes et Semailles, ed. Carmona (1985); Ladegaillerie, “Découpes et isotopies de surfaces topologiques”; Ladegaillerie, “Classes d’isotopie de plongements de 1-complexes dans les surfaces”.

Where the sources disagree in emphasis, the narrative should retain the tension. Grothendieck remembered two exceptional student projects amid general failure. The archive exhibition sees an original teaching program whose vision was not always realized. Bibliography shows concrete results. None of these statements cancels another. Together they describe a beginning that was productive but not self-sustaining.

A problem takes local form

The first opened primary publication is Yves Ladegaillerie’s four-page note, “Classification topologique des plongements des 1-complexes compacts dans les surfaces,” in the July 22, 1974, Comptes rendus. Ladegaillerie is the author and Henri Cartan the presenter. Its abstract announces equivalences for oriented and unoriented tubular neighborhoods of compact one-complexes and a characterization of embeddings of minimal genus in oriented surfaces. Those are the paper’s public claims; Grothendieck’s later report supplies the supervisory setting, not a second byline.2626Schneps and Lochak, Geometric Galois Actions 1, 7–48, 13–15; Ladegaillerie, “Classification topologique des plongements des 1-complexes compacts dans les surfaces,” 129.

The note begins from a compact one-complex, a finite graph considered as a topological space, and replaces the bare drawing by a tubular neighborhood: a compact surface with boundary that retracts to the graph. In an oriented thickening, the circular order of half-edges around each vertex becomes part of the data. Theorem 8.3 on printed page 129 states the relevant equivalence of categories. Thus two graphs with the same vertices and edges can carry different neighborhood information; the paper classifies more than the abstract graph.2727Ladegaillerie, “Classification topologique des plongements des 1-complexes compacts dans les surfaces,” 129–30.

For a general reader, one may compare the problem to asking whether two routes of cord sewn onto the same piece of cloth can be moved into one another without unpicking the cord or letting it pass through itself. The analogy makes isotopy stricter than a free deformation. It stops before classification: an abstract graph may have loops and branching, a surface may carry handles or boundary, and the actual theorem depends on precise topological data not captured by cloth and cord. The comparison is an entry point, not a proof and not a claim that Ladegaillerie used this example.2828Ladegaillerie, “Classification topologique des plongements des 1-complexes compacts dans les surfaces,” 129–30.

The note handles the unoriented case by adding structure rather than erasing orientation from the language. Sections 9–11 introduce a fixed-point-free involution and an antisymmetric circularization, and section 11 gives another category equivalence. The compact statement matters historically: by June 1974, the session date printed on page 131, the project had definitions and results precise enough for an academy announcement. It does not show that this final vocabulary existed at the project’s first conversation.2929Ladegaillerie, “Classification topologique des plongements des 1-complexes compacts dans les surfaces,” 129–31.

Section 12 defines equivalence of embeddings by a homeomorphism of the ambient compact surfaces and analyzes the complementary surface after a tubular neighborhood is removed. Section 13 asks for the least genus of an oriented surface supporting an embedding.
